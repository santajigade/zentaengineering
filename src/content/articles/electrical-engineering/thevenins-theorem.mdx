<Key title="Short answer">

**Thevenin's theorem** states that any linear two-terminal circuit can be replaced at its terminals by one voltage source V<sub>th</sub> in series with one resistance R<sub>th</sub>. V<sub>th</sub> is the open-circuit terminal voltage, and R<sub>th</sub> is the resistance seen at the terminals with all independent sources turned off.

</Key>

Thevenin's theorem is one of the most useful tools in circuit analysis. It lets an engineer collapse a large network into a two-element model and then study what happens when only the load changes. This guide explains the theorem, gives the step by step method, covers circuits with dependent sources, links Thevenin and Norton forms, and solves a complete example including maximum power transfer.

## What Thevenin's theorem says

The French engineer Léon Charles Thévenin published the theorem in 1883. It applies to any circuit made of linear elements (resistors, inductors, capacitors, linear dependent sources) and independent sources. Looking into two terminals A and B, the whole circuit behaves exactly like:

- an ideal voltage source V<sub>th</sub>, equal to the voltage across A and B when nothing is connected, in series with
- a resistance R<sub>th</sub>, equal to the resistance measured across A and B with independent sources deactivated.

"Behaves exactly like" means that for any load connected across A and B, the load voltage and current are the same in the original circuit and in the equivalent. What happens inside the original circuit, such as the power lost in its internal resistors, is not represented by the equivalent.

The load current follows directly from the model:

$$
I_L = \frac{V_{th}}{R_{th} + R_L}
$$

## Steps to apply Thevenin's theorem

1. **Identify the load.** Choose the element or branch you want to study and remove it. Mark the two terminals A and B where it was connected.
2. **Find V<sub>th</sub>.** Calculate the open-circuit voltage between A and B using any method: voltage divider, nodal analysis, mesh analysis or superposition. The basic tools are covered in [Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws](/electrical-engineering/ohms-law-kirchhoffs-laws/).
3. **Find R<sub>th</sub>.** Turn off every independent source: replace voltage sources with short circuits and current sources with open circuits. Then find the equivalent resistance between A and B by series and parallel reduction. Where resistors form a bridge, a [star delta transformation](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/star-delta-transformation-formula/) may be needed.
4. **Draw the equivalent.** Place V<sub>th</sub> in series with R<sub>th</sub>, with the polarity that gives the open-circuit voltage you found.
5. **Reconnect the load.** Calculate the load current and voltage from the simple series circuit.

An alternative to step 3 is to find the short-circuit current I<sub>sc</sub> between A and B. Then R<sub>th</sub> = V<sub>th</sub> / I<sub>sc</sub>. This is also how an equivalent is found from measurements on real equipment: measure the open-circuit voltage, then the current into a known load, and solve for R<sub>th</sub>.

### Circuits with dependent sources

Dependent (controlled) sources must not be switched off, because their value depends on a voltage or current elsewhere in the circuit. Two methods are used:

- **Test source method:** turn off independent sources only, apply a test voltage V<sub>t</sub> across A and B, find the current I<sub>t</sub> it delivers, and take R<sub>th</sub> = V<sub>t</sub> / I<sub>t</sub>.
- **Open circuit and short circuit method:** find V<sub>th</sub> and I<sub>sc</sub> with all sources active, then divide.

With dependent sources R<sub>th</sub> can even come out negative, which indicates that the circuit can supply energy, as in an amplifier model.

## Thevenin and Norton equivalents

Norton's theorem gives the dual result: the same terminals can be modelled by a current source I<sub>N</sub> in parallel with a resistance R<sub>N</sub>. The two forms are related by source transformation:

$$
R_N = R_{th} \qquad I_N = \frac{V_{th}}{R_{th}}
$$

Use whichever form makes the next step easier. A Thevenin model suits loads added in series; a Norton model suits loads added in parallel.

## Maximum power transfer

For a source with a fixed Thevenin resistance, the power delivered to a resistive load is greatest when R<sub>L</sub> = R<sub>th</sub>. The maximum power is then:

$$
P_{max} = \frac{V_{th}^2}{4 R_{th}}
$$

At this point the efficiency is only 50 percent, because equal power is lost in R<sub>th</sub>. Power systems therefore do not operate at matched conditions; they keep the source resistance much smaller than the load to achieve high efficiency. Matching is used in communication and signal circuits, where getting the most power to a load matters more than efficiency. In AC circuits the condition becomes a load impedance equal to the complex conjugate of the source impedance.

To check the result, solve the original circuit directly with the 4 Ω load. R3 plus R<sub>L</sub> is 6 Ω, which in parallel with R2 gives 2 Ω. The total resistance is 8 Ω, so the source current is 3 A and the voltage across R2 is 6 V. The load branch current is 6 / 6 = 1 A, matching the Thevenin result.

## Where Thevenin's theorem is used

- **Load studies:** finding the current in one branch for many values of load without re-solving the whole network.
- **Fault calculations:** the Thevenin impedance at a bus in a power system sets the short circuit current at that point.
- **Electronics:** modelling a sensor, signal source or bias network as a source with an output resistance, which shows how much the reading drops when a meter or amplifier input loads it.
- **Instrumentation:** checking that a transmitter loop or bridge output is not pulled down by the input resistance of the measuring device.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving the load connected while finding V<sub>th</sub>.
- Opening a voltage source instead of shorting it when finding R<sub>th</sub>.
- Switching off dependent sources.
- Using the equivalent to calculate power inside the original network.
- Getting the polarity of V<sub>th</sub> wrong, which reverses the load current.

## Thevenin's theorem interview and viva questions

1. **What does Thevenin's theorem state?** Any linear two-terminal circuit can be replaced at its terminals by a voltage source V<sub>th</sub> in series with a resistance R<sub>th</sub>, giving the same load voltage and current for any load.
2. **How do you find R<sub>th</sub> in a circuit with only independent sources?** Remove the load, replace voltage sources with short circuits and current sources with open circuits, then find the equivalent resistance between the terminals.
3. **Why must dependent sources not be switched off?** Their value depends on a voltage or current elsewhere in the circuit, so they are left active and R<sub>th</sub> is found with a test source or from V<sub>th</sub> / I<sub>sc</sub>.
4. **How are the Thevenin and Norton equivalents related?** They are linked by source transformation: R<sub>N</sub> = R<sub>th</sub> and I<sub>N</sub> = V<sub>th</sub> / R<sub>th</sub>.
5. **What is the efficiency at maximum power transfer?** It is 50 percent, because equal power is lost in R<sub>th</sub>, which is why power systems are not operated at matched conditions.
