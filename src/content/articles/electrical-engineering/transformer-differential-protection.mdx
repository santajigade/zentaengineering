<Key title="Short answer">

Differential protection of a transformer compares the currents entering and leaving the protected zone through current transformers on every winding. For load and external faults the matched secondary currents balance and the relay stays stable. An internal fault creates a difference current, and the biased relay trips the breakers on all sides at high speed.

</Key>

Differential protection is the main protection for power transformers above a few MVA, and it is device 87T in IEEE C37.2 numbering. It is a unit scheme: it protects only the zone between its current transformers (CTs), so it needs no time grading with downstream relays and can trip instantly.

## Principle of differential protection

The principle is [Kirchhoff's current law](/electrical-engineering/ohms-law-kirchhoffs-laws/). If no current is lost inside a zone, what goes in must come out. CTs are installed on both sides of the transformer, and their secondaries are connected so that, in a healthy condition, the two secondary currents circulate round the pilot wires. This is the Merz-Price circulating current arrangement described in the Network Protection and Automation Guide (NPAG). The relay operating element sits across the midpoint of the pilots, so it only sees the difference between the two CT currents.

For normal load or a fault outside the zone, the same current passes through, the secondaries balance and the operating current is close to zero. For a fault between the CTs, current flows into the zone from one or both sides and the difference no longer cancels. If it exceeds the setting, the relay trips the circuit breakers on every winding.

## Why transformer differential protection is harder than for a busbar

A transformer does not pass the same current on both sides, and several effects create a difference current even with no fault. IEEE C37.91 and NPAG list these:

- Line currents differ by the voltage ratio. CT ratios rarely match exactly, so amplitude matching factors are applied, once by interposing CTs and now in numerical relay software.
- A delta star transformer such as Dyn11 shifts the line currents by 30 degrees. Electromechanical schemes connected the CTs on the star side in delta and those on the delta side in star. Numerical relays apply a vector group matrix instead.
- For an external earth fault on an earthed star winding, zero sequence current flows on that side but not in the delta side lines. It must be filtered out, which the delta connected CTs or the relay's zero sequence elimination do.
- Moving the on-load tap changer changes the ratio, so the matched currents drift apart over the tap range.
- On a heavy through fault, one CT may saturate before the other and produce a false difference.
- Energising a transformer draws a large magnetising inrush current on one side only, which the relay sees as internal. Overexcitation raises the magnetising current in the same way.

## Biased (percentage) differential characteristic

To stay stable for mismatch and CT errors, the relay uses a restraint or bias current. One common definition is the average of the matched currents. The operating threshold rises with the bias current, so a larger through current needs a larger difference before tripping. Taking currents into the zone as positive:

$$
I_d = \left| \bar I_1 + \bar I_2 \right|
$$

$$
I_r = \frac{\left| \bar I_1 \right| + \left| \bar I_2 \right|}{2}
$$

A single slope characteristic trips when:

$$
I_d > I_{s1} + K \, I_r
$$

where I_s1 is the minimum pickup and K is the bias slope. Many relays add a second, steeper slope at high bias current to cover CT saturation on severe through faults. IEC 60255-187-1 defines how these restrained characteristics and the unrestrained element are specified and tested.

## Inrush and overexcitation blocking

Magnetising inrush can look like a large internal fault. Its waveform is offset and peaky, with a high second harmonic content, while fault current is mostly fundamental. Transformer relays measure the second harmonic in the differential current and block or restrain the biased element while it exceeds the set percentage of the fundamental. Overexcitation, from high voltage or low frequency, produces a strong fifth harmonic, so many relays also include fifth harmonic blocking. For more on the inrush waveform, see this explanation of [transformer inrush current](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/transformer-inrush-current/).

An unrestrained high set element is added for heavy internal faults. It ignores harmonics and is set above the maximum expected inrush, so a severe fault is cleared even if CT saturation creates harmonics.

## Operation step by step

1. CTs on each winding measure the line currents and send them to the relay.
2. The relay applies amplitude matching factors and the vector group correction.
3. It removes zero sequence current where the winding connection requires it.
4. It calculates the differential and bias currents for each phase.
5. It checks the second and fifth harmonic blocking, then compares the point against the biased characteristic.
6. If the point lies in the trip zone, or the unrestrained element picks up, it trips the breakers on all sides.

<dt>Given</dt><dd>A 20 MVA, 132/33 kV transformer. HV CT ratio 100/1, LV CT ratio 500/1. Numerical relay with illustrative settings I_s1 = 0.2 per unit and slope K = 30 %. <dt>Find</dt><dd>The amplitude matching factors and whether the relay trips in each case.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Vector group and zero sequence correction already applied in the relay, currents in phase on each side, bias current taken as the average of the two currents, no harmonic blocking active.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>HV full load current = 20 000 000 ÷ (√3 × 132 000) = 87.48 A, secondary = 87.48 ÷ 100 = 0.875 A<br />LV full load current = 20 000 000 ÷ (√3 × 33 000) = 349.9 A, secondary = 349.9 ÷ 500 = 0.700 A<br />Matching factors: 1 ÷ 0.875 = 1.143 (HV), 1 ÷ 0.700 = 1.429 (LV)<br />Case A: I_d = 3.6 − 4.0 = 0.4 pu (magnitude), I_r = (3.6 + 4.0) ÷ 2 = 3.8 pu, threshold = 0.2 + 0.3 × 3.8 = 1.34 pu<br />Case B: I_d = 3.0 + 0.5 = 3.5 pu, I_r = (3.0 + 0.5) ÷ 2 = 1.75 pu, threshold = 0.2 + 0.3 × 1.75 = 0.725 pu</dd>

## Key formulas for differential protection

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full load line current | I_FL = S ÷ (√3 × V) | S in VA, V = line voltage in V, I_FL in A |
| CT secondary current | I_sec = I_FL ÷ CT ratio | CT ratio as primary/secondary, I_sec in A |
| Amplitude matching factor | k = 1 ÷ I_sec at full load | makes rated current 1 pu on each side |
| Differential current | I_d = abs(I_1 + I_2) | matched currents in pu, into the zone positive |
| Bias current | I_r = (abs(I_1) + abs(I_2)) ÷ 2 | pu, one common definition |
| Trip condition, single slope | I_d > I_s1 + K × I_r | I_s1 = minimum pickup in pu, K = slope |

## Advantages and limitations

Differential protection is fast, selective and independent of other relays because it only responds to faults inside its zone. It detects phase to phase faults and most winding to earth faults. Its sensitivity to faults near the star point of an impedance earthed winding is low, so restricted earth fault protection is added. It may miss low level interturn faults, which is why the Buchholz relay and backup [overcurrent relays](/electrical-engineering/overcurrent-relay/) remain on large units. It also depends on correct CT wiring, polarity and ratio, so commissioning includes stability tests with primary or secondary injection.

## Applications

Biased differential protection is standard on generator transformers, grid and distribution substation transformers, and large motor feeder transformers. The same principle protects generators, motors, busbars and cables, with lines using communication links instead of pilot wires. For background on the transformer itself, see the [transformer working principle](/electrical-engineering/transformer-working-principle/) and the [circuit breakers](/electrical-engineering/circuit-breaker-working-principle/) that the relay trips.

## Differential protection interview and viva questions

1. **What is the basic principle of differential protection?** The relay compares the currents entering and leaving a zone. For a healthy zone they balance, and an internal fault produces a difference current that operates the relay.
2. **Why is a bias or restraint current used?** CT errors, ratio mismatch and tap changer movement create a difference current that grows with through current, so the threshold is raised in proportion to the bias current.
3. **Why does inrush current cause a problem, and how is it handled?** It flows on one side only and looks like an internal fault. The relay detects its high second harmonic content and blocks the biased element.
4. **How was the 30 degree shift of a delta star transformer compensated in older schemes?** The CTs on the star side were connected in delta and those on the delta side in star, which also removed zero sequence current.
5. **Why is an unrestrained high set element included?** It clears severe internal faults instantly even when CT saturation produces harmonics that could block the biased element.
