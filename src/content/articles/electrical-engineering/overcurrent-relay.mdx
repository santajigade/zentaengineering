<Key title="Short answer">

An overcurrent relay measures the current in a circuit through a current transformer (CT). When the current rises above a set **pickup value**, the relay starts timing, and if the current stays high long enough it **trips the circuit breaker**. The operating time can be instantaneous, fixed (definite time) or **inversely related to the current** (IDMT), so heavier faults are cleared faster.

</Key>

## Why overcurrent protection is needed

Short circuits and overloads cause currents far above normal. Without protection, conductors overheat, insulation is damaged and equipment can be destroyed or catch fire. Overcurrent relays are the most widely used protection in distribution networks, industrial plants and as backup protection in transmission systems, because they are simple, reliable and easy to coordinate.

## How an overcurrent relay works

1. **Measurement.** A CT reduces the primary current to a standard secondary value, usually 1 A or 5 A at rated current. The relay measures this secondary current.
2. **Comparison.** The relay compares the measured current with its **current setting** Is (also called the plug setting or pickup).
3. **Timing.** If the current exceeds Is, the relay calculates its operating time from the chosen characteristic and starts a timer.
4. **Tripping.** If the current is still above the setting when the timer runs out, the relay closes its output contact. This energises the trip coil of the circuit breaker, which opens the faulty circuit.
5. **Reset.** If the current falls below the reset level before the timer expires, the relay resets without tripping.

Modern numerical relays do all of this in software and record the fault current and time for later analysis.

## Operating characteristics

| Characteristic | ANSI code | Behaviour | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Instantaneous | 50 | Trips with no intentional delay above a high setting | Close-in heavy faults, transformer primary protection |
| Definite time | 51 (DT) | Trips after a fixed delay once the setting is exceeded | Networks where fault level varies little |
| Inverse definite minimum time (IDMT) | 51 | Delay decreases as current increases | Most feeder and transformer protection |
| Earth fault | 50N / 51N | Same functions on residual (earth) current | Detecting earth faults with lower settings |
| Directional overcurrent | 67 | Trips only for current flowing in one direction | Parallel feeders and ring networks |

## The IEC IDMT equation

IEC 60255-151 defines the inverse time curves with one equation:

$$
t = \text{TMS} \times \frac{k}{\left( I / I_s \right)^{\alpha} - 1}
$$

Here t is the operating time in seconds, TMS is the time multiplier setting, I is the measured current and Is is the current setting. The ratio I / Is is called the **plug setting multiplier** (PSM).

| IEC curve | k | α |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard inverse (SI) | 0.14 | 0.02 |
| Very inverse (VI) | 13.5 | 1 |
| Extremely inverse (EI) | 80 | 2 |
| Long time inverse (LTI) | 120 | 1 |

Very and extremely inverse curves are useful where the fault current falls sharply with distance along a feeder, and for grading with fuses, which also have steep curves.

## Time grading and coordination

In a radial network, relays are installed at several points between the source and the load. They must be **coordinated** so that only the breaker closest to the fault trips, keeping the rest of the network in service. This property is called **selectivity** or discrimination.

- Each upstream relay is set to operate slower than the downstream relay for the same fault current.
- The difference in operating time is the **grading margin**. It must cover the downstream breaker opening time, relay timing errors, CT errors and a safety allowance. Values of about 0.25 to 0.4 s are common, with the lower end typical for modern numerical relays and fast breakers.
- Upstream current settings are also higher, so that they do not trip for currents the downstream protection should clear.

Engineers check coordination by plotting all relay and fuse curves on a log-log time-current graph and confirming the margins at the maximum and minimum fault levels.

## Choosing the settings

Settings are a balance between sensitivity and stability:

- **Current setting (pickup):** set above the maximum load current the circuit can carry, including allowed overloads, so the relay never trips on normal load. It must also be well below the minimum fault current at the far end of the protected zone, so that every fault is detected.
- **Instantaneous element:** set above the largest current that flows for faults beyond the next downstream protection, and above transformer inrush and motor starting currents, so it trips only for close-in faults.
- **Earth fault setting:** can be much lower than the phase setting, because normal load produces little residual current. It must stay above any standing unbalance or leakage.
- **Time multiplier:** chosen during the grading study, starting from the relay nearest the load and working back towards the source.

Settings should be recorded in a protection settings schedule and re-checked whenever the network changes, for example when a new transformer or a larger feeder cable increases the fault level.

## Numerical relay features

Modern numerical relays combine several functions in one device. Besides phase and earth fault overcurrent, a typical feeder relay offers negative sequence overcurrent for broken conductors, thermal overload, breaker failure protection, auto-reclosing, event and disturbance recording, and communication with SCADA over protocols such as IEC 61850 or Modbus. Settings groups allow the relay to switch between different settings for different network configurations.

## CT selection

The CT must deliver an accurate current to the relay up to the maximum fault current without saturating. Its burden, which includes the relay input and the resistance of the connecting leads, must stay within the CT's rated burden.

## Applications

- **Distribution feeders:** IDMT phase and earth fault protection with an instantaneous element for close-in faults.
- **Transformers:** overcurrent protection on the primary as backup to differential protection.
- **Motors:** overcurrent and earth fault elements, combined with thermal overload protection.
- **Transmission lines:** backup to distance and differential protection.
