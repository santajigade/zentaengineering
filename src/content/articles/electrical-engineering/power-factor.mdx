<Key title="Short answer">

Power factor is the ratio of **real power** (kW), which does useful work, to **apparent power** (kVA), which the supply must deliver. For sinusoidal voltage and current it equals cos φ, where φ is the phase angle between them. A low power factor means more current for the same useful power, which increases losses and costs. It is usually corrected by adding **capacitors** close to inductive loads.

</Key>

## Real, reactive and apparent power

In an AC circuit with inductance or capacitance, current and voltage are not in phase. The power drawn has three parts:

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Real (active) power | P | W, kW | Power converted into work, heat or light |
| Reactive power | Q | var, kvar | Power that flows back and forth to build magnetic and electric fields |
| Apparent power | S | VA, kVA | The product of RMS voltage and current |

They form the **power triangle**:

$$
S^2 = P^2 + Q^2, \qquad \text{PF} = \frac{P}{S} = \cos\varphi
$$

In a three-phase system, S = √3 × V × I, where V is the line voltage and I the line current.

When the load current contains harmonics, as with drives and rectifiers, the **true power factor** is lower than cos φ of the fundamental. It equals the displacement power factor multiplied by a distortion factor. Capacitors correct only the displacement part.

## Causes of low power factor

- **[Induction motors](/electrical-engineering/induction-motor-working-principle/),** which need magnetising current. Their power factor is lowest at light load.
- **[Transformers](/electrical-engineering/transformer-working-principle/),** which draw magnetising current even at no load.
- **Inductive loads** such as induction furnaces, welding transformers and older magnetic lamp ballasts.
- **Non-linear loads** such as rectifiers and drives, which lower the true power factor through [harmonic](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/active-harmonic-filter-working-sizing/) distortion.

## Effects of low power factor

For the same real power, a lower power factor means a higher current, because I is proportional to 1 / PF. This causes:

- **Higher I²R losses** in [cables](/electrical-engineering/cable-sizing/), transformers and switchgear.
- **Larger voltage drop,** which can affect equipment performance.
- **Reduced capacity:** transformers, generators and cables are rated in kVA or amperes, so less real power can be supplied.
- **Higher bills:** many tariffs include kVA maximum demand charges or penalties for low power factor or excess reactive energy.

## Power factor correction

Capacitors draw leading reactive current that cancels part of the lagging reactive current drawn by inductive loads. The supply then delivers only the remaining reactive power. The capacitor rating needed to raise the power factor from cos φ1 to cos φ2 at real power P is:

$$
Q_c = P\left(\tan\varphi_1 - \tan\varphi_2\right)
$$

## How power factor is measured

Power factor can be found in several ways:

- **Power analysers and multifunction meters** measure voltage and current waveforms and calculate P, Q, S and power factor directly. Good instruments report both displacement and true power factor.
- **Energy bills** often show active energy (kWh) and reactive energy (kvarh) for the billing period. The average power factor is kWh / √(kWh² + kvarh²).
- **Motor nameplates** give the power factor at rated load. It is lower at part load, so measured values are more reliable for sizing correction.

A load survey over at least one typical working week shows how much the power factor and reactive demand vary, which decides whether fixed or automatic correction is needed.

## Where to install capacitors

| Location | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| At individual motors | Reduces current in the whole supply path, including the motor cable | Many small units; capacitor idle when motor is off |
| At sub-distribution boards | Corrects groups of loads with fewer units | Feeder cables to the loads are not relieved |
| At the main switchboard (central APFC) | Lowest cost per kvar, easy to monitor and control | Internal cables and transformers downstream still carry reactive current |

When correcting at a motor, the capacitor must not exceed the motor's no-load magnetising reactive power. A larger capacitor can cause self-excitation when the motor is switched off while still turning, producing dangerous overvoltages.

## Methods of correction

| Method | How it works | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed capacitors at the load | Capacitor connected with each motor or transformer | Large motors running at steady load |
| Automatic power factor correction (APFC) panel | Controller switches capacitor steps in and out to follow the load | Main LV switchboards of plants and buildings |
| Detuned capacitor banks | Capacitors with series reactors tuned below the 5th harmonic | Sites with drives, rectifiers and UPS systems |
| Active harmonic filters or static var compensators | Power electronics inject reactive and harmonic currents | Rapidly varying loads, high harmonic sites |
| Synchronous condensers | Over-excited synchronous machines supply reactive power | Transmission networks and very large plants |

## Power factor of common loads

Typical power factor values help when estimating correction, but measured values should always be used for final sizing:

- **Induction motors** at full load usually have a power factor of roughly 0.8 to 0.9, falling sharply at light load.
- **Resistive heaters and incandescent lamps** have a power factor close to 1.
- **Drives and switch mode power supplies** may show a high displacement power factor but a lower true power factor because of harmonics, unless they have power factor correction stages.

## Practical cautions

- **Avoid over-correction.** Fixed capacitors sized for full load can make the power factor leading at light load.
- **Watch for resonance.** Capacitors and the supply inductance form a resonant circuit. If it is tuned near a harmonic produced by the plant, voltages and currents can rise sharply and damage capacitors.
- **Discharge before work.** Capacitors hold charge after disconnection. They are fitted with discharge resistors, and the discharge time must elapse before anyone touches them.
- **Switching transients.** Use capacitor-duty contactors with pre-insertion resistors or thyristor switching for frequently switched steps.
