<Key title="Short answer">

The **circuit breaker working principle** is that a breaker carries normal current and automatically interrupts fault current. When it trips, its contacts separate and an arc forms between them. The breaker stretches and cools the arc in air, vacuum or SF6 gas, so the arc goes out at a current zero and does not restrike.

</Key>

## What a circuit breaker does

A circuit breaker has four duties:

- **Carry** its rated current continuously without overheating.
- **Make** (close onto) a circuit, including one that already has a fault on it.
- **Break** normal load current when switched off.
- **Interrupt** short-circuit current quickly enough to limit damage, when commanded by its own trip unit or by an external [protection relay](/electrical-engineering/overcurrent-relay/).

### 1. Detection and tripping

Low voltage breakers usually have a built-in trip unit. A **thermal** element (a bimetal strip) bends with sustained overload current and releases the latch after a delay. A **magnetic** element (a coil) responds almost instantly to high short-circuit currents. Electronic trip units do the same job using current sensors and a microprocessor.

Medium and high voltage breakers are tripped by external **protection relays**, which measure current and voltage through instrument transformers and energise the breaker trip coil.

### 2. Contact separation and the arc

The trip mechanism releases stored energy, usually in springs, to drive the contacts apart. Current does not stop when the contacts part. The voltage across the small gap ionises the medium, and current continues to flow as an **arc**, a column of very hot conducting plasma.

### 3. Arc extinction

In an AC circuit the current passes through zero twice in each cycle. At a current zero the arc briefly loses its energy supply. Interruption succeeds if the gap regains its insulating strength faster than the **transient recovery voltage** (TRV) rises across it. If it does not, the arc restrikes and the breaker tries again at the next current zero.

Breakers help the gap recover by:

- **Lengthening** the arc, for example by driving it into arc chutes with splitter plates.
- **Cooling** it with gas flow or by contact with cold surfaces.
- **Removing** the ionised particles, which happens very quickly in a vacuum.

## Types of circuit breaker

### By voltage and construction

| Type | Typical voltage range | Arc quenching | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| MCB (miniature circuit breaker) | Low voltage, small currents | Arc chute in air | Final circuits in buildings |
| MCCB (moulded case circuit breaker) | Low voltage, up to a few thousand amperes | Arc chute in air | Feeders, motor and panel protection |
| ACB (air circuit breaker) | Low voltage main incomers | Arc chutes in open air | Main switchboards |
| VCB (vacuum circuit breaker) | Medium voltage, commonly up to about 36 kV | Vacuum interrupter | MV switchgear and motor feeders |
| SF6 circuit breaker | Medium and high voltage, up to the highest transmission levels | Sulphur hexafluoride gas | HV substations, gas insulated switchgear |
| Oil circuit breaker | Medium and high voltage | Mineral oil | Older installations, largely replaced |

SF6 is an excellent insulating and arc quenching gas, but it is a very strong greenhouse gas. Manufacturers are therefore developing vacuum and alternative gas designs for higher voltages.

### MCB trip curves

IEC 60898-1 defines the instantaneous (magnetic) trip range of MCBs as a multiple of rated current In:

| Curve | Instantaneous trip range | Typical loads |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B | 3 to 5 × In | Resistive loads, lighting, long cables |
| C | 5 to 10 × In | General commercial and small motor loads |
| D | 10 to 20 × In | High inrush loads such as transformers and large motors |

## Key ratings

- **Rated voltage and current:** the system voltage and continuous current the breaker is designed for.
- **Breaking capacity:** the highest fault current it can safely interrupt. For LV breakers IEC 60947-2 defines the ultimate breaking capacity Icu and the service breaking capacity Ics.
- **Making capacity:** the peak current it can close onto without damage.
- **Short-time withstand current Icw:** the current it can carry for a stated time, which allows selective (time-graded) tripping.

## Selecting breaking capacity

The breaking capacity must be at least the **prospective short-circuit current** at the point of installation. Close to a distribution [transformer](/electrical-engineering/transformer-working-principle/), a simple estimate assumes an infinite source behind the transformer:

$$
I_{FL} = \frac{S}{\sqrt{3}\,V}, \qquad I_{sc} \approx \frac{I_{FL}}{Z_{pu}}
$$

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transformer full load current | IFL = S ÷ (√3 V) | S rating in VA, V secondary line voltage in V, IFL in A |
| Prospective fault current (infinite source) | Isc ≈ IFL ÷ Zpu | Zpu = transformer impedance per unit (5 percent = 0.05) |
| Breaking capacity check | Icu ≥ Isc | Icu ultimate breaking capacity, Isc prospective short-circuit current, both in A or kA |

## Selectivity between breakers

In a radial low voltage system, breakers are installed in series: a main incomer, feeder breakers and final circuit breakers. For a fault on a final circuit, only the breaker closest to the fault should trip, so that the rest of the installation stays in service. This is called **selectivity** or discrimination.

- **Current selectivity** relies on the downstream breaker tripping at a lower current than the upstream one. It is often only partial, because large fault currents can exceed both instantaneous settings.
- **Time selectivity** delays the upstream breaker's short-circuit trip, which requires it to withstand the fault current for that time (its Icw rating).
- **Energy selectivity** uses current-limiting downstream breakers that clear the fault so fast that the upstream breaker does not see enough energy to trip.

Manufacturers publish selectivity tables that show which combinations of their breakers are selective, and up to which fault current.

## Circuit breaker or fuse?

| Feature | Circuit breaker | Fuse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| After operation | Reset or reclose | Replace the fuse link |
| Remote operation and relay tripping | Yes | No |
| Adjustable settings | Yes, on many MCCBs and ACBs | No |
| Current limiting | Some designs | Excellent, very fast for high faults |
| Cost for high breaking capacity | Higher | Lower |

## Maintenance and testing

Breakers are inspected and tested at intervals set by the manufacturer. Typical checks include contact resistance measurement, operating time tests, insulation resistance tests, trip unit testing by secondary or primary injection, and inspection of the operating mechanism and arc chutes. For vacuum interrupters, vacuum integrity is checked with a high voltage test.

## Circuit breaker working principle interview and viva questions

1. **Why does current keep flowing when the breaker contacts first separate?** The voltage across the small gap ionises the medium, so current continues as an arc of hot conducting plasma until it is extinguished.
2. **Why is a current zero important for AC interruption?** At a current zero the arc briefly loses its energy supply, and interruption succeeds if the gap regains insulating strength faster than the transient recovery voltage rises.
3. **How do the thermal and magnetic trip elements of an LV breaker differ?** The bimetal thermal element bends under sustained overload and trips after a delay, while the magnetic coil responds almost instantly to high short-circuit current.
4. **What is the difference between B, C and D curve MCBs?** Their instantaneous trip ranges are 3 to 5, 5 to 10 and 10 to 20 times rated current, matching resistive, general and high inrush loads.
5. **What is selectivity between breakers?** Only the breaker closest to a fault trips, so the rest of the installation stays in service; it is achieved by current, time or energy selectivity.
