<Key title="Short answer">

An **insulation resistance test** is a non-destructive check that applies a DC voltage, typically 500 V to 5 kV, between a conductor and [earth](/electrical-engineering/electrical-earthing/) or between conductors, and measures the small current through the insulation. The result, in megohms or gigohms, should be high and rising; low or falling readings point to moisture, contamination or damage.

</Key>

## Why the insulation resistance test is done

Insulation in [motors](/electrical-engineering/induction-motor-working-principle/), generators, [transformers](/electrical-engineering/transformer-working-principle/), cables and switchgear degrades with heat, moisture, dirt, vibration and age. An IR test is a simple, non-destructive way to:

- check new installations before they are energised,
- check equipment after repair, flooding or long storage,
- track insulation condition over time in a [maintenance](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/transformer-maintenance-checklist/) programme.

A single reading is useful, but the **trend** of readings taken under similar conditions is far more informative.

## What the tester measures

When a DC voltage is applied across insulation, the total current has three components:

| Current | Cause | Behaviour over time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capacitive charging current | Charging the capacitance of the insulation | Falls to almost zero within seconds |
| Absorption (polarisation) current | Alignment of molecules in the insulation material | Decays over minutes |
| Leakage (conduction) current | Current through and across the insulation surface | Stays roughly constant |

The tester divides the test voltage by the total current. As the charging and absorption currents die away, the indicated resistance rises towards a value set by the leakage current. Moisture and contamination increase leakage current, so they keep the reading low and flat.

## Choosing the test voltage

The test voltage should stress the insulation without exceeding what it can safely withstand. IEEE 43 recommends DC test voltages for rotating machine windings based on rated voltage:

| Winding rated voltage (V) | Recommended DC test voltage (V) |
| --- | --- |
| Below 1000 | 500 |
| 1000 to 2500 | 500 to 1000 |
| 2501 to 5000 | 1000 to 2500 |
| 5001 to 12,000 | 2500 to 5000 |
| Above 12,000 | 5000 to 10,000 |

For low voltage installations, IEC 60364-6 specifies the test voltages and minimum values for verification of circuits. Electronic equipment, surge protective devices and drives must be disconnected first, because the test voltage can damage them.

## Polarization index and dielectric absorption ratio

Because the reading rises with time, ratios of readings taken at two times are used to judge insulation quality independently of size and temperature:

$$
\text{PI} = \frac{IR_{10\,\text{min}}}{IR_{1\,\text{min}}}, \qquad \text{DAR} = \frac{IR_{60\,\text{s}}}{IR_{30\,\text{s}}}
$$

IEEE 43 recommends a minimum PI of 2.0 for windings with class B, F and H insulation, and 1.5 for class A. A PI close to 1 means the leakage current dominates, which usually indicates moisture or contamination. IEEE 43 also notes that PI is not a meaningful indicator when the 1 minute reading is already above 5000 MΩ.

## Minimum insulation resistance for machines

IEEE 43 gives recommended minimum 1 minute values, corrected to 40 °C, for rotating machines:

| Winding type | Recommended minimum IR (1 minute, 40 °C) |
| --- | --- |
| Most AC windings built after about 1970 (form-wound coils) | 100 MΩ |
| Random-wound and form-wound windings rated below 1 kV | 5 MΩ |
| Older windings and some DC windings | kV + 1 MΩ, where kV is the rated voltage in kilovolts |

## Effect of temperature

Insulation resistance falls as temperature rises. A common rule of thumb is that it roughly halves for every 10 °C increase, although the exact relationship depends on the material. Readings should therefore be corrected to a reference temperature, such as 40 °C in IEEE 43, before comparing them with limits or earlier results. Humidity also lowers readings, especially through surface leakage.

## Keeping useful records

The value of insulation testing comes from comparing results over time. Each record should include the date, equipment identity, test voltage, readings at 30 seconds, 1 minute and 10 minutes where taken, winding or ambient temperature, humidity, the instrument used and the test connections. Plotting temperature-corrected readings for the same machine over several years shows gradual deterioration long before a failure, so repairs can be planned during a shutdown.

## Safe test procedure

1. Isolate the equipment, lock it off and prove it is dead.
2. Disconnect sensitive electronics, surge arresters and capacitors that could be damaged or affect the result.
3. Clean and dry terminals to reduce surface leakage.
4. Connect the tester: line lead to the conductor, earth lead to the frame or earth. Use the guard terminal to remove surface leakage if needed.
5. Apply the test voltage for the required time and record the readings, temperature and humidity.
6. After the test, **discharge** the insulation by earthing it. Large windings and long cables can hold a dangerous charge; IEEE 43 recommends earthing for at least four times the test duration.

## Testing cables

Insulation resistance of cables is tested between each core and the other cores connected to earth, and between each core and the armour or screen. Points to remember:

- **Length matters.** A long [cable](/electrical-engineering/cable-sizing/) has more insulation in parallel, so its resistance is lower than a short cable of the same type. Results are sometimes converted to MΩ·km for comparison.
- **Capacitance matters.** Long cables take time to charge, so readings should be taken after the reading stabilises, and the cable must be discharged afterwards.
- **Disconnect both ends.** Equipment at either end, such as drives, meters or surge arresters, must be disconnected or the result will reflect that equipment instead of the cable.

For medium and high voltage cables, an IR test is usually followed by withstand tests such as very low frequency (VLF) or DC tests according to the cable standard, and by sheath tests for cables with an outer sheath.

## Key formulas for insulation resistance test

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indicated insulation resistance | IR = V ÷ I | V = DC test voltage in V, I = total current in A, IR in Ω (shown as MΩ or GΩ) |
| Polarization index | PI = IR(10 min) ÷ IR(1 min) | Ratio of readings, no unit; IEEE 43 minimum 2.0 for class B, F and H |
| Dielectric absorption ratio | DAR = IR(60 s) ÷ IR(30 s) | Ratio of readings, no unit |
| Minimum IR, older and some DC windings | IR(min) = kV + 1 | IR(min) in MΩ at 1 minute and 40 °C, kV = rated voltage in kilovolts |

## Interpreting results

- **High and rising:** insulation is in good condition.
- **Low but rising:** often surface moisture or dirt; cleaning and drying usually help.
- **Low and flat or falling:** moisture absorbed into the insulation, contamination or damage; investigate before energising.
- **Sudden drop between routine tests:** a warning sign even if the value is still above the minimum.

## Insulation resistance test interview and viva questions

1. **What currents flow when the DC test voltage is applied?** Capacitive charging current, which falls to almost zero within seconds, absorption current, which decays over minutes, and leakage current, which stays roughly constant.
2. **What is the polarization index and what minimum does IEEE 43 recommend?** PI is the 10 minute reading divided by the 1 minute reading. IEEE 43 recommends at least 2.0 for class B, F and H insulation and 1.5 for class A.
3. **What test voltage is used for a winding rated below 1000 V?** IEEE 43 recommends 500 V DC for windings rated below 1000 V.
4. **Why are readings corrected for temperature?** Insulation resistance falls as temperature rises, roughly halving for every 10 °C, so readings are corrected to a reference such as 40 °C before comparing them with limits or earlier results.
5. **Why must equipment be discharged after the test?** Large windings and long cables hold a dangerous charge; IEEE 43 recommends earthing them for at least four times the test duration.
