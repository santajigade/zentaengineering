  - q: "Why does a PFR need less volume than a CSTR for the same conversion?"
    a: "For reactions whose rate falls as reactant is used up (positive order kinetics), a CSTR operates entirely at the low outlet concentration and therefore the lowest rate. A PFR passes through high concentrations near its inlet, so its average rate is higher and less volume is needed."
  - q: "When is a CSTR preferred over a PFR?"
    a: "When good temperature control is needed for strongly exothermic reactions, for liquid phase reactions with slurries or viscous fluids that need agitation, when the reaction is autocatalytic at low conversion, and when selectivity benefits from keeping a reactant concentration low. <Key title="Short answer">

In the **CSTR vs PFR** comparison, a continuous stirred tank reactor is perfectly mixed, so its uniform contents equal the outlet composition, while a plug flow reactor is a tube with no back-mixing, so concentration falls along its length. For most reactions a PFR needs less volume; a CSTR gives easier temperature control.

</Key>

Reactor design starts with two idealised models. Real reactors fall between them, but these ideals give the design equations, explain why reactors behave as they do and set limits for what a real reactor can achieve. Understanding the CSTR vs PFR comparison is central to chemical reaction engineering.

**CSTR (mixed flow reactor).** A tank with an agitator, fed and drained continuously. The ideal assumes perfect mixing: every point in the tank has the same composition and temperature, equal to the outlet stream. Feed is diluted the moment it enters.

**PFR (tubular reactor).** Fluid flows through a tube with a flat velocity profile and no mixing in the flow direction, but complete mixing across the cross section. Each element of fluid spends exactly the same time in the reactor, so it behaves like a small batch reactor moving down the tube.

## Design equations

For a reactant A fed at molar flow FA0, with conversion X and rate of disappearance −rA, the steady state mole balances give:

$$
\text{CSTR:}\;\; V = \frac{F_{A0}\,X}{-r_A}\Big|_{\text{exit}} \qquad \text{PFR:}\;\; V = F_{A0}\int_0^X \frac{dX}{-r_A}
$$

The CSTR rate is evaluated at the exit conditions. The PFR integrates the rate along the tube.

For a **first order liquid phase reaction** (−rA = k CA, constant density), these become simple expressions in space time τ = V / v₀:

$$
\tau_{\text{CSTR}} = \frac{X}{k(1 - X)} \qquad \tau_{\text{PFR}} = \frac{-\ln(1 - X)}{k}
$$

### The Levenspiel plot

Plotting FA0 / (−rA) against X gives a curve that rises with X for normal kinetics. The PFR volume is the area under the curve; the CSTR volume is the rectangle of height equal to the exit value. The rectangle is always larger for such kinetics, which shows visually why the PFR is smaller.

## Key formulas for CSTR vs PFR

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Space time | τ = V ÷ v₀ | V = reactor volume (L), v₀ = inlet volumetric flow (L/min), τ in min |
| CSTR design equation | V = F_A0 X ÷ (−r_A) at exit | F_A0 in mol/min, X = conversion, −r_A in mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹ |
| PFR design equation | V = F_A0 ∫ dX ÷ (−r_A), from 0 to X | same symbols, rate integrated along the tube |
| First order CSTR | τ = X ÷ [k (1 − X)] | k = rate constant (min⁻¹) |
| First order PFR | τ = −ln(1 − X) ÷ k | constant density, isothermal |
| N equal CSTRs in series, first order | 1 − X = 1 ÷ (1 + k τᵢ)ᴺ | τᵢ = space time of each tank |

## CSTRs in series

Splitting the CSTR volume into several tanks in series lets the early tanks run at higher concentration, so the total volume falls. With N equal tanks, a first order reaction gives 1 − X = 1 / (1 + kτᵢ)ᴺ, where τᵢ is the space time of each tank. As N increases, the train approaches PFR behaviour. In the example above, two equal tanks need a total space time of about 8.6 min instead of 18. This is why industrial liquid phase processes often use a cascade of stirred tanks.

## CSTR vs PFR: comparison table

| Feature | CSTR | PFR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mixing | Perfect, uniform composition | None in flow direction |
| Concentration | Constant, equal to outlet | Falls along length |
| Volume for given conversion (positive order) | Larger | Smaller |
| Temperature control | Easy, large mixed volume | Harder; hot spots possible |
| Typical phase | Liquid, slurries | Gas phase, also liquids |
| Construction | Stirred vessel | Tube, often multitubular |
| Residence time distribution | Wide (exponential) | Narrow (all fluid same time) |
| Start-up and cleaning | Easy | Harder for long tubes |

## Effect on selectivity

When more than one reaction can occur, the reactor type affects not only volume but also how much of the desired product forms. For parallel reactions in which the desired reaction has a higher order in the reactant than the undesired one, high reactant concentration favours the desired product, so a PFR or batch reactor is better. If the desired reaction has the lower order, a CSTR, which keeps the reactant concentration low, gives better selectivity. For series reactions such as A to R to S, where R is wanted, a PFR usually gives a higher maximum yield of R because every fluid element has the same residence time and can be stopped at the optimum.

## Comparison with batch reactors

A batch reactor is filled, reacted and emptied. Every element of fluid spends the same time in it, so for the same reaction time it achieves the same conversion as a PFR with equal space time. Batch reactors suit small volumes, many products and slow reactions, but add downtime for filling, heating, emptying and cleaning. Semi-batch operation, in which one reactant is added gradually, is used to control heat release and selectivity.

## When to use each

- **Choose a CSTR** for liquid phase reactions that need agitation or suspension of solids, strongly exothermic reactions where uniform temperature is vital, reactions where a low reactant concentration improves selectivity, and multipurpose plants.
- **Choose a PFR** for gas phase reactions, high conversion requirements, fast reactions and catalytic processes in packed tubes. Fixed bed catalytic reactors, such as the ammonia converters in the [Haber-Bosch process](/chemical-engineering/haber-bosch-ammonia-process/), approach plug flow.

## Real reactors

Real reactors show dead zones, channelling and partial back-mixing. Engineers measure the **residence time distribution** with tracer tests and describe it with models such as tanks in series or axial dispersion. The heat removed or added is calculated with an [energy balance](/chemical-engineering/energy-balance/) and transferred through jackets, coils or external [heat exchangers](/chemical-engineering/heat-exchanger-types/). Flows and compositions entering and leaving come from the plant [material balance](/chemical-engineering/material-balance/), and reactor vessels are designed as [pressure vessels](/chemical-engineering/pressure-vessels-basics/). Temperature, pressure, level and flow loops keep the reactor at its design point; see [process instrumentation](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/) on InstrumentationBlog.in.

## CSTR vs PFR interview and viva questions

1. **What does perfect mixing mean in an ideal CSTR?** Every point in the tank has the same composition and temperature, equal to the outlet stream, so feed is diluted the moment it enters.
2. **Why is the CSTR rate evaluated at exit conditions?** Because the whole tank is at the outlet composition, the reaction proceeds everywhere at the exit rate, which for positive order kinetics is the lowest rate in the process.
3. **How does the Levenspiel plot compare the two reactors?** With F_A0 ÷ (−r_A) plotted against X, the PFR volume is the area under the curve and the CSTR volume is the rectangle at the exit value, which is larger for normal kinetics.
4. **Why are CSTRs often used in series?** Early tanks operate at higher concentration and rate, so the total volume falls, and as the number of tanks increases the train approaches PFR behaviour.
5. **Which reactor gives a higher yield of an intermediate in series reactions A to R to S?** Usually the PFR, because every fluid element has the same residence time and can be stopped at the optimum for R.
