<Key title="Short answer">

The **distillation column working principle** is repeated vaporisation and condensation. Vapour rising from the reboiler meets liquid reflux falling from the condenser, and on every tray or packed section the more volatile component moves into the vapour. The distillate leaving the top is rich in the light component; the bottoms is rich in the heavy component.

</Key>

Distillation is the most widely used separation in the chemical, petroleum and pharmaceutical industries. Crude oil fractionation, solvent recovery, air separation, alcohol production and purification of monomers all depend on it. It works because the components of a liquid mixture have different volatilities: when the mixture boils, the vapour is richer in the more volatile component than the liquid it came from.

## Vapour-liquid equilibrium and relative volatility

For an ideal mixture, **Raoult's law** says the partial pressure of each component equals its mole fraction in the liquid times its pure component vapour pressure: pᵢ = xᵢ Pᵢˢᵃᵗ. The vapour composition then follows from Dalton's law, yᵢ = pᵢ / P.

The ease of separating two components A (lighter) and B is measured by the **relative volatility**:

$$
\alpha_{AB} = \frac{y_A / x_A}{y_B / x_B} \approx \frac{P_A^{sat}}{P_B^{sat}}
$$

For a binary mixture this gives the equilibrium curve y = αx / (1 + (α − 1)x). The larger α is, the easier the separation. When α is close to 1, many stages and high reflux are needed; at α = 1 (an azeotrope), simple distillation cannot separate the mixture.

- **Column shell:** a vertical vessel containing trays or packing.
- **Trays or packing:** provide intimate contact between rising vapour and falling liquid. Sieve, valve and bubble cap trays are common; packing may be random (rings, saddles) or structured (corrugated sheets).
- **Feed nozzle:** the feed enters at the feed stage, chosen near where the column composition matches the feed.
- **Condenser:** condenses the overhead vapour, usually with cooling water or air. A total condenser condenses all of it.
- **Reflux drum and reflux:** collect the condensate; part returns to the top tray as reflux, the rest leaves as distillate.
- **Reboiler:** heats the bottom liquid, often with steam, to generate the vapour that drives the column. Kettle and thermosiphon reboilers are common heat exchangers; see [heat exchanger types](/chemical-engineering/heat-exchanger-types/).

The section above the feed is the **rectifying (enriching) section**, which purifies the light component; below the feed is the **stripping section**, which removes light component from the heavy product.

## Distillation column working principle, stage by stage

On an ideal (equilibrium) stage, vapour from the stage below and liquid from the stage above mix and leave in equilibrium. The vapour picks up more of the light component and leaves upwards; the liquid gives it up, picks up heavy component and flows down through a downcomer. Each stage gives a small enrichment; many stages stacked together give a sharp separation. Temperature falls from bottom to top, because the top is rich in the lighter, lower boiling component.

Real trays do not reach equilibrium, so the number of actual trays is the number of ideal stages divided by an overall tray efficiency. Efficiency depends on the tray design, the physical properties of the mixture and the vapour and liquid loads, and is estimated from correlations, data from similar columns or tests. Packed columns use the height equivalent to a theoretical plate (HETP) in the same way: packed height equals the number of ideal stages multiplied by HETP.

## Reflux ratio and number of stages

The **reflux ratio** R = L / D (reflux returned / distillate taken) is the key operating variable:

- At **total reflux** (no product), the separation needs the **minimum number of stages**, found from the Fenske equation.
- At **minimum reflux**, an infinite number of stages would be needed (found by the Underwood method or graphically).
- Practical designs use R of about 1.2 to 1.5 times the minimum, trading capital cost (more stages) against energy cost (more reflux and reboiler duty).

For binary mixtures the **McCabe-Thiele method** steps off stages graphically between the equilibrium curve and the operating lines, giving a clear picture of how reflux and feed condition affect the design. The column [material balance](/chemical-engineering/material-balance/) (F = D + B and F zF = D xD + B xB) fixes the product flows.

The Fenske equation for the minimum number of theoretical stages is:

$$
N_{min} = \frac{\ln\left[\dfrac{x_D}{1 - x_D}\cdot\dfrac{1 - x_B}{x_B}\right]}{\ln \alpha}
$$

## Key formulas for the distillation column working principle

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raoult's law | pᵢ = xᵢ Pᵢˢᵃᵗ | pᵢ = partial pressure (kPa), xᵢ = liquid mole fraction, Pᵢˢᵃᵗ = pure component vapour pressure (kPa) |
| Dalton's law | yᵢ = pᵢ ÷ P | yᵢ = vapour mole fraction, P = total pressure (kPa) |
| Relative volatility | α = (y_A ÷ x_A) ÷ (y_B ÷ x_B) ≈ P_Aˢᵃᵗ ÷ P_Bˢᵃᵗ | dimensionless |
| Binary equilibrium curve | y = α x ÷ [1 + (α − 1) x] | mole fractions of the light component |
| Reflux ratio | R = L ÷ D | L = reflux flow, D = distillate flow (mol/h) |
| Overall material balance | F = D + B and F z_F = D x_D + B x_B | F, D, B in mol/h; z_F, x_D, x_B mole fractions |
| Fenske equation | N_min = ln[(x_D ÷ (1 − x_D)) × ((1 − x_B) ÷ x_B)] ÷ ln α | minimum theoretical stages at total reflux |

## Types of distillation

- **Batch distillation:** a charge is boiled and products taken off over time; flexible for small multiproduct plants.
- **Continuous distillation:** steady feed and products, as described above.
- **Vacuum distillation:** for heat sensitive or high boiling materials; packing is preferred for low pressure drop.
- **Steam distillation:** live steam lowers the boiling temperature of immiscible organics such as essential oils.
- **Azeotropic and extractive distillation:** an added component changes relative volatility to break azeotropes.

## Operation and control

Columns are controlled by managing the energy balance (reboiler duty), material balance (distillate and bottoms flows) and pressure. Key measurements include column pressure and differential pressure across the trays, which warns of flooding, tray temperatures, which indicate composition, and the levels of the reflux drum and column base. [DP transmitters](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/dp-transmitter-level-measurement/) are widely used for those levels. Common problems are flooding (too much vapour or liquid), weeping (too little vapour), foaming and fouling of trays and reboilers. Overall energy use can be reduced by heat integration with [pinch analysis](/chemical-engineering/pinch-analysis/).

## Distillation column interview and viva questions

1. **Why does temperature fall from the bottom to the top of a column?** The top is rich in the lighter, lower boiling component and the bottom in the heavier, higher boiling component.
2. **What happens at total reflux and at minimum reflux?** Total reflux, with no product taken, needs the minimum number of stages given by the Fenske equation, while minimum reflux would need an infinite number of stages.
3. **What reflux ratio is used in practice?** About 1.2 to 1.5 times the minimum reflux, as a trade off between capital cost for more stages and energy cost for more reflux and reboiler duty.
4. **How are ideal stages converted into real trays or packed height?** Actual trays equal ideal stages divided by the overall tray efficiency, and packed height equals ideal stages multiplied by the HETP.
5. **What are flooding and weeping?** Flooding is caused by too much vapour or liquid traffic and is warned of by rising differential pressure, while weeping occurs when vapour flow is too low to hold liquid on the trays.
