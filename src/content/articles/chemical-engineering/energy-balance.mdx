<Key title="Short answer">

An **energy balance** applies the first law of thermodynamics to a process: energy in minus energy out equals energy accumulated. For a steady flow unit with negligible kinetic and potential energy changes it becomes Q − W<sub>s</sub> = ΔH, the heat added minus shaft work done equals outlet minus inlet enthalpy.

</Key>

Energy balances size heaters, coolers, condensers, reboilers and reactor jackets, and they set the steam, cooling water and fuel consumption of a plant. Together with the [material balance](/chemical-engineering/material-balance/), the energy balance is one of the two basic calculations every chemical engineer performs. This guide explains the forms of energy involved, the closed and open system equations, how enthalpy changes are found, and a step by step method with a solved example.

## Forms of energy in an energy balance

A system can hold energy in three forms:

- **Internal energy (U):** energy of molecular motion and interactions; it depends on temperature, phase and composition.
- **Kinetic energy (E<sub>k</sub>):** ½ m v², from bulk motion of the material.
- **Potential energy (E<sub>p</sub>):** m g z, from height in a gravity field.

Energy crosses the system boundary in two ways other than with flowing material:

- **Heat (Q):** energy transferred because of a temperature difference.
- **Work (W):** energy transferred by a force acting through a distance, such as a pump impeller, a compressor or a turbine shaft.

Sign conventions vary between textbooks. This article follows Felder, Rousseau and Bullard: Q is positive when heat is transferred to the system, and W is positive when work is done by the system on the surroundings.

## Closed system energy balance

A closed (batch) system exchanges no material with the surroundings during the process. The first law gives:

$$
Q - W = \Delta U + \Delta E_k + \Delta E_p
$$

For a batch reactor or a sealed tank that does not move, ΔE<sub>k</sub> and ΔE<sub>p</sub> are zero. If no moving parts do work, W = 0 and the heat added equals the change in internal energy. The thermodynamic background is in [laws of thermodynamics explained for engineers](/mechanical-engineering/laws-of-thermodynamics/).

## Open system steady state energy balance

Most continuous process units are open systems at steady state. Material flows in and out, and nothing accumulates. Work is split into **shaft work** W<sub>s</sub>, delivered through a rotating shaft, and **flow work**, the work needed to push streams in and out. Flow work is absorbed by using enthalpy, H = U + PV, for each stream. The result is:

$$
\dot{Q} - \dot{W}_s = \Delta \dot{H} + \Delta \dot{E}_k + \Delta \dot{E}_p
$$

where each Δ term means the total for outlet streams minus the total for inlet streams, for example:

$$
\Delta \dot{H} = \sum_{out} \dot{m}_j \hat{H}_j - \sum_{in} \dot{m}_i \hat{H}_i
$$

Ĥ is specific enthalpy (kJ/kg or kJ/mol). For heat exchangers, heaters, mixers and most reactors, shaft work and the kinetic and potential terms are negligible, so Q̇ = ΔḢ. For an adiabatic unit Q̇ = 0 and the balance gives the outlet temperature.

## Calculating enthalpy changes

Enthalpy is always relative to a **reference state**, a chosen phase, temperature and pressure where Ĥ = 0. Any convenient reference may be used, as long as it is the same for a given species in every stream. Steam tables use liquid water at the triple point as their reference.

Common contributions:

- **Sensible heat:** a temperature change without phase change. With a constant mean heat capacity, ΔĤ = c<sub>p</sub> (T<sub>2</sub> − T<sub>1</sub>). For wide ranges, integrate a c<sub>p</sub> polynomial from Perry's handbook or a similar source.
- **Latent heat:** a phase change at constant temperature, such as the heat of vaporization ΔĤ<sub>v</sub>. Values for water are read from steam tables.
- **Heat of mixing or solution:** significant for some systems, such as sulfuric acid in water.
- **Heat of reaction:** for reactive systems, the standard heat of reaction (or heats of formation) is included. Exothermic reactions release heat that must be removed, which is a key factor when choosing between a [CSTR and a PFR](/chemical-engineering/cstr-vs-pfr/).

A useful approach is to build a **hypothetical path**: take each species from the reference state to its stream condition through steps whose enthalpy changes are tabulated (heat, change phase, mix, react). Because enthalpy is a state function, the path does not change the answer.

## Steps to solve an energy balance problem

1. Draw and label the flowchart; complete the material balance so all stream flows and compositions are known.
2. Choose a reference state for each species.
3. Build an inlet and outlet enthalpy table: flow and specific enthalpy for every species in every stream.
4. Calculate Ĥ for each entry from heat capacities, latent heats, steam tables or heats of formation.
5. Decide which of Q, W<sub>s</sub>, ΔE<sub>k</sub> and ΔE<sub>p</sub> are zero or negligible, and write the simplified balance.
6. Solve for the unknown, usually Q or an outlet temperature, and check that the sign makes physical sense.

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>2.0 kg/s of liquid water is heated from 25 °C to 80 °C in a shell and tube heater. The heating medium is saturated steam at 150 °C, which condenses and leaves as saturated liquid at 150 °C. Mean c<sub>p</sub> of water = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K). Latent heat of steam at 150 °C, ΔĤ<sub>v</sub> = 2113.8 kJ/kg (steam tables).</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>The heat duty and the steam consumption.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Steady state; no heat loss to the surroundings; no shaft work; kinetic and potential energy changes negligible; condensate leaves without subcooling.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>Water side: Q = ṁ c<sub>p</sub> ΔT = 2.0 × 4.18 × (80 − 25) = 459.8 kW<br />Steam side: the heat released equals the heat absorbed, so ṁ<sub>steam</sub> × 2113.8 = 459.8<br />ṁ<sub>steam</sub> = 459.8 / 2113.8 = 0.2175 kg/s<br />In hourly terms: 0.2175 × 3600 = 783 kg/h</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>Heat duty about 460 kW; steam consumption about 0.218 kg/s (783 kg/h)</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Real heaters lose some heat to the surroundings and may subcool the condensate, so actual steam use differs. Using a constant c<sub>p</sub> is acceptable for liquid water over this range but not for gases over wide temperature ranges.</dd>
</dl>
</Example>

The equipment that carries out this duty is described in [heat exchanger types and how they work](/chemical-engineering/heat-exchanger-types/). On a plant, the duty is often checked from measured flow and temperatures; see the [temperature measurement](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/temperature-measurements/) and flow measurement articles on InstrumentationBlog.in.

## Key formulas for the energy balance

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closed system | Q − W = ΔU + ΔE_k + ΔE_p | Q = heat to system (kJ), W = work by system (kJ), U = internal energy (kJ) |
| Open system, steady state | Q̇ − Ẇ_s = ΔḢ + ΔĖ_k + ΔĖ_p | rates in kW; Ẇ_s = shaft work by system |
| Enthalpy change of streams | ΔḢ = Σ out ṁ Ĥ − Σ in ṁ Ĥ | ṁ in kg/s, Ĥ in kJ/kg |
| Enthalpy | H = U + PV | P = pressure, V = volume; PV is the flow work |
| Sensible heat | Q = ṁ c_p (T₂ − T₁) | c_p in kJ/(kg·K), T in °C or K |
| Latent heat | Q = ṁ ΔĤ_v | ΔĤ_v = heat of vaporization (kJ/kg) |
| Kinetic and potential energy | E_k = ½ m v², E_p = m g z | m in kg, v in m/s, g = 9.81 m/s², z in m |

## Applications of the energy balance

- **Utility sizing:** steam, cooling water, refrigeration and fuel demand for each unit.
- **Adiabatic temperature rise:** predicting how hot a reactor gets if cooling fails, an input to process safety studies.
- **Heat recovery:** matching hot and cold streams across a plant, the basis of [pinch analysis](/chemical-engineering/pinch-analysis/).
- **Separation units:** condenser and reboiler duties of a distillation column follow from an overall and stagewise energy balance.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing sign conventions for heat and work from different textbooks.
- Using different reference states for the same species in inlet and outlet streams.
- Forgetting latent heat when a stream changes phase.
- Solving the energy balance before the material balance is complete.

## Energy balance interview and viva questions

1. **What does the first law say for a steady state open system?** Heat added minus shaft work done by the system equals the change in enthalpy, kinetic energy and potential energy of the streams, outlet minus inlet.
2. **Why is flow work not written separately for flowing streams?** It is included in enthalpy, H = U + PV, so using stream enthalpies accounts for the work of pushing material across the boundary.
3. **What is a reference state and does its choice matter?** It is a phase, temperature and pressure where specific enthalpy is taken as zero; any reference works if the same one is used for a species in every stream.
4. **When can kinetic and potential energy terms be neglected?** For heat exchangers, reactors and columns, where they are small compared with enthalpy changes, but not for turbines, nozzles, high velocity gases or large elevation changes.
5. **Why is the material balance solved first?** The stream flows and compositions from the material balance are the inputs needed to calculate the enthalpy of every stream.
