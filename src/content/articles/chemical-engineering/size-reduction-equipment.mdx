faq:
  - q: "What is the difference between crushing and grinding?"
    a: "Crushing reduces large lumps, typically from hundreds of millimetres down to a few millimetres or centimetres, using slow compressive machines such as jaw and cone crushers. Grinding takes the crushed material down to fine powder, often below 1 mm and down to micrometres, in mills that use impact and attrition."
  - q: "What is the critical speed of a ball mill?"
    a: "It is the speed at which the balls would just be carried round the shell by centrifugal force instead of tumbling. For a mill of diameter D and ball diameter d in metres, it is about 42.3 divided by the square root of (D minus d) revolutions per minute. Mills normally run at roughly 65 to 80 percent of it."
  - q: "Why is size reduction so energy intensive?"

<Key title="Short answer">

**Size reduction equipment** is the group of machines that break solids into smaller pieces by compression, impact, attrition and cutting. Crushers handle coarse lumps, grinding mills produce intermediate and fine powders, and ultrafine mills reach micrometre sizes. The energy needed for a given size change is estimated with Rittinger's, Kick's or Bond's law.

</Key>

Many processes need solids of a particular size: cement clinker ground to powder, ores liberated before separation, coal pulverised for burners, catalysts and pharmaceuticals milled for fast reaction or dissolution. Size reduction, also called comminution, increases surface area, frees valuable minerals from waste rock, and makes materials easier to handle, mix and react. It is also one of the largest energy users in mining and cement plants, so choosing the right equipment matters.

## How particles are broken

Every machine uses one or more of four mechanisms:

- **Compression:** the particle is squeezed between two surfaces until it fractures. Used for hard, coarse material in jaw, gyratory and cone crushers and crushing rolls.
- **Impact:** a fast moving hammer or bar strikes the particle, or the particle is thrown against a surface. Used in hammer mills and impact crushers for brittle material.
- **Attrition:** particles are rubbed between surfaces or against each other, producing fines. Important in ball mills and stirred mills.
- **Cutting:** a sharp edge slices tough or fibrous material such as rubber, plastics and plant material in knife cutters.

## Types of size reduction equipment

### Coarse crushers

- **Jaw crusher:** a fixed jaw and a swinging jaw form a V shaped chamber. Material is nipped and crushed as the moving jaw closes, then falls lower as it opens, leaving through the gap at the bottom. Simple and robust for primary crushing of hard rock.
- **Gyratory crusher:** a conical head gyrates inside a conical bowl, crushing continuously around its circumference. High capacity primary crusher for large mines.

### Intermediate crushers

- **Cone crusher:** similar to a gyratory crusher but with a flatter head, used for secondary and tertiary crushing.
- **Crushing rolls:** two rolls rotating towards each other nip and compress particles. Smooth rolls give a product with few fines. High pressure grinding rolls compress a bed of particles and are used in cement and mineral plants for energy savings.
- **Hammer mill:** swinging hammers on a fast rotor shatter friable material by impact against breaker plates; a screen at the outlet controls product size.

### Fine grinders

- **Ball mill:** a rotating horizontal drum partly filled with steel or ceramic balls. Balls are lifted by the shell and cascade or cataract onto the charge, grinding by impact and attrition. Common for cement, ores, ceramics and pigments.
- **Rod mill:** uses long steel rods instead of balls, giving a more uniform product with less overgrinding.
- **Vertical roller mill:** rollers press material against a rotating table, combining grinding, drying and classification. Widely used for cement raw meal and coal.

### Ultrafine grinders

- **Fluid energy (jet) mill:** high velocity air or steam jets make particles collide with each other. No grinding media, so contamination is low; used for pharmaceuticals and pigments.
- **Stirred media mill:** small beads agitated in a slurry produce particles in the micrometre range.

### Cutting machines

- **Knife cutters and granulators** for plastics, rubber, paper and food.

## Energy laws for size reduction

Three classical laws relate energy to the change in particle size. With feed size D₁ and product size D₂:

$$
\text{Rittinger:}\; E = K_R\left(\frac{1}{D_2} - \frac{1}{D_1}\right) \qquad \text{Kick:}\; E = K_K \ln\frac{D_1}{D_2}
$$

Rittinger's law (energy proportional to new surface) fits fine grinding best; Kick's law (energy depends only on reduction ratio) fits coarse crushing. **Bond's law** lies between them and is the one most used in practice:

$$
W = 10\,W_i\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{P_{80}}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{F_{80}}}\right)
$$

W is the work input in kWh per tonne, Wi is the **Bond work index** of the material (the energy to reduce it from very large size to 80 percent passing 100 µm, in kWh per tonne), and F80 and P80 are the sizes in micrometres through which 80 percent of the feed and product pass. Work indices are measured in standard laboratory tests.

## Ball mill operating speed

The critical speed, at which balls would centrifuge and stop grinding, is approximately:

$$
N_c = \frac{42.3}{\sqrt{D - d}}\ \text{rpm}
$$

with mill and ball diameters D and d in metres. Mills run at about 65 to 80 percent of critical speed so that the balls cascade and cataract effectively.

## Key formulas for size reduction equipment

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reduction ratio | R = D₁ / D₂ | D₁ = feed size, D₂ = product size, same length units |
| Rittinger's law | E = K_R (1/D₂ − 1/D₁) | E = energy per unit mass, K_R = Rittinger constant for the material |
| Kick's law | E = K_K ln(D₁/D₂) | E = energy per unit mass, K_K = Kick constant for the material |
| Bond's law | W = 10 W_i (1/√P80 − 1/√F80) | W and W_i in kWh/t, F80 and P80 in µm |
| Mill power | P = W × feed rate | P in kW, W in kWh/t, feed rate in t/h |
| Ball mill critical speed | N_c = 42.3 / √(D − d) | N_c in rpm, D = mill diameter in m, d = ball diameter in m |

## Selecting size reduction equipment

- **Feed and product size:** reduction ratios per stage are limited, so coarse to fine reduction uses several stages.
- **Hardness and abrasiveness:** hard abrasive ores suit compression crushers; soft friable materials suit hammer mills.
- **Moisture and stickiness:** wet sticky feeds clog crushers and screens; wet grinding may be preferred.
- **Heat sensitivity:** cryogenic or jet milling avoids heat damage to plastics and pharmaceuticals.
- **Capacity and energy:** closed circuit grinding with a classifier returns oversize and avoids overgrinding, saving energy.
- **Contamination:** media wear can contaminate products; ceramic linings or jet mills reduce it.

Dust control, noise enclosures and safe guarding of moving parts are essential for all size reduction equipment. Size reduction is usually followed by screening or classification, and the solids are then conveyed, mixed or fed to reactors and separators. Its throughput and product splits enter the plant [material balance](/chemical-engineering/material-balance/). Mill motors and drives are [induction motors](/electrical-engineering/induction-motor-working-principle/), and bearings and gears need correct [lubrication](/mechanical-engineering/types-of-lubrication/). Mill power and feed rate are monitored with standard process instrumentation.

## Size reduction equipment interview and viva questions

1. **Name the four mechanisms used to break particles.** Compression, impact, attrition and cutting; most machines combine more than one, for example a ball mill uses impact and attrition.
2. **Which energy law suits coarse crushing and which suits fine grinding?** Kick's law fits coarse crushing because energy depends only on the reduction ratio, while Rittinger's law, based on new surface, fits fine grinding best.
3. **What is the Bond work index?** It is the energy in kWh per tonne needed to reduce a material from very large size to 80 percent passing 100 µm, measured in standard laboratory tests.
4. **Why does a ball mill run below its critical speed?** At the critical speed the balls centrifuge with the shell and stop grinding, so mills run at about 65 to 80 percent of it to make the balls cascade and cataract.
5. **Why is closed circuit grinding used?** A classifier returns oversize to the mill and removes finished product, which avoids overgrinding and saves energy.
