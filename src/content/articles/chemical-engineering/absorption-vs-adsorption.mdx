<Key title="Short answer">

In **absorption vs adsorption**, absorption is the uptake of a substance into the whole volume of another phase, such as a gas dissolving into a liquid solvent. Adsorption is the accumulation of molecules on the surface of a solid or liquid, usually inside porous solids. Absorption is a bulk process; adsorption is a surface process.

</Key>

The two words differ by one letter, and both remove a component from a gas or liquid stream, so they are easy to confuse. The absorption vs adsorption distinction matters because it decides the equipment, the equilibrium model, the way the separating agent is regenerated and the scale at which each process makes sense. This article compares them side by side, then works a Langmuir isotherm example.

## What absorption and adsorption mean

IUPAC defines **absorption** as a process in which one material (the absorbate) is retained by another (the absorbent) and penetrates its bulk. In chemical engineering the usual case is **gas absorption**: a soluble gas such as ammonia, carbon dioxide or hydrogen sulphide passes into a liquid solvent. The solute spreads through the liquid by diffusion and mixing until it reaches its solubility limit.

**Adsorption** is the enrichment of one or more components at an interface. The solid is the **adsorbent**, and the molecules held on it are the **adsorbate**. Industrial adsorbents such as activated carbon, silica gel, activated alumina and zeolites are highly porous, so almost all of their surface lies inside the pores. IUPAC classifies pores as micropores (below 2 nm wide), mesopores (2 to 50 nm) and macropores (above 50 nm).

## Absorption vs adsorption: comparison table

| Feature | Absorption | Adsorption |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where the solute goes | Into the bulk of the absorbent | Onto the surface of the adsorbent |
| Separating agent | Liquid solvent (water, amines, oils) | Porous solid (activated carbon, zeolites, silica gel, activated alumina) |
| Nature | Bulk phenomenon, solute distributed through volume | Surface phenomenon, capacity set by surface area |
| Equilibrium model | Henry's law for dilute physical absorption | Adsorption isotherms such as Langmuir and Freundlich |
| Heat effect | Heat of solution, plus heat of reaction in chemical absorption | Exothermic; chemisorption releases more heat than physisorption |
| Typical operation | Continuous, steady state counter-current column | Cyclic fixed beds, adsorb then regenerate |
| Regeneration | Stripping or distillation of the rich solvent | Temperature swing, pressure swing or purge, or replacement |
| Best suited to | Large gas flows with moderate to high solute content | Low concentrations, deep purification, drying |
| Typical equipment | Packed or tray absorber with a stripper | Fixed bed adsorbers in pairs or sets |

## Differences explained

### Equilibrium

For dilute physical absorption, equilibrium follows **Henry's law**: the partial pressure of the solute over the liquid is proportional to its mole fraction in the liquid. Solubility rises as temperature falls and as pressure rises, which is why absorbers run cool and often at elevated pressure.

Adsorption equilibrium is described by an **isotherm**, the loading on the solid at a fixed temperature as a function of fluid concentration or pressure. The **Langmuir isotherm** assumes a uniform surface, one molecule per site, a single layer and no interaction between adsorbed molecules. At low pressure it reduces to a straight line, similar to Henry's law. At high pressure the loading levels off at the monolayer capacity, because the surface is full. A liquid can keep dissolving solute up to its solubility limit, but a surface has a fixed number of sites.

### Mass transfer

In absorption, the solute crosses a gas film and a liquid film at the interface, as described by the **two film theory**. Column packing or trays create a large gas and liquid contact area. In adsorption the solute travels from the bulk fluid to the outer surface of the particle, then diffuses into the pores before attaching to the wall. In a fixed bed this creates a **mass transfer zone** that moves down the bed. When it reaches the outlet, the solute concentration rises, giving the **breakthrough curve**, and the bed must be switched and regenerated.

### Physical and chemical uptake

Both processes have a physical and a chemical form. **Physical absorption** dissolves the gas without reaction, while **chemical absorption** uses a reactive solvent, such as aqueous amines for carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulphide, which raises capacity at low partial pressures. Likewise, **physisorption** relies on van der Waals forces and is readily reversed, while **chemisorption** forms surface bonds and is limited to a monolayer.

### Regeneration and operation

An absorber usually runs continuously. The rich solvent goes to a **stripper**, where heat, steam or reduced pressure releases the solute, and the lean solvent returns to the top of the absorber. Adsorbers are batch units in a cyclic process. **Temperature swing adsorption** heats the bed to release the adsorbate, while **pressure swing adsorption** lowers the pressure. Two or more beds alternate so that one adsorbs while another regenerates.

## Equations for absorption and adsorption

Henry's law for a dilute solute A:

$$
p_A = H\,x_A
$$

Langmuir isotherm and fractional coverage:

$$
q = \frac{q_m K p}{1 + K p} \qquad \theta = \frac{q}{q_m}
$$

Solute balance for a counter-current absorber on a solute free basis, with Y the gas phase and X the liquid phase mole ratio, subscript 1 at the bottom and 2 at the top:

$$
G_s\,(Y_1 - Y_2) = L_s\,(X_1 - X_2)
$$

<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>A gas adsorbs on an activated carbon that follows the Langmuir isotherm with monolayer capacity q_m = 4.0 mol/kg and constant K = 0.25 kPa⁻¹ at the operating temperature. The solute partial pressure in the feed is 12 kPa. The bed must hold 45 mol of solute per cycle.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>The equilibrium loading, the fractional coverage, the minimum mass of carbon, and the gain in loading if the partial pressure were doubled.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Isothermal bed; Langmuir model valid; the whole bed reaches equilibrium with the feed (no mass transfer zone left unused); no competing adsorbates.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>K p = 0.25 × 12 = 3.0<br />θ = 3.0 ÷ (1 + 3.0) = 0.75<br />q = 4.0 × 0.75 = 3.0 mol/kg<br />Carbon mass = 45 ÷ 3.0 = 15 kg<br />At 24 kPa: K p = 6.0, θ = 6.0 ÷ 7.0 = 0.857, q = 3.43 mol/kg, a rise of about 14 percent</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>Loading 3.0 mol/kg at 75 percent coverage, so at least 15 kg of carbon is needed. Doubling the partial pressure raises loading by only about 14 percent, because the surface is close to saturation.</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>A real bed needs extra length for the mass transfer zone, and heat released on adsorption raises the bed temperature and lowers capacity. Real carbons have uneven surfaces, so other isotherms may fit data better.</dd>
</dl>
</Example>

## Key formulas for absorption vs adsorption

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Henry's law | p_A = H x_A | p_A = solute partial pressure (kPa), x_A = liquid mole fraction, H = Henry's constant (kPa) |
| Langmuir isotherm | q = q_m K p ÷ (1 + K p) | q = loading (mol/kg), q_m = monolayer capacity (mol/kg), K = Langmuir constant (kPa⁻¹), p in kPa |
| Fractional coverage | θ = q ÷ q_m | θ dimensionless, 0 to 1 |
| Absorber solute balance | G_s (Y_1 − Y_2) = L_s (X_1 − X_2) | G_s, L_s = solute free gas and liquid flows (mol/s), Y, X = mole ratios |
| Adsorbent mass (equilibrium) | m = n ÷ q | n = solute to be held (mol), m in kg |

## When to use absorption or adsorption

- **Choose absorption** for large gas flows that carry a significant amount of soluble gas, where a solvent can be circulated continuously. Examples are acid gas removal from natural gas and synthesis gas, recovery of ammonia, and control of soluble pollutants in a [wet scrubber](/environmental-engineering/wet-scrubber-working-principle/).
- **Choose adsorption** when the impurity is dilute and very low outlet levels are required, or when the target is water vapour or an organic vapour. Examples are drying gases with silica gel, alumina or molecular sieves, solvent vapour recovery on activated carbon, hydrogen purification and air separation by pressure swing adsorption, and activated carbon polishing in the [water treatment process](/environmental-engineering/water-treatment-process/).
- **Combine them** when a bulk cut by absorption is followed by adsorption for final purification.

Absorbers and strippers share their tray and packing hardware with the [distillation column](/chemical-engineering/distillation-column-working-principle/), and the solvent circulation rate comes from the plant [material balance](/chemical-engineering/material-balance/). Rich and lean solvent streams are usually cross exchanged in [heat exchangers](/chemical-engineering/heat-exchanger-types/) to save stripper steam.

## Absorption vs adsorption interview and viva questions

1. **What is the basic difference between absorption and adsorption?** Absorption takes the solute into the bulk of the absorbent, while adsorption holds it only on the surface, so adsorption capacity depends on surface area.
2. **Why do industrial adsorbents have high porosity?** Nearly all the usable surface lies inside the pores, so a porous structure gives a very large surface per unit mass and therefore a high capacity.
3. **What does the Langmuir isotherm assume?** A uniform surface with a fixed number of identical sites, one molecule per site, a single layer and no interaction between adsorbed molecules.
4. **What is breakthrough in a fixed bed adsorber?** It is the point when the mass transfer zone reaches the bed outlet and the solute concentration in the outlet starts to rise, signalling that the bed must be regenerated.
5. **How is the solvent from an absorber regenerated?** The rich solvent is sent to a stripper, where heat, steam or lower pressure drives the solute out, and the lean solvent is returned to the absorber.
