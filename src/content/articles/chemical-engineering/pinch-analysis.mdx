<Key title="Short answer">

**Pinch analysis** is a systematic method for setting energy targets in a process by combining hot and cold streams into composite curves. The pinch is where the curves come closest, separated by the minimum approach temperature ΔTmin. The method gives the minimum hot and cold utility before any heat exchanger network is designed.

</Key>

Most process plants heat some streams and cool others. Using steam to heat a feed while using cooling water to cool a product at a similar temperature wastes energy and money. Pinch analysis, developed by Bodo Linnhoff and colleagues in the late 1970s, gives a systematic way to find how much heat can be recovered between process streams, and sets energy targets that designers can aim for. It has saved large amounts of energy in refineries, chemical plants, food processing and paper mills.

## Basic data: hot and cold streams

The analysis starts from the heat and [material balance](/chemical-engineering/material-balance/). Each stream that changes temperature is listed with:

- **Supply temperature** (where it starts) and **target temperature** (where it must end).
- **Heat capacity flow rate** CP = mass flow × specific heat, in kW/K. The heat load is CP × ΔT.

**Hot streams** must be cooled; **cold streams** must be heated. Hot utility (steam, fired heaters) and cold utility (cooling water, refrigeration) supply what heat recovery cannot.

## Composite curves and the pinch

All hot streams are combined into a single **hot composite curve** on a temperature versus enthalpy (heat load) plot, by adding their CP values over each temperature interval. The same is done for the cold streams. The cold composite is then slid horizontally until, at its closest point, it is exactly ΔTmin below the hot composite.

The plot shows three targets at a glance:

- The **horizontal overlap** is the maximum heat that can be recovered between process streams.
- The part of the cold composite that extends beyond the hot composite at the top is the **minimum hot utility**, QH,min.
- The part of the hot composite that extends beyond the cold composite at the bottom is the **minimum cold utility**, QC,min.

A larger ΔTmin moves the curves apart, raising both utility targets but needing less exchanger area. A smaller ΔTmin saves energy at the cost of more area. The best value minimises total cost.

## The problem table method

Composite curves are good for insight, but targets are calculated numerically with the **problem table algorithm**:

1. **Shift temperatures:** subtract ΔTmin/2 from hot stream temperatures and add ΔTmin/2 to cold stream temperatures, so that any feasible heat transfer becomes possible on a single shifted scale.
2. **Form intervals** between all the shifted supply and target temperatures.
3. **Calculate the surplus or deficit** in each interval: (ΣCP of hot streams − ΣCP of cold streams) × interval width.
4. **Cascade the heat** from the hottest interval downwards, assuming zero hot utility at the top.
5. **Find the largest negative value** in the cascade. Adding that amount as hot utility at the top makes every value zero or positive. That amount is QH,min, the bottom of the cascade is QC,min, and the point where the cascade reaches zero is the pinch.

## The three golden rules of pinch analysis

To achieve the minimum utility targets, a heat exchanger network must obey:

1. **Do not transfer heat across the pinch.** Every kilowatt moved from above to below the pinch adds a kilowatt to both hot and cold utility.
2. **Do not use cold utility above the pinch.** The region above the pinch is a heat sink; it needs only hot utility.
3. **Do not use hot utility below the pinch.** The region below is a heat source; it needs only cold utility.

Designers then build the network starting at the pinch, where matches are most constrained, using rules on the number of streams and CP values to make feasible matches. Each match becomes an exchanger chosen from the available [heat exchanger types](/chemical-engineering/heat-exchanger-types/).

## Key formulas for pinch analysis

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat capacity flow rate | CP = m × cp | m = mass flow in kg/s, cp = specific heat in kJ/(kg K), CP in kW/K |
| Stream heat load | Q = CP × (T_supply − T_target) | Q in kW, temperatures in °C or K (magnitude of the change) |
| Shifted hot temperature | T* = T − ΔTmin/2 | T* = shifted temperature, ΔTmin in K |
| Shifted cold temperature | T* = T + ΔTmin/2 | T* = shifted temperature, ΔTmin in K |
| Interval surplus or deficit | ΔH_i = (ΣCP_hot − ΣCP_cold) × ΔT_i | ΔH_i in kW, ΔT_i = shifted interval width in K |
| Overall energy balance | QC,min − QH,min = ΣQ_hot − ΣQ_cold | Q values in kW |

## Applications and extensions

- **Heat exchanger network design** for new plants and retrofits of existing plants.
- **Placement of utilities:** steam levels, furnaces, heat pumps (should work across the pinch) and heat engines (should not).
- **Distillation integration:** reboilers and condensers should be placed so that they do not cross the pinch; see the [distillation column](/chemical-engineering/distillation-column-working-principle/).
- **Total site analysis:** several plants sharing a steam system.
- **Water and hydrogen pinch:** the same targeting logic for minimum fresh water or hydrogen use.

Large plants such as the [Haber-Bosch ammonia process](/chemical-engineering/haber-bosch-ammonia-process/) are highly heat integrated in this way. Pinch analysis rests on the energy balance from the [laws of thermodynamics](/mechanical-engineering/laws-of-thermodynamics/), and reliable temperature measurement is needed to collect good stream data from operating plants.

## Pinch analysis interview and viva questions

1. **What is the pinch in pinch analysis?** It is the point where the hot and cold composite curves come closest, separated by exactly ΔTmin. It divides the process into a heat sink above and a heat source below.
2. **Why are stream temperatures shifted in the problem table method?** Subtracting ΔTmin/2 from hot streams and adding ΔTmin/2 to cold streams places them on one scale, so any interval where hot is above cold allows feasible heat transfer.
3. **What happens if heat is transferred across the pinch?** Every kilowatt moved from above to below the pinch adds a kilowatt to both the hot utility and the cold utility.
4. **How does ΔTmin affect the design?** A larger ΔTmin raises both utility targets but needs less exchanger area, while a smaller ΔTmin saves energy but needs more area, so the best value minimises total cost.
5. **Where should a heat pump be placed relative to the pinch?** A heat pump should work across the pinch, taking heat from below and delivering it above, while a heat engine should not cross the pinch.
