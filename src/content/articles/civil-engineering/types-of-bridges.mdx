<Key title="Short answer">

The main **types of bridges** by structural form are **beam (girder)**, **truss**, **arch**, **cantilever**, **suspension** and **cable-stayed** bridges. They differ in how they carry load to the supports: beams by bending, trusses by axial forces in members, arches mainly by compression, and suspension and cable-stayed bridges by tension in cables carried to towers. The span, site and loads decide which type is economical.

</Key>

A bridge carries a road, railway, pipeline or footpath over an obstacle. Every bridge has the same job, transferring loads to the ground, but the structural form changes how forces travel. Understanding the types of bridges in terms of load path helps engineers choose a form for a span and helps students connect bridge shapes with the basic actions of bending, compression and tension.

## Parts of a bridge

Whatever the type, most bridges have the same main parts:

- **Superstructure:** the deck, girders, trusses, arches or cables that carry traffic across the span.
- **Bearings:** devices between superstructure and substructure that transfer load while allowing rotation and thermal movement.
- **Substructure:** piers and abutments that carry loads down; abutments also retain the approach embankment.
- **Foundations:** open footings, wells or piles that pass the load to the ground.
- **Expansion joints, parapets, drainage and approach slabs** that complete the structure.

## Beam (girder) bridges

A beam bridge is a deck carried by beams spanning between supports. The beams resist load by **bending**: the top is in compression, the bottom in tension, as shown by [shear force and bending moment diagrams](/civil-engineering/shear-force-and-bending-moment/). Forms include solid slabs for short spans, T-beam and box girders in reinforced or prestressed concrete, and steel plate or box girders acting compositely with a concrete deck.

Beam bridges are the most common type because they are simple and fast to build, especially with precast or launched girders. Bending moment grows with the square of span, so very long beam spans need deep, heavy sections, which limits their economy. Continuous girders over several supports reduce the midspan moment.

## Truss bridges

A truss is a framework of members arranged in triangles. Loads applied at the joints produce mainly **axial tension or compression** in each member, so material is used efficiently. Common patterns include Pratt, Warren and Howe trusses. Truss bridges are popular for railways and for steel bridges where long members can be fabricated and bolted on site. Members are usually built from [steel sections](/civil-engineering/steel-sections-types/) such as angles, channels, I-sections and box sections.

## Arch bridges

An arch carries load mainly in **compression** along its curved shape, which suits masonry and concrete because both are strong in compression. The arch pushes outwards at its ends (horizontal thrust), so it needs strong abutments or rock, or a tie that holds the ends together (tied arch or bowstring). The deck can sit on top of the arch, hang below it, or pass through it.

## Cantilever bridges

Cantilever arms project from piers, often balanced on each side, and either meet at midspan or carry a short suspended span between them. The arms are in **hogging** bending, with tension at the top. Balanced cantilever construction, adding segments alternately on each side of a pier, lets large concrete box girder bridges be built without falsework over rivers and valleys.

## Suspension bridges

The deck hangs from vertical hangers attached to two main cables. The cables, in **tension**, pass over tall towers and are anchored into massive anchorages at each end. The towers are in compression. This form achieves the longest spans in the world because high strength steel wire is extremely efficient in tension. Suspension decks are relatively flexible, so wind stability must be studied carefully.

## Cable-stayed bridges

Straight cables run directly from one or more towers to points along the deck. Cable tension supports the deck and, because the cables are inclined, it also compresses the deck horizontally, balancing the cable forces within the structure. Cable-stayed bridges are stiffer than suspension bridges, need no heavy anchorages and are very economical for medium to long spans. Cables can be arranged in fan, harp or semi-fan patterns.

## Comparing the types of bridges

| Type | Main action | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beam (girder) | Bending | Short to medium spans; flyovers, river bridges, viaducts |
| Truss | Axial tension and compression | Railways, steel bridges, medium spans |
| Arch | Compression with horizontal thrust | Valleys and gorges with good rock |
| Cantilever | Hogging bending | Long concrete box girders, balanced construction |
| Cable-stayed | Cable tension, deck compression | Medium to long spans |
| Suspension | Main cable tension | The longest spans |

Bridges are also classified by material (timber, masonry, RCC, prestressed concrete, steel, composite), by use (road, rail, pedestrian, pipeline), by movement (fixed, bascule, swing, lift) and by position of the deck (deck, through, half through).

## Choosing a bridge type

Engineers weigh span and clearance needed, foundation conditions (an arch needs firm abutments; see [bearing capacity of soil](/civil-engineering/bearing-capacity-of-soil/)), loads, construction method and access, materials and skills available, maintenance, cost and appearance. Concrete superstructures are designed by [limit state design](/civil-engineering/limit-state-design-rcc/), while complex forms such as cable-stayed bridges are analysed with computer models based on [finite element analysis](/mechanical-engineering/finite-element-analysis-basics/).
