<Key title="Short answer">

The Proctor compaction test is a laboratory test that compacts soil in a mould at several water contents with a fixed rammer energy, then plots dry density against water content. The peak of that curve gives the maximum dry density and the optimum moisture content, which are used to specify and check field compaction.

</Key>

The Proctor compaction test, introduced by R. R. Proctor in 1933, tells you how dense a soil can be made with a given effort and how much water it needs to get there. Earthworks for embankments, road subgrades, dam cores and backfill behind walls are all specified as a percentage of the laboratory maximum dry density. Two versions are in use: the standard (light) test and the modified (heavy) test.

## What the Proctor compaction test measures

Compaction pushes soil particles closer together by removing air from the voids. It raises shear strength and stiffness, which matters for [bearing capacity](/civil-engineering/bearing-capacity-of-soil/), and it reduces settlement and permeability. Water helps up to a point: a little moisture lubricates the particles so they slide into a denser packing. Past a certain water content the water itself fills the space and the dry density falls again.

In the test, each specimen is weighed in a mould of known volume, giving the bulk density, and a sample is oven dried to find its water content. The dry density, which is the mass of solids per unit volume, is then plotted against water content. The peak of the curve gives the maximum dry density (MDD), the highest dry density reached with that compactive effort, and the optimum moisture content (OMC), the water content at which the MDD occurs.

## Standard and modified Proctor test compared

The two tests use the same steps. They differ in rammer mass, drop height and number of layers, so the energy delivered to each cubic metre of soil is different. ASTM gives the energy in its test titles; the other values below come from the standards in the reference list.

| Item | Standard (light) | Modified (heavy) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ASTM / AASHTO | ASTM D698, AASHTO T 99 | ASTM D1557, AASHTO T 180 |
| Indian Standard | IS 2720 (Part 7):1980 | IS 2720 (Part 8):1983 |
| Rammer | 24.4 N (5.5 lbf); IS 2.6 kg | 44.5 N (10 lbf); IS 4.9 kg |
| Free drop | 305 mm (12 in); IS 310 mm | 457 mm (18 in); IS 450 mm |
| Layers | 3 | 5 |
| Blows per layer, small mould | 25 | 25 |
| Small mould volume | 944 cm³ (ASTM 101.6 mm); 1000 cm³ (IS) | Same moulds |
| Compactive energy | 600 kN·m/m³ | 2700 kN·m/m³ |

For coarser soils a larger mould is used with 56 blows per layer, which keeps the energy per unit volume the same. IS 2720 uses a 2250 cm³ mould for soils with particles up to 20 mm, and ASTM D698 Method C uses a 152.4 mm mould. The modified test was developed for the heavier compaction plant used on airfields and highways, and it typically gives a higher MDD at a lower OMC, as Das and Sobhan show.

## Proctor compaction test procedure step by step

1. Air dry the soil, break up the lumps and sieve it (4.75 mm for the small IS mould).
2. Mix a portion with water to a low starting water content and let it stand so the moisture spreads evenly.
3. Weigh the empty mould with its base plate, then fit the collar.
4. Place the soil in equal layers and give each layer the specified number of blows, spread evenly over the surface, with the rammer falling freely through its full drop.
5. Remove the collar, trim the soil level with the top of the mould and weigh the mould with soil.
6. Extrude the specimen and take a sample from its middle for an oven dry water content test.
7. Raise the water content by 1 to 2 percent and repeat until the wet mass falls or stops rising, so the peak is bracketed.

Four or five points are normally used, with about two on each side of the optimum.

## Compaction curve, OMC and zero air voids line

The compaction curve is the dry density plotted against water content for each specimen. On the dry side of optimum, adding water lets the particles move into a denser arrangement, so the dry density rises. On the wet side, water occupies space that solids could fill and the dry density falls.

The zero air voids (ZAV) line is the dry density the soil would have if every void were filled with water (degree of saturation S = 1). It depends only on the water content and the specific gravity of solids Gs. No compaction can remove all the air, so the measured curve always lies below the ZAV line, and on the wet side it runs nearly parallel to it. Lines for 5 or 10 percent air voids are often drawn as well. If a test point plots above the ZAV line, Gs or the water content is wrong.

Compactive effort shifts the curve. More energy raises the MDD and lowers the OMC, so the modified curve sits above and to the left of the standard one. Soil type matters too: well graded sands and gravels reach higher dry densities with sharp peaks, while clays give lower, flatter curves at higher water contents.

## Key formulas for the Proctor compaction test

$$
\rho_d = \frac{\rho}{1 + w}
$$

$$
\rho_{zav} = \frac{G_s \, \rho_w}{1 + w \, G_s}
$$

$$
E = \frac{N_L \, N_B \, W \, h}{V}
$$

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bulk density | ρ = M ÷ V | M = wet soil mass in g, V = mould volume in cm³ |
| Dry density | ρd = ρ ÷ (1 + w) | w = water content as a decimal, densities in g/cm³ |
| Zero air voids density | ρzav = Gs ρw ÷ (1 + w Gs) | Gs = specific gravity of solids, ρw = density of water |
| Air content | na = 1 − (ρd ÷ ρw)(1 ÷ Gs + w) | na as a decimal of total volume |
| Compactive energy | E = NL × NB × W × h ÷ V | NL = layers, NB = blows per layer, W = rammer weight in N, h = drop in m, V in m³, E in J/m³ |
| Relative compaction | RC = ρd,field ÷ MDD × 100 | percent |

You can check the energies in the comparison table with the last formula. For the ASTM standard test, 3 × 25 × 24.4 N × 0.305 m ÷ 0.000944 m³ is about 591 kN·m/m³, which the standard rounds to 600. For the IS heavy test, 5 × 25 × 4.9 × 9.81 N × 0.45 m ÷ 0.001 m³ is about 2700 kN·m/m³.

## Using Proctor test results in the field

The laboratory MDD becomes the target for the site. A specification states a minimum relative compaction, often 90 to 95 percent of standard or modified MDD, and a band of water content around the OMC. The field dry density is measured on each compacted lift with a sand replacement test, a core cutter or a nuclear gauge, and compared with the MDD. For example, a field dry density of 1.78 g/cm³ against an MDD of 1.86 g/cm³ gives a relative compaction of 1.78 ÷ 1.86 = 95.7 percent.

Water content also changes the soil structure. Clays compacted dry of optimum tend to have a flocculated structure with higher permeability, while those compacted wet of optimum are more dispersed, less permeable and more compressible, as Das and Sobhan explain. That is why the water content band matters as much as the density for clay cores and liners. Well compacted subgrade is the base of every [flexible or rigid pavement](/civil-engineering/flexible-vs-rigid-pavement/), and compacted fill under shallow [foundations](/civil-engineering/types-of-foundations/) relies on the same control.

## Factors that affect the compaction curve

- Compactive effort: higher energy raises MDD and lowers OMC.
- Soil type and grading: well graded granular soils reach higher densities than uniform sands or plastic clays.
- Water content decides which side of the peak the soil sits on.
- Reuse of soil: recompacting the same sample breaks particles and can raise the density, so fresh soil for each point is preferred for soils that crush easily.
- Oversize particles: particles larger than the mould allows must be removed and, if significant, corrected for.

## Proctor compaction test interview and viva questions

1. **What two results does the Proctor compaction test give?** The maximum dry density and the optimum moisture content, read from the peak of the dry density against water content curve.
2. **What is the difference between the standard and modified Proctor tests?** The modified test uses a heavier rammer, a higher drop and five layers instead of three, giving about 2700 kN·m/m³ against 600 kN·m/m³.
3. **Why can the compaction curve never cross the zero air voids line?** The ZAV line is the density at full saturation, and compaction cannot remove all the air from the voids.
4. **What happens to MDD and OMC when the compactive effort increases?** The MDD rises and the OMC falls, so the curve moves up and to the left.
5. **How are laboratory results used on site?** The field dry density of each lift is compared with the MDD to give relative compaction, which must meet the specified minimum.
