<Key title="Short answer">

In **surface vs underground mining**, the choice depends mainly on how deep the orebody is. **Surface mining** removes overburden to reach shallow deposits: **open pit**, **strip mining**, **quarrying** and **placer** methods. It offers high production, low cost per tonne and good safety, but disturbs large areas and becomes uneconomic as the **stripping ratio** rises with depth. **Underground mining** reaches deep or narrow deposits through **shafts, declines and adits**, using methods such as **room and pillar**, **cut and fill**, **sublevel stoping** and **block caving**. It has a smaller surface footprint but higher cost, slower production and more complex ventilation and ground control.

</Key>

Every mine plan begins with a basic question: should the deposit be mined from the surface or from underground? The answer shapes capital cost, production rate, recovery, safety, environmental impact and the life of the operation. Geology, depth, shape, grade and economics all play a part.

## Surface mining methods

### Open pit mining

A large excavation is developed in a series of horizontal **benches**, typically 10 to 15 m high, using drilling and blasting, shovels or excavators and haul trucks. Ramps spiral down the pit walls for haulage. Used for large, near-surface, often massive deposits of copper, gold, iron ore and diamonds. Pit slope angles, set by geotechnical studies, strongly affect the amount of waste to be moved.

### Strip mining

Used for flat lying, layered deposits such as coal, lignite, phosphate and oil sands. Overburden is removed in long strips and placed in the mined-out strip behind, often by draglines or bucket wheel excavators. This allows progressive backfilling and reclamation.

### Quarrying

Extraction of building stone, aggregates and limestone from benches, sometimes as dimension stone cut in blocks.

### Placer and dredging

Recovery of heavy minerals such as gold, tin and mineral sands from loose sediments using water, dredges and gravity separation.

### Stripping ratio

The key economic measure of surface mining is the **stripping ratio**:

$$
SR = \frac{\text{waste removed}}{\text{ore mined}}
$$

The **break-even stripping ratio** is the ratio at which open pit mining costs as much as underground mining:

$$
SR_{be} = \frac{C_{ug} - C_{op}}{C_{w}}
$$

where C_ug and C_op are underground and open pit mining costs per tonne of ore, and C_w is the cost of moving a tonne of waste.

## Underground mining methods

Access is by vertical **shafts** with hoists, inclined **declines** for trucks, or horizontal **adits** into hillsides. Methods are grouped by how the ground is supported.

### Naturally supported methods

- **Room and pillar:** for flat lying coal, potash, salt and some metal deposits. Rooms are mined out, leaving pillars of ore to support the roof. Pillars may be partly recovered at the end.
- **Sublevel and open stoping:** large open voids (stopes) are drilled and blasted from sublevels in strong rock; broken ore is drawn from the bottom. Highly productive for steep, regular orebodies.

### Artificially supported methods

- **Cut and fill:** ore is mined in horizontal slices, and each void is filled with waste rock, sand or cemented paste backfill, which forms the floor for the next slice. Suits irregular, high grade ores and weak rock; selective but costly.
- **Longwall mining:** a shearer cuts along a long coal face under hydraulic roof supports that advance with the face; the roof caves behind. Very high production in coal.

### Caving methods

- **Block caving:** a large block of ore is undercut so it caves under its own weight; broken ore is drawn through drawpoints below. Very low cost per tonne for massive, low grade deposits at depth, but high upfront development and surface subsidence.
- **Sublevel caving:** ore is blasted from sublevels while the hanging wall caves behind.

## Surface vs underground mining: comparison

| Factor | Surface mining | Underground mining |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Depth | Shallow, usually up to a few hundred metres | Deep, can exceed 3000 m |
| Production rate | Very high | Lower, except block caving |
| Cost per tonne of ore | Low | Higher |
| Ore recovery | High, often above 90% | Variable, 50 to 90% depending on method |
| Dilution | Low to moderate | Moderate to high in some methods |
| Safety | Generally safer | Higher risk; needs ground control and ventilation |
| Surface disturbance | Large pits and waste dumps | Smaller footprint; subsidence possible with caving |
| Capital and development time | Lower, faster start | Higher, longer development |

## Factors in choosing a method

- **Depth and shape of the orebody:** shallow, wide deposits favour surface mining; deep, steep or narrow ones favour underground.
- **Grade:** high grade ore can support costlier selective underground methods.
- **Rock strength:** strong rock allows large open stopes; weak rock needs fill or caving. See [rock mass classification](/mining-engineering/rock-mass-classification/).
- **Production rate and mine life** targets.
- **Environmental and social constraints:** land availability, water, communities and closure plans, assessed through an [environmental impact assessment](/environmental-engineering/environmental-impact-assessment/).
- **Safety and workforce** considerations, including [mine ventilation](/mining-engineering/mine-ventilation-systems/) and [mine hazards](/mining-engineering/mine-hazards-and-controls/).

## Trends in mining methods

Modern mines increasingly use automation and remote operation: autonomous haul trucks in open pits, tele-remote loaders underground, and battery electric equipment that reduces diesel emissions and ventilation demand. Data from fleet management, drill monitoring and geotechnical sensors feeds digital mine plans updated in near real time. Deep, low grade orebodies are pushing more operations toward large scale caving, while environmental pressure encourages progressive rehabilitation of surface mines and paste backfill underground to reduce surface tailings.

Both approaches rely on [drilling and blasting](/mining-engineering/drilling-and-blasting-basics/) to break rock and on [mineral processing](/mining-engineering/froth-flotation/) to turn ore into saleable concentrate. Choosing between surface vs underground mining is ultimately an engineering and economic optimisation over the whole life of the deposit.
