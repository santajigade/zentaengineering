<Key title="Short answer">

**Mine ventilation systems** supply fresh air to underground workings and remove contaminated air. **Main fans** at the surface create a pressure difference that drives air down **intake shafts**, through the working areas, and out through **return airways** and **upcast shafts**. **Doors, stoppings, regulators and air crossings** direct the flow, while **auxiliary fans and ducts** ventilate dead-end headings. Ventilation provides oxygen, dilutes **methane, CO, NOx, diesel exhaust and dust**, and controls **heat**. Airflow and pressure follow **Atkinson's equation**, p = RQ².

</Key>

Ventilation is often called the lifeblood of an underground mine. It is also one of the largest energy users, often consuming a significant share of a mine's electricity. Designing an efficient, safe ventilation system is a core responsibility of mining engineers.

## Purposes of mine ventilation

- **Oxygen supply:** keep oxygen at or above 19.5% by volume.
- **Gas dilution:** dilute methane well below its lower explosive limit of 5% (operational limits are usually 1 to 1.5%), and remove CO, CO₂, H₂S, NO₂ and radon.
- **Diesel exhaust:** dilute particulate matter and gases from loaders, trucks and drills.
- **Dust control:** carry away respirable dust, including silica and coal dust.
- **Heat and humidity control:** remove heat from rock, machines and auto-compression of air in deep shafts, keeping working temperatures acceptable.
- **Blasting fumes:** clear fumes after blasting so workers can safely re-enter.

- **Main fans:** large axial or centrifugal fans, usually at the top of an upcast shaft (exhausting) or downcast shaft (forcing). They run continuously, often with standby units.
- **Shafts and airways:** downcast (intake) and upcast (return) shafts, and drifts, declines and roadways that carry air.
- **Stoppings:** walls that separate intake and return airways, preventing short circuits.
- **Doors and airlocks:** allow traffic between airways while limiting leakage.
- **Regulators:** adjustable openings that add resistance to a branch to control how much air it receives.
- **Air crossings (overcasts):** let intake and return airways cross without mixing.
- **Booster fans:** underground fans that boost flow in a section, used with care to avoid recirculation.
- **Auxiliary fans and ducts:** supply air to dead-end headings, as forcing (blowing fresh air to the face), exhausting (sucking from the face), or overlap systems.
- **Monitoring:** fixed sensors for methane, CO, airflow velocity, temperature and differential pressure, linked to the surface control room.

## Airflow and pressure: Atkinson's equation

Air moving through an airway loses pressure to friction. Atkinson's square law gives:

$$
p = R\,Q^2
$$

where p is the frictional pressure drop (Pa), Q the airflow (m³/s) and R the airway **resistance** (N·s²/m⁸). Resistance depends on airway length, perimeter, area and roughness:

$$
R = \frac{k\,L\,P}{A^3}
$$

where k is the Atkinson friction factor (kg/m³), L length, P perimeter and A cross-sectional area. The strong A³ term shows why larger airways greatly reduce resistance. Airways in series add resistances; in parallel they share flow, similar to [electrical circuits](/electrical-engineering/ohms-law-kirchhoffs-laws/), and network analysis uses Kirchhoff style laws for flow and pressure.

The fan's air power is:

$$
P_\text{air} = p \times Q
$$

## Ventilation surveys

Ventilation engineers regularly measure airflow and pressure throughout the mine. Anemometers measure air velocity across a cross section, which multiplied by area gives quantity. Pressure surveys with barometers or gauges and tubes give the pressure drop along each branch, from which resistances are calculated and the ventilation network model is calibrated. Smoke tubes show the direction of slow airflows. Survey results reveal leakage through stoppings, recirculation and areas with too little air.

## Determining air quantity

Required airflow is set by the largest of several needs: diesel engine power (m³/s per kW), number of workers, gas emission rates, dust and heat loads, and minimum velocities in workings. Regulations specify minimum quantities and velocities. Typical working face velocities range from about 0.3 to 4 m/s, high enough to sweep gas and dust but low enough to avoid raising dust.

## Ventilation on demand and energy saving

Fan power rises with the cube of airflow, following the same affinity laws that apply to [centrifugal pumps](/mechanical-engineering/centrifugal-pump-working-principle/). **Ventilation on demand (VOD)** systems use tracking of vehicles and people, gas sensors and variable speed drives to send air only where and when it is needed, cutting energy use substantially. Battery electric vehicles reduce diesel exhaust and allow lower airflow. Well sealed stoppings and larger, smoother airways also reduce losses.

## Cooling and heating

Deep mines become hot because rock temperature increases with depth (the geothermal gradient) and air heats up as it descends shafts (auto-compression, about 1 °C per 100 m). Refrigeration plants, bulk air coolers and ice systems cool intake air, using the same principles as the [vapour compression refrigeration cycle](/mechanical-engineering/vapour-compression-refrigeration-cycle/). In cold climates, intake air is heated to stop shafts from icing.

## Mine ventilation systems and safety

Ventilation is a primary control against explosions, toxic exposure and heat illness. Plans show airflow directions and quantities, are updated as the mine advances, and are checked by regular surveys. Fan failure triggers alarms and evacuation procedures. Ventilation also matters after [drilling and blasting](/mining-engineering/drilling-and-blasting-basics/), when fumes must clear before re-entry, and in fire emergencies, when controlled airflow keeps smoke away from escape routes, as covered in [mine hazards and controls](/mining-engineering/mine-hazards-and-controls/).
