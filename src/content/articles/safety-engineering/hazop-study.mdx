<Key title="Short answer">

A **HAZOP study** (hazard and operability study) is a structured team review that finds hazards and operability problems in a process design. The **P&IDs** are divided into **nodes**. For each node, the team states the **design intent**, then combines **parameters** (flow, pressure, temperature, level, composition) with **guide words** (**no, more, less, reverse, as well as, part of, other than**) to generate **deviations** such as "no flow" or "more pressure". For each credible deviation, the team records **causes**, **consequences**, existing **safeguards** and **recommendations**. The method is described in **IEC 61882**.

</Key>

HAZOP was developed at ICI in the UK in the 1960s and is now the standard hazard identification method for chemical plants, refineries, oil and gas facilities, pharmaceutical plants and many other process industries. Its strength is that a multidisciplinary team, guided by simple words, systematically asks "what if the process does not behave as intended?" for every part of the design.

## The HAZOP team

A typical team of five to eight people includes:

- **HAZOP leader (chair or facilitator):** independent, experienced in the method; guides discussion and keeps it systematic.
- **Scribe (recorder):** documents the discussion in the worksheet, often with HAZOP software.
- **Process engineer:** explains the design intent and process behaviour.
- **Instrument and control engineer:** knows the control loops, alarms and trips.
- **Operations representative:** brings practical operating experience.
- **Mechanical, safety, maintenance and vendor specialists** as needed.

The leader needs to keep the team focused on identifying problems, not designing solutions in the meeting.

1. **Prepare:** define scope and objectives, collect up to date P&IDs, process flow diagrams, heat and material balances, cause and effect charts, equipment data sheets and operating procedures. The relationship between these drawings is explained in [PFD vs P&ID](/chemical-engineering/pfd-vs-pid/).
2. **Divide into nodes:** sections of the process with a clear design intent, such as a feed line, a reactor, a column or a heat exchanger train.
3. **Describe the design intent** of the node: what it should do, with what conditions.
4. **Generate deviations** by combining parameters with guide words.
5. **Identify causes** that could realistically produce each deviation.
6. **Evaluate consequences**, assuming safeguards fail, to understand the worst credible outcome.
7. **List safeguards** already in the design: control loops, alarms, trips, relief valves, procedures.
8. **Make recommendations** where safeguards are judged inadequate, often after ranking risk with a [risk matrix](/safety-engineering/risk-matrix/).
9. **Follow up** to ensure every action is closed.

## Guide words and deviations

| Guide word | Meaning | Example deviation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No (not, none) | Complete negation of intent | No flow |
| More | Quantitative increase | More pressure, more temperature |
| Less | Quantitative decrease | Less flow, less level |
| As well as | Qualitative addition | Contamination, extra phase (water in oil) |
| Part of | Qualitative decrease | Wrong concentration, missing component |
| Reverse | Logical opposite | Reverse flow |
| Other than | Complete substitution | Wrong material, other operating mode |

Additional guide words such as **early, late, before and after** are useful for batch and sequence operations. Teams also review **other operating modes**: start-up, shutdown, maintenance, sampling, utility failure and emergency.

## The HAZOP worksheet

Each row typically includes: node, parameter, guide word, deviation, causes, consequences, safeguards, risk ranking (optional), recommendation, responsible party and action number. Software tools manage worksheets, action tracking and reports.

## Strengths and limitations of a HAZOP study

**Strengths:** systematic and thorough; draws on the knowledge of many disciplines; finds both safety and operability problems; creates a record that supports design changes, operator training and later reviews.

**Limitations:** time consuming; depends on team experience and good leadership; focuses on process deviations and may miss hazards such as dropped objects or external events unless specifically addressed; relies on up to date drawings. It identifies hazards but does not quantify risk; methods such as LOPA, [FMEA](/safety-engineering/fmea-explained/) and quantitative risk assessment complement it.

## HAZOP for batch and procedural operations

Continuous processes are studied line by line, but batch processes and procedures are studied step by step. For each step of a batch recipe or operating procedure, the team applies guide words such as **no** (step omitted), **more** or **less** (wrong quantity), **early** or **late**, **before** or **after** (wrong sequence) and **other than** (wrong action). This procedural HAZOP finds hazards in charging, heating, sampling, transferring and cleaning operations that a line by line study may miss.

## Making HAZOP effective

- Use accurate, frozen drawings and give the team time to review them first.
- Choose node sizes that keep discussions focused.
- Limit session length to keep concentration high.
- Record actions clearly and specifically, and track them to closure.
- Revisit the HAZOP when modifications are made, under management of change procedures.

Good HAZOPs prevent accidents long before a plant is built. Combined with sound [root cause analysis](/safety-engineering/root-cause-analysis-methods/) of incidents during operation, they form the backbone of process safety management.
