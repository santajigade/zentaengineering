<Key title="Short answer">

**FMEA** (failure modes and effects analysis) is a structured, bottom-up method to find how a product or process could fail, what the effects would be, why it could happen and how to prevent or detect it. For each **failure mode**, the team rates **severity (S)** of the effect, **occurrence (O)** of the cause and **detection (D)** of the current controls, each typically from 1 to 10. Their product is the **risk priority number**, **RPN = S × O × D**, used to prioritise actions. The **AIAG-VDA** method now uses an **Action Priority** table that emphasises severity. Main types are **design FMEA** and **process FMEA**.

</Key>

FMEA was developed by the US military in the late 1940s, adopted by NASA for the Apollo programme and spread through the automotive, aerospace, medical device and process industries. It is a proactive tool: it looks for failures before they happen, while the design or process can still be changed cheaply.

## Types of FMEA

- **Design FMEA (DFMEA):** analyses product designs: components, interfaces, materials, tolerances and functions.
- **Process FMEA (PFMEA):** analyses manufacturing and assembly processes, step by step.
- **System FMEA:** analyses systems and subsystems and their interactions.
- **FMEA-MSR (monitoring and system response):** for diagnostics in mechatronic systems.
- **FMECA:** adds criticality analysis with quantitative failure rates.

## The FMEA team

A cross functional team of design, manufacturing, quality, maintenance and, where useful, supplier and customer representatives, led by a facilitator. FMEA relies on collective knowledge; an FMEA done by one person at a desk misses most of its value.

1. **Define scope:** the system, subsystem, component or process, with block diagrams or process flow charts.
2. **List functions or process steps** and their requirements.
3. **Identify failure modes:** the ways each function could fail, such as cracked, leaking, wrong dimension, missing part, no output.
4. **Determine effects** of each failure mode on the next level, the end user, safety and regulations. Rate **severity** (1 = no effect, 10 = hazardous without warning).
5. **Identify causes** (design weaknesses or process errors) and rate **occurrence** (1 = extremely unlikely, 10 = very high).
6. **List current controls:** prevention controls that reduce occurrence and detection controls such as tests and inspections. Rate **detection** (1 = almost certain to be detected, 10 = cannot be detected).
7. **Calculate RPN** or determine action priority.
8. **Recommend actions,** assign owners and target dates.
9. **Implement and re-rate** S, O and D to confirm risk reduction.
10. **Keep the FMEA as a living document,** updated with design changes, field failures and lessons learned.

## RPN calculation

$$
RPN = S \times O \times D
$$

RPN ranges from 1 to 1000. Severity can usually only be reduced by changing the design; occurrence by preventing causes; detection by better inspection or testing. Prevention is preferred to detection.

## Action priority (AIAG-VDA)

The 2019 AIAG-VDA handbook replaced RPN thresholds with an **Action Priority (AP)** table that rates each combination of S, O and D as **high**, **medium** or **low** priority. High severity (9 to 10) failure modes get high priority unless occurrence and detection are both very low. This avoids the problem where, for example, S = 10, O = 2, D = 2 (RPN 40) ranks below a nuisance failure with S = 4, O = 5, D = 5 (RPN 100).

## Rating scales

Each organisation should use defined rating scales so that teams score consistently. Severity scales describe effects on safety, regulatory compliance, function and customer satisfaction; a 9 or 10 is reserved for safety or legal non-compliance. Occurrence scales link ratings to evidence such as failure rates, process capability (Cpk) or field data. Detection scales describe how likely the current controls are to catch the failure before it reaches the customer, from error proofing (very likely) to no control (cannot detect). Using examples from past products helps calibrate the team.

## Linking FMEA to the control plan

Every high priority failure mode should lead to a control in the control plan: an error proofing device, an inspection with a defined frequency and reaction plan, or a statistical process control chart. The control plan lists each characteristic, the measurement method, sample size and frequency, and what to do when results are out of limits. This link turns the FMEA from a document into real protection on the shop floor.

## Benefits and limitations of FMEA

**Benefits:** finds failure modes early, improves design and process robustness, prioritises resources, documents knowledge, supports control plans and meets customer and regulatory requirements (such as IATF 16949 in automotive and ISO 14971 risk management for medical devices).

**Limitations:** time consuming for complex systems; considers single failures and may miss combinations and human or software interactions; subjective ratings; can become a paperwork exercise if not used to drive action. Combining FMEA with fault tree analysis, [HAZOP](/safety-engineering/hazop-study/) for process plants, and data from [reliability measures](/safety-engineering/mtbf-mttr-availability/) addresses some of these gaps.

## FMEA in practice

FMEA is most effective when started early, reviewed at design and process changes, linked to the control plan and [control charts](/manufacturing-engineering/control-charts-spc/) for critical characteristics, and updated with field failure data from [root cause analysis](/safety-engineering/root-cause-analysis-methods/). It is also a core tool in the Improve phase of [Six Sigma DMAIC](/manufacturing-engineering/six-sigma-dmaic/) and in reliability centred maintenance.
