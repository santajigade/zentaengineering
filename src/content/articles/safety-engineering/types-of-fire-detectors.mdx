<Key title="Short answer">

The main types of **fire detectors** are: **smoke detectors** (optical or photoelectric, ionisation, beam and aspirating), which give the earliest warning in most buildings; **heat detectors** (fixed temperature and rate of rise, including linear heat cable), for areas where smoke detectors would false alarm; **flame detectors** (UV, IR, UV/IR and multi-spectrum IR), for fast flaming fires in large or outdoor spaces such as process plants and hangars; **gas detectors** for carbon monoxide or flammable gases; and **multi-sensor detectors** that combine signals to reduce false alarms. Choice depends on the expected fire type, environment, ceiling height and required response speed.

</Key>

Early detection is the foundation of fire safety. It gives people time to escape, alerts the fire brigade and can trigger automatic systems such as sprinklers, gas suppression, smoke control and plant shutdown. No single detector suits every place, so engineers match detector types to the fire risk and environment, following codes such as NFPA 72, EN 54, BS 5839 and IS 2189.

## How fires develop

A fire typically passes through stages: **incipient** (invisible combustion products and fine particles), **smouldering** (visible smoke), **flaming** (flames and heat) and **intense heat**. Different detectors respond at different stages. Smoke detectors usually react first, heat detectors later, and flame detectors respond instantly once flames are visible to them.

## Smoke detectors

### Optical (photoelectric) point detectors

An LED shines into a dark chamber. In clean air the light misses the photodiode; smoke particles scatter light onto it, triggering an alarm. Optical detectors are most sensitive to the larger, visible particles of **smouldering fires** (upholstery, cables, paper) and are the standard choice for offices, hotels, homes, corridors and escape routes.

### Ionisation point detectors

A small radioactive source (americium-241) ionises air between two electrodes, producing a tiny current. Smoke particles attach to ions and reduce the current. Very sensitive to small particles from **fast flaming fires**, but prone to false alarms from cooking and draughts. Their use is declining because of environmental rules on radioactive sources.

### Optical beam detectors

A transmitter sends an infrared beam across a large open space, such as a warehouse, atrium or hall, to a receiver or reflector up to about 100 m away. Smoke reduces the received signal. Suitable for high ceilings where point detectors are hard to reach and smoke spreads out.

### Aspirating smoke detection (ASD)

A fan draws air continuously through a network of pipes with sampling holes to a very sensitive laser detector. ASD gives **very early warning** and is used in data centres, clean rooms, telecom rooms, heritage buildings, cold stores and places where detectors must be hidden or are hard to maintain.

## Heat detectors

- **Fixed temperature:** alarm when the temperature reaches a set point, typically about 57 to 90 °C, using bimetal, fusible elements or thermistors.
- **Rate of rise:** alarm when temperature rises faster than a set rate, such as about 8 to 10 °C per minute, giving earlier response to fast fires; usually combined with a fixed temperature element.
- **Linear heat detection cable:** detects heat anywhere along its length; used in cable trays, conveyors, tunnels, car parks and tank roofs.

Heat detectors are robust and rarely false alarm, so they suit kitchens, boiler rooms, garages and dusty or steamy areas, but they respond later than smoke detectors.

## Flame detectors

Flame detectors look for radiation emitted by flames:

- **UV detectors:** very fast, but can be triggered by welding arcs and lightning, and blinded by smoke or oil films.
- **IR detectors:** respond to the CO₂ emission band near 4.3 µm and the flicker frequency of flames.
- **UV/IR and multi-spectrum IR (IR3):** compare several bands to reject false sources, with long detection ranges.

They protect oil and gas facilities, refineries, fuel loading stations, turbine enclosures, aircraft hangars and warehouses with flammable liquids, where fires grow very quickly.

## Gas and multi-sensor detectors

- **Carbon monoxide fire detectors** sense CO from smouldering fires, useful in bedrooms and some hotel rooms where smoke detectors false alarm.
- **Flammable gas detectors** (catalytic, infrared, open path) detect leaks before ignition in plants handling hydrocarbons; they are often part of safety systems designed to a [safety integrity level](/safety-engineering/safety-integrity-level/).
- **Multi-sensor detectors** combine optical, heat and sometimes CO signals with algorithms, improving sensitivity to real fires and immunity to nuisance sources.

## Types of fire detectors compared

| Detector | Best for | Response | False alarm sources | Typical locations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Optical smoke | Smouldering fires | Early | Dust, steam | Offices, rooms, corridors |
| Ionisation smoke | Fast flaming fires | Early | Cooking, draughts | Declining use |
| Beam smoke | Large open spaces | Early to medium | Building movement, obstructions | Warehouses, atria |
| Aspirating | Very early warning | Very early | Requires good design | Data centres, clean rooms |
| Heat | Fires in dirty areas | Later | Very few | Kitchens, boiler rooms |
| Flame (IR3, UV/IR) | Fast flammable liquid fires | Very fast once flames visible | Sunlight, hot objects (reduced in IR3) | Process plants, hangars |

## Siting, zoning and maintenance

Codes specify maximum coverage per detector and spacing, typically around 7.5 m radius for point smoke detectors and 5.3 m for heat detectors under BS 5839 for flat ceilings, with adjustments for ceiling height, beams and airflow. Detectors connect to a fire alarm control panel in **conventional zones** or **addressable loops**, which identify each device. Regular testing, cleaning and replacement keep detectors reliable, and alarm causes are reviewed to reduce false alarms.

Detection is the first link in the fire safety chain; the next is choosing the right extinguishing agent, explained in [fire classes and extinguishers](/safety-engineering/fire-classes-and-extinguishers/). Detector selection is often informed by a [risk matrix](/safety-engineering/risk-matrix/) and plant hazard studies.
