<Key title="Short answer">

**Fire classes** group fires by the type of fuel so the right extinguishing agent can be chosen. Under the European system (EN 2): **Class A** ordinary combustible solids (wood, paper, textiles); **Class B** flammable liquids (petrol, oils, solvents); **Class C** flammable gases; **Class D** combustible metals (magnesium, sodium, lithium); and **Class F** cooking oils and fats. **Electrical fires** involving live equipment are treated separately. The US (NFPA 10) uses Class C for electrical and Class K for cooking oils. Main agents are **water**, **foam**, **dry powder**, **CO₂**, **wet chemical**, **clean agents** and special **Class D powders**.

</Key>

Using the wrong extinguisher can make a fire worse: water on burning oil or live electrical equipment can be deadly. Knowing fire classes is therefore basic safety knowledge for everyone, and essential for engineers designing fire protection for buildings, plants and vehicles.

## The fire triangle and tetrahedron

A fire needs three things: **fuel**, **heat** and **oxygen**. A fourth element, the **chemical chain reaction**, sustains flaming combustion, giving the fire tetrahedron. Extinguishing agents work by removing one or more elements:

- **Cooling** removes heat (water, wet chemical).
- **Smothering** separates fuel from oxygen (foam, CO₂, fire blankets).
- **Starving** removes fuel (shutting off a gas valve).
- **Chemical inhibition** breaks the chain reaction (dry powder, clean agents).

## The fire classes in detail

### Class A: ordinary combustible solids

Wood, paper, cardboard, textiles, many plastics and rubber. These materials burn with glowing embers deep in the fuel. **Water** is the best agent, cooling the fuel and penetrating it; **foam** and **ABC dry powder** also work. Water mist and sprinklers are common fixed systems.

### Class B: flammable liquids

Petrol, diesel, solvents, paints, alcohols, lubricating oils. Burning occurs at the liquid surface. Use **foam** (AFFF or fluorine free foams, with alcohol resistant types for polar solvents), **dry powder** or **CO₂**. Never use a water jet, which spreads the burning liquid.

### Class C: flammable gases (EU)

LPG, natural gas, hydrogen, acetylene. The first and most important action is to **isolate the gas supply**. Extinguishing a gas flame without stopping the flow can allow an explosive cloud to build up and re-ignite. **Dry powder** can knock down the flame if needed for escape; water spray cools nearby equipment.

### Class D: combustible metals

Magnesium, aluminium powder, titanium, sodium, potassium, lithium. These burn at very high temperatures and react violently with water, and sometimes with CO₂. Use **special Class D dry powders** (such as sodium chloride or graphite based) or dry sand, applied gently to smother the fire.

### Class F (EU) / Class K (US): cooking oils and fats

Deep fat fryers in commercial kitchens reach temperatures where oils auto-ignite. **Wet chemical** extinguishers spray a potassium salt solution that reacts with the hot fat (saponification), forming a soapy crust that seals the surface and cools it. Fire blankets suit small pans.

### Electrical fires

Fires involving energised equipment. **De-energise** the supply first if safe to do so. Use **CO₂** or **clean agents**, which are non-conductive and leave no residue, or dry powder. Water and foam should not be used on live equipment unless the extinguisher is tested and rated for electrical use at the relevant voltage. Electrical protection such as [circuit breakers](/electrical-engineering/circuit-breaker-working-principle/) and correct [earthing](/electrical-engineering/electrical-earthing/) reduce the risk of such fires.

## Fixed suppression systems

Beyond portable extinguishers, buildings and plants use:

- **Sprinklers:** heat activated heads that discharge water over a fire, the most effective life safety system in buildings.
- **Water mist and deluge systems:** for turbines, transformers, conveyors and process units.
- **Foam systems:** for fuel storage tanks, bund areas and aircraft hangars.
- **Gaseous systems:** CO₂ (unoccupied spaces), inert gases and chemical clean agents for data centres, control rooms and archives.
- **Kitchen hood wet chemical systems.**

These are usually triggered by [fire detectors](/safety-engineering/types-of-fire-detectors/) or by heat sensitive heads.

## Using an extinguisher safely

Remember **PASS**: **Pull** the pin, **Aim** at the base of the fire, **Squeeze** the handle, **Sweep** side to side. Only tackle small fires, keep an escape route behind you, raise the alarm first, and leave immediately if the fire grows. Inspect extinguishers regularly and service them as required by NFPA 10, BS 5306 or IS 2190.

## Fire classes in risk assessment

Identifying fire classes present in each area is part of a fire risk assessment, together with ignition sources, people at risk and existing controls, often ranked with a [risk matrix](/safety-engineering/risk-matrix/). In process plants, fire scenarios also come out of [HAZOP studies](/safety-engineering/hazop-study/), and protection is designed as one of several layers.
