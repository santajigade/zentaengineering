<Key title="Short answer">

The **Bernoulli equation in flow measurement** links the pressure drop across a restriction to the rise in fluid velocity: ΔP = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²). Combined with continuity, it gives Q = C·E·A₂·√(2ΔP/ρ). Flow therefore follows the square root of differential pressure, which is why orifice, venturi and pitot meters need square root extraction.

</Key>

Differential pressure (DP) meters are still the most widely installed flowmeters in process plants. An orifice plate, a DP transmitter and two impulse lines are cheap, well understood and backed by a full international standard. All of it rests on one result from fluid mechanics. The general [Bernoulli equation](/mechanical-engineering/bernoulli-equation/) covers derivation and assumptions; this article follows it into the instrument loop, from the pipe to the 4 to 20 mA signal.

## The Bernoulli equation in flow measurement: deriving the DP flow equation

Take a horizontal pipe of area A₁ with a restriction of area A₂. For steady, incompressible, frictionless flow, Bernoulli between the upstream tapping (1) and the throat (2) gives:

$$
p_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 = p_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\Delta P = p_1 - p_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\left(v_2^2 - v_1^2\right)
$$

The same volume passes both sections, so continuity says A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. Write the diameter ratio as β = d/D, so v₁ = β²v₂. Substituting:

$$
\Delta P = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2\left(1 - \beta^4\right)
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
v_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^4}}\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta P}{\rho}}
$$

Multiplying by the throat area gives the ideal volume flow. A real meter loses some energy to friction, and behind an orifice the jet keeps contracting to a vena contracta smaller than the bore. A **discharge coefficient** C, found by calibration, corrects for both:

$$
Q = C \, E \, A_2 \sqrt{\frac{2\Delta P}{\rho}}, \qquad E = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^4}}
$$

E is the velocity of approach factor. For gases, ISO 5167 adds an expansibility factor ε and writes the equation as mass flow:

$$
q_m = \frac{C}{\sqrt{1-\beta^4}}\,\varepsilon\,\frac{\pi}{4}d^2\sqrt{2\,\Delta P\,\rho_1}
$$

Everything in front of the square root is fixed by the meter and the fluid. That leaves the part an instrument engineer cares about:

$$
Q = K\sqrt{\Delta P}
$$

## Why flow follows the square root of DP

The kinetic energy term ½ρv² is squared in velocity, so pressure drop grows as the square of flow. Double the flow and the DP rises four times. Halve it and the DP falls to a quarter.

| Flow, % of max | DP, % of span | 4 to 20 mA from a linear DP transmitter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 100 | 20.0 mA |
| 70.7 | 50 | 12.0 mA |
| 50 | 25 | 8.0 mA |
| 30 | 9 | 5.44 mA |
| 10 | 1 | 4.16 mA |

This square law is the reason DP meters have limited [turndown ratio](/instrumentation-control/turndown-ratio/). At 10% flow the transmitter sees just 1% of its span.

## Square root extraction

A DP transmitter measures pressure, but operators and controllers need flow. Somewhere in the loop the signal must be square rooted:

- **In the transmitter.** Most smart DP transmitters have a square root output mode, so the 4 to 20 mA signal is already linear in flow.
- **In the DCS or PLC.** The transmitter sends linear DP and the control system applies the square root in its input block.
- **In older systems,** a separate square root extractor module or a gauge with a square root scale did the job.

Only do it in one place. Applying the square root twice, once in the transmitter and again in the DCS, is a common commissioning mistake that makes the flow reading too high at low flow.

Near zero DP the square root curve is very steep, so a tiny offset or noise produces a large flow reading. A **low flow cutoff**, typically a few percent of flow, forces the output to zero below that point.

For the plain relation between pipe area, velocity and flow rate, see the [flow rate formula](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/flow-rate-formula/) on instrumentationblog.in.

## The same equation in other DP meters

- **Venturi tube.** A smooth converging cone, throat and long diverging cone. C is about 0.98 to 0.995 and most of the DP is recovered downstream.
- **Flow nozzle.** A rounded inlet without the long diffuser. C is high, near 0.99, but recovery is poorer than a venturi.
- **Pitot tube and averaging pitot.** The impact port brings the flow to rest, so it reads stagnation pressure. The difference from static pressure is ½ρv², giving v = √(2ΔP/ρ) at that point, or across the pipe for an averaging probe.
- **Elbow meter.** Centrifugal force in a bend makes the outer wall pressure higher than the inner. The DP still follows a square law, though the coefficient usually needs field calibration.

## Permanent pressure loss

Not all the DP is lost. Downstream of the restriction the fluid slows and pressure partly recovers. For an orifice plate, ISO 5167-2 gives an approximation for the permanent loss:

$$
\Delta\varpi \approx \left(1 - \beta^{1.9}\right)\Delta P
$$

At β = 0.6 that is about 62% of the measured DP. A venturi loses typically only 5 to 20%, which matters for pumping cost on large lines.

## Worked example: water through an orifice plate

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>Water at 20°C (ρ = 998 kg/m³) flows in a 100 mm bore pipe through a concentric orifice of d = 60 mm. The DP transmitter reads 250 mbar (25,000 Pa). Take C = 0.61.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>Volume flow, mass flow and approximate permanent pressure loss.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Fully developed turbulent flow, incompressible liquid (ε = 1), C constant at this Reynolds number.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>β = 60 ÷ 100 = 0.6, β⁴ = 0.1296<br />E = 1 ÷ √(1 − 0.1296) = 1.0719<br />A₂ = π/4 × 0.06² = 0.002827 m²<br />√(2ΔP/ρ) = √(2 × 25,000 ÷ 998) = 7.078 m/s<br />Q = 0.61 × 1.0719 × 0.002827 × 7.078 = 0.01309 m³/s = 47.1 m³/h<br />q_m = 0.01309 × 998 = 13.06 kg/s<br />Permanent loss ≈ (1 − 0.6^1.9) × 25,000 ≈ 15,500 Pa = 155 mbar</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>About 47.1 m³/h (13.1 kg/s) of water, with roughly 155 mbar of the 250 mbar DP lost permanently</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>C was assumed. For custody or design work, calculate C from the ISO 5167 Reader-Harris/Gallagher equation and iterate with the Reynolds number; check straight pipe lengths too.</dd>
</dl>
</Example>

The pipe velocity here is 1.67 m/s, a [Reynolds number](/mechanical-engineering/reynolds-number/) of about 166,000, comfortably turbulent.

## Key formulas for Bernoulli in flow measurement

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bernoulli across a restriction | ΔP = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²) | ΔP in Pa, ρ in kg/m³, v in m/s |
| Continuity | A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ | A in m² |
| Beta ratio | β = d ÷ D | d = bore, D = pipe internal diameter |
| Velocity of approach factor | E = 1 ÷ √(1 − β⁴) | Dimensionless |
| Volume flow | Q = C·E·A₂·√(2ΔP/ρ) | C = discharge coefficient, Q in m³/s |
| Mass flow (ISO 5167) | q_m = C·E·ε·(π/4)d²·√(2ΔPρ₁) | ε = expansibility factor, q_m in kg/s |
| Square root law | Q = K√ΔP, so Q/Q_max = √(ΔP/ΔP_max) | K = meter constant |
| Pitot tube velocity | v = √(2ΔP/ρ) | ΔP = stagnation minus static pressure |
| Orifice permanent loss | Δϖ ≈ (1 − β^1.9)ΔP | Approximation from ISO 5167-2 |

## Bernoulli in flow measurement interview and viva questions

1. **Derive why flow is proportional to √ΔP.** Bernoulli gives ΔP = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²); continuity relates v₁ to v₂; solving gives v₂ ∝ √ΔP, and Q = A₂v₂.
2. **What does a 12 mA signal mean on a DP flow loop?** If the transmitter output is linear DP, it means 50% DP or 70.7% flow. If square root extraction is on, it means 50% flow.
3. **Why is the orifice discharge coefficient about 0.6?** The jet keeps contracting after the bore to a vena contracta, so the effective flow area is smaller than the bore area, and friction adds a small loss.
4. **What is the beta ratio and its usual range?** The bore diameter divided by pipe diameter. ISO 5167 orifice plates are generally used between about 0.1 and 0.75.
5. **Why is a low flow cutoff needed?** The square root curve is steep near zero, so small DP offsets or noise appear as a large flow; the cutoff forces the reading to zero.
6. **Why does a venturi have lower permanent pressure loss than an orifice?** Its long diverging cone slows the fluid gradually and recovers most of the pressure, while the orifice jet breaks up into eddies that waste energy.
7. **What happens if square root extraction is applied in both the transmitter and the DCS?** The flow reading becomes the fourth root of DP, reading too high at all flows below maximum.
