<Key title="Short answer">

**Turndown ratio** is the maximum value an instrument can measure accurately divided by the minimum value it can measure with the same accuracy: TR = Q_max / Q_min. A flowmeter that reads 100 m³/h down to 10 m³/h within its accuracy limit has a 10:1 turndown. Higher turndown means a wider usable range.

</Key>

Every instrument has a range where it works well. Push it too low and the signal sinks into noise, friction or offset error. Turndown ratio puts a number on that usable range, and it is one of the first figures an instrument engineer checks on a flowmeter datasheet. A meter with too little turndown will read well at design flow and badly during start-up, night shifts or reduced throughput.

## Turndown ratio formula

$$
TR = \frac{Q_{max}}{Q_{min}}
$$

where Q_max is the highest flow (or pressure, level, heat output) the device handles within its stated accuracy, and Q_min is the lowest. The result is written as a ratio such as 10:1.

The same idea works in the other direction for an application. If a gas line runs between 100,000 and 1,000,000 m³ per day, the process needs a turndown of 10:1, so the meter must offer at least that. If the chosen meter tops out at 2,000,000 m³ per day, it must reach 100,000 m³ per day accurately, which means 20:1 from that meter.

Turndown is also quoted for things that are not measuring devices. A burner with 4:1 turndown can fire down to a quarter of its maximum heat output and still burn cleanly. A control valve's rangeability is the ratio of the largest to the smallest flow it can control.

## Turndown and rangedown on a transmitter

On a pressure or DP transmitter the word means something slightly different. Here it is the **rangedown**: the upper range limit (URL) of the sensor divided by the smallest span you are allowed to calibrate it to.

$$
TD = \frac{URL}{\text{minimum span}}
$$

A DP transmitter with a 2500 mbar URL and a 25 mbar minimum span has a 100:1 turndown. That lets one model cover many duties, which suits spares stock. The catch is accuracy. Datasheets give accuracy as a percentage of calibrated span, often with an extra term that grows once the span drops below a stated fraction of URL. Set the same unit to a span of 25 mbar and its error, as a share of that span, can be several times larger than at full URL. So high transmitter turndown buys flexibility, not free precision.

## Why DP flowmeters have low turndown

Orifice plates, venturis, nozzles and pitot tubes all work on the [Bernoulli equation](/instrumentation-control/bernoulli-equation-flow-measurement/), and their differential pressure rises with the **square** of flow:

$$
\Delta P \propto Q^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad Q \propto \sqrt{\Delta P}
$$

At 50% flow the DP is 25% of full scale. At 10% flow it is only 1%. The transmitter's error is a fixed slice of its DP span, so at low flow that slice becomes a large share of a tiny signal. Square root extraction halves the relative error when it converts DP to flow, but it cannot rescue a signal that has almost vanished.

That is why a single orifice meter is usually good for about 3:1 to 5:1. Plants that need more either fit two DP transmitters with different spans on the same orifice (a "stacked" arrangement that switches between them) or choose a different meter.

## Typical turndown ratios

Figures below are typical published values. Makers quote their own, and the ratio always belongs with the accuracy it was stated at.

| Device | Typical turndown | What limits it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Orifice plate with one DP transmitter | 3:1 to 5:1 | Square law, DP falls to a tiny signal |
| Venturi or flow nozzle | 4:1 to 10:1 | Square law, same as orifice |
| Turbine meter | 10:1 | Bearing friction and drag at low flow |
| Vortex meter | 10:1 to 20:1 | Low Reynolds number stops vortex shedding |
| Rotary positive displacement meter | 10:1 to 80:1 | Leakage past rotors at low flow |
| Multipath ultrasonic meter | about 50:1 | Timing resolution at low velocity |
| Magnetic flowmeter | 30:1 to 100:1 or more | Signal noise at very low velocity |
| Coriolis mass flowmeter | 100:1 or more | Zero stability |
| Thermal mass flowmeter | 100:1 and above | Sensor noise and heat loss effects |

For an introduction to how each of these meters works, see the flow measurement section at [instrumentationblog.in](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/flow-measurement/).

## Worked example: how low can an orifice meter read?

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>An orifice meter is calibrated for 0 to 250 mbar DP at maximum flow. The DP transmitter accuracy is ±0.1% of span. The user wants the flow reading to stay within ±1% of reading.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>The DP error at 10% flow, and the turndown the meter can deliver.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Only transmitter error is considered; the orifice coefficient is taken as constant. Since Q ∝ √ΔP, the relative flow error is half the relative DP error.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>Transmitter error = 0.001 × 250 = 0.25 mbar<br />At 10% flow: ΔP = 0.1² × 250 = 2.5 mbar, so DP error = 0.25 ÷ 2.5 = 10% and flow error ≈ 5%<br />For a 1% flow error the DP error must be 2% or less: ΔP_min = 0.25 ÷ 0.02 = 12.5 mbar<br />Q_min / Q_max = √(12.5 ÷ 250) = 0.224, so TR = 1 ÷ 0.224 ≈ 4.5</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>At 10% flow the reading may be off by about 5%; for ±1% of reading the meter manages only about 4.5:1 turndown</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>The discharge coefficient also drifts at low Reynolds number, and installation effects add error, so real turndown is often a little lower.</dd>
</dl>
</Example>

## Key formulas for turndown ratio

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Turndown ratio | TR = Q_max ÷ Q_min | Q in any consistent flow unit, TR written as TR:1 |
| Transmitter turndown (rangedown) | TD = URL ÷ span_min | URL and span in the same pressure unit |
| DP flow relation | Q = K √ΔP | K = meter constant, ΔP in Pa or mbar |
| Relative flow error from DP error | δQ/Q ≈ ½ × δΔP/ΔP | Fractions or percent |
| Minimum flow for a target error | Q_min/Q_max = √(e_tx ÷ (2 × e_Q)) | e_tx = transmitter error as a fraction of span, e_Q = allowed flow error as a fraction of reading |

## How to choose a meter with the right turndown

1. Find the real minimum and maximum flows, including start-up, turndown operation and future capacity.
2. Divide them to get the required turndown, then allow some margin.
3. Compare with the meter's turndown **at the accuracy you need**, not its widest headline figure.
4. Check that the maximum flow sits comfortably inside the meter range and not right at its top, since oversizing a meter eats turndown at the bottom.
5. For DP meters needing more than about 5:1, consider stacked transmitters or another technology.

A low flow cutoff in the transmitter or control system is usually set near the bottom of the usable range, so the reading drops to zero instead of showing noise when the line is shut.

## Turndown ratio interview and viva questions

1. **Define turndown ratio.** It is the maximum measurable value divided by the minimum value measured within the stated accuracy, written as a ratio such as 10:1.
2. **Why does an orifice plate have low turndown?** DP varies with the square of flow, so at 10% flow the DP is only 1% of span and the transmitter's fixed error becomes a large share of the signal.
3. **What is the turndown of a DP transmitter?** It is the upper range limit divided by the minimum calibratable span, for example 2500 mbar ÷ 25 mbar = 100:1.
4. **Does a 100:1 transmitter turndown give 100:1 flow turndown on an orifice?** No. Flow turndown is limited by the square law and by accuracy at low DP, which keeps an orifice meter near 3:1 to 5:1.
5. **How can you extend the range of an orifice meter?** Fit two DP transmitters with different spans on the same orifice and switch between them, or use a meter with higher turndown such as a Coriolis or magnetic flowmeter.
6. **What is the turndown required if flow varies from 20 to 160 m³/h?** 160 ÷ 20 = 8, so 8:1.
