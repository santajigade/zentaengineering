<Key title="Short answer">

**Artificial lift methods** add energy to a well when reservoir pressure alone cannot lift fluid to the surface at the required rate. The main methods are: **sucker rod pumping** (a surface pump jack drives a downhole plunger pump), **electrical submersible pumps** (ESP, a multistage centrifugal pump driven by a downhole motor), **gas lift** (gas injected into the tubing lightens the fluid column), **progressive cavity pumps** (PCP, a helical rotor for viscous or sandy oil) and **hydraulic pumps** (jet or piston pumps driven by power fluid). Choice depends on rate, depth, gas, sand, viscosity, location and cost.

</Key>

Most oil wells eventually need help to produce. Early in a field's life, reservoir pressure may push oil all the way to the surface (natural flow). As pressure falls and more water is produced, the fluid column in the tubing becomes too heavy. Artificial lift reduces the bottomhole flowing pressure, which increases inflow from the reservoir and keeps wells economic. Most producing oil wells worldwide rely on some form of artificial lift.

## The principle

Flow from the reservoir into the well depends on the difference between reservoir pressure and **bottomhole flowing pressure** (P_wf). The **productivity index** J relates them:

$$
q = J\,(P_r - P_{wf})
$$

The fluid column in the tubing exerts a pressure of roughly ρgh plus friction. Artificial lift either **pumps** fluid up (rod pumps, ESP, PCP, hydraulic pumps) or **lightens** the column (gas lift), lowering P_wf and raising q.

## Sucker rod pumping

A surface **pumping unit** (pump jack or beam pump) converts the rotation of an electric motor or engine into reciprocating motion. A string of steel **sucker rods** carries this motion to a **downhole plunger pump** with a standing valve and a travelling valve. On the upstroke, the travelling valve closes and lifts fluid while the standing valve opens to let reservoir fluid in; on the downstroke the roles reverse.

- **Strengths:** simple, well understood, easy to service, works at low rates and low bottomhole pressure, handles high temperature.
- **Limits:** depth and rate limited by rod strength (typically up to about 3000 m and a few hundred m³/day); sensitive to gas and sand; poor in deviated wells due to rod wear.
- **Monitoring:** dynamometer cards plot rod load against position and diagnose pump problems; pump off controllers stop the unit when the well pumps dry.

## Electrical submersible pumps (ESP)

An ESP is a **multistage centrifugal pump** directly coupled to a downhole electric motor, with a seal section protecting the motor and a gas separator at the intake. Power comes from the surface through an armoured cable, often from a **variable speed drive**. The working principle of each stage is the same as a [centrifugal pump](/mechanical-engineering/centrifugal-pump-working-principle/).

- **Strengths:** very high rates (from about 30 to over 4000 m³/day), works in deviated wells, efficient.
- **Limits:** sensitive to free gas, sand and high temperature; failures need a workover rig; high power demand.

## Gas lift

High pressure gas is injected down the **annulus** and enters the tubing through **gas lift valves** set at chosen depths. The gas mixes with the produced fluid, reducing its density and therefore the hydrostatic pressure of the column.

- **Continuous gas lift** for wells with good inflow; **intermittent gas lift** for low rate wells.
- **Strengths:** few downhole moving parts, handles sand and gas well, suits deviated and offshore wells, valves can be replaced by wireline.
- **Limits:** needs a supply of high pressure gas and compressors; less effective at very low reservoir pressure.

## Progressive cavity pumps (PCP)

A helical steel **rotor** turns inside an elastomer **stator**, forming sealed cavities that move fluid steadily upward. PCPs handle heavy, viscous oil and sand well and are energy efficient, but the elastomer limits temperature and some chemical exposure.

## Hydraulic pumps

A **power fluid**, usually treated oil or water, is pumped down at high pressure to drive a downhole **jet pump** (a venturi with no moving parts) or a **piston pump**. Jet pumps tolerate sand and gas and can be retrieved by circulation without a rig, at the cost of lower efficiency.

## Artificial lift methods compared

| Method | Typical rate | Depth | Gas handling | Sand handling | Deviated wells |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rod pump | Low | Shallow to medium | Fair | Fair | Poor |
| ESP | Medium to very high | Medium to deep | Fair (with separator) | Fair | Good |
| Gas lift | Medium to high | Deep | Excellent | Good | Excellent |
| PCP | Low to medium | Shallow to medium | Fair | Excellent | Fair |
| Jet pump | Low to medium | Deep | Good | Good | Good |

## Optimisation and surveillance

Once installed, lift systems are tuned continuously. Variable speed drives match ESP and PCP speed to inflow, gas lift injection rates are allocated between wells to maximise total oil for the gas available, and rod pump controllers adjust stroke rate to avoid pounding. Downhole gauges, surface data and well tests together reveal declining performance early, so a worn pump can be replaced before it fails.

## Selecting a method

Engineers compare well data (rate, depth, temperature, gas oil ratio, sand, viscosity, deviation), field infrastructure (power, gas supply, access for workover rigs) and economics (capital cost, energy use, expected run life). Many fields use several methods, choosing per well and changing as conditions evolve. Monitoring with downhole [pressure sensors](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/pressure-measurement/), surface flow meters and smart controllers optimises production and extends equipment life.

Artificial lift is the link between the reservoir's [porosity and permeability](/petroleum-engineering/porosity-and-permeability/) and the surface facilities, and it often decides how much of the oil in place is finally recovered.
