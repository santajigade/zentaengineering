<Key title="Short answer">

**Crude oil distillation** is the first and largest process in every refinery. Crude is **desalted**, preheated, then heated in a **furnace** to about **350 to 380 °C** and fed to an **atmospheric distillation column**, where vapours rise and condense on trays at different heights according to boiling point. Products are drawn off from top to bottom: **gases and naphtha**, **kerosene**, **diesel (gas oil)** and **atmospheric residue**. The residue is heated again and sent to a **vacuum column**, which separates **vacuum gas oils** from **vacuum residue** at lower temperatures to avoid cracking.

</Key>

Crude oil is a mixture of thousands of hydrocarbons, from methane to molecules with more than 70 carbon atoms. It is not useful as it comes from the ground. Distillation separates it into fractions by boiling range, which become fuels directly or feed downstream conversion units. Because all crude passes through it, the crude distillation unit (CDU) sets the refinery's capacity.

## Crude oil quality and API gravity

Crudes are described by density, usually expressed as **API gravity**:

$$
\text{°API} = \frac{141.5}{SG} - 131.5
$$

where SG is specific gravity at 60 °F. Light crudes (above about 31 °API) yield more naphtha and diesel; heavy crudes (below about 22 °API) yield more residue. Sulphur content divides crudes into sweet (below about 0.5%) and sour.

## Step 1: desalting and preheating

Crude contains salt water, sediment and salts that would corrode equipment and foul exchangers. It is mixed with wash water and passed through an **electrostatic desalter**, where an electric field coalesces water droplets that settle out. The crude is then preheated to about 250 to 280 °C in a train of [heat exchangers](/chemical-engineering/heat-exchanger-types/) against hot product streams, recovering energy.

## Step 2: furnace

A fired heater raises the crude to about 350 to 380 °C, partly vaporising it. Temperature is limited because hydrocarbons start to crack thermally above this range, forming coke and gas.

## Step 3: atmospheric distillation column

The hot mixture enters the **flash zone** near the bottom of a tall column (often 50 m high) operating slightly above atmospheric pressure. Vapours rise through 30 to 50 **trays**, meeting liquid flowing down. On each tray, heavier components condense and lighter ones vaporise, so temperature falls from bottom to top. This is the same principle explained in [distillation column working principle](/chemical-engineering/distillation-column-working-principle/).

- **Overhead:** vapours are condensed; non-condensable gases (methane to butane) go to gas processing, and **naphtha** is partly returned as reflux.
- **Side draws:** **kerosene** and **light and heavy gas oil (diesel)** are withdrawn from intermediate trays. Each passes through a small **side stripper** where steam removes light components, improving the flash point.
- **Pumparounds:** liquid is drawn off, cooled and returned lower in the column to remove heat and balance vapour loads.
- **Bottom:** steam is injected to strip light ends from the **atmospheric residue**.

## Step 4: vacuum distillation

The atmospheric residue is reheated to about 380 to 420 °C and fed to a **vacuum column** operating at an absolute pressure of roughly 25 to 100 mbar at the top, produced by steam ejectors or vacuum pumps. Lower pressure lowers boiling points, so heavy molecules can be vaporised without cracking. Vacuum columns are very wide and use structured **packing** instead of trays to minimise pressure drop.

Products:

- **Light and heavy vacuum gas oil (VGO):** feed for fluid catalytic cracking or hydrocracking, or for lube oil base stocks.
- **Vacuum residue:** feed for cokers, visbreakers, deasphalting or bitumen production.

## Typical fractions and uses

| Fraction | Approximate boiling range | Main uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Refinery gas, LPG | Below 30 °C | Fuel gas, LPG, petrochemicals |
| Light naphtha | 30 to 90 °C | Gasoline blending, isomerisation |
| Heavy naphtha | 90 to 180 °C | Catalytic reforming to gasoline |
| Kerosene | 150 to 250 °C | Jet fuel, kerosene |
| Diesel (gas oil) | 230 to 350 °C | Diesel, heating oil |
| Vacuum gas oil | 350 to 550 °C | FCC and hydrocracker feed, lubes |
| Vacuum residue | Above 550 °C | Coker feed, bitumen, fuel oil |

## Energy use in crude oil distillation

The crude unit is one of the largest energy users in a refinery, mainly through the furnace. Good heat integration in the preheat train, designed with methods such as [pinch analysis](/chemical-engineering/pinch-analysis/), can recover most of the heat in the products and cut fuel use substantially. Fouling of preheat exchangers by asphaltenes and salts gradually lowers the furnace inlet temperature, so exchangers are cleaned on a planned schedule and antifoulant chemicals are injected. Some units add a preflash drum or column before the furnace to remove light ends early and free furnace capacity.

## Control and operation

Operators control furnace outlet temperature, column top pressure and temperature, reflux rate, pumparound duties and side draw rates to meet product specifications such as flash point, distillation end point and colour. Key instruments include thermocouples along the column, [flow meters](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/flow-measurement/) on every stream, differential pressure across trays and online analysers. Corrosion from salts, naphthenic acids and sulphur is managed through desalting, chemical injection and material selection.

## After crude oil distillation

Distillation only separates; conversion units then upgrade the fractions: reformers raise naphtha octane, hydrotreaters remove sulphur, catalytic crackers and hydrocrackers turn gas oils into gasoline and diesel, and cokers upgrade residue. The light ends removed in distillation also feed gas plants related to the [LNG process](/petroleum-engineering/lng-process/), and the refined products leave by [pipeline](/petroleum-engineering/pipeline-pigging/), ship, rail or truck.
