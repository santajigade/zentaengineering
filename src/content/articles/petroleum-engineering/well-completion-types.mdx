<Key title="Short answer">

**Well completion** is the set of equipment and operations that turn a drilled and cased hole into a safe producing or injection well. It defines how the reservoir connects to the wellbore, by leaving the hole open or by cementing casing and perforating it, and adds sand control, tubing, packers and safety valves as the reservoir requires.

</Key>

A well completion is the bridge between drilling and production. Once the hole reaches the reservoir, engineers must decide how fluids will enter the well, how the hole will be kept stable, how separate zones will be isolated and how oil or gas will travel to surface. Those choices affect productivity, safety and the cost of every later intervention, so completion design is one of the most important decisions in a well's life.

## What a well completion has to do

Every completion design balances the same set of jobs, described in detail by Bellarby and in the SPE handbook:

- **Connect** the reservoir to the wellbore with as little damage and pressure loss as possible.
- **Keep the hole stable** and stop formation sand from entering the well.
- **Isolate zones** so that water, gas or a depleted layer does not interfere with the productive interval.
- **Convey fluids** to surface through tubing sized for the expected flow rate.
- **Provide safety barriers**, such as a packer and a subsurface safety valve, so the well can be shut in.
- **Allow future work**: logging, stimulation, artificial lift and recompletion.

Completions are usually grouped by the lower completion (the part across the reservoir) and the upper completion (tubing, packer and valves above it).

### Open hole completion

In an **open hole completion**, production casing is set and cemented just above the reservoir, and the reservoir section is drilled and left without casing. Fluid enters along the whole exposed rock face, so there is no perforation pressure drop and the full formation thickness contributes. It is cheap and simple, and it suits strong, consolidated rock such as many carbonates, and long horizontal wells.

The weaknesses are serious. There is no cement to isolate water or gas layers inside the open section, selective stimulation is difficult, and weak rock can collapse or produce sand. For this reason open hole sections are often fitted with screens or a slotted liner.

### Cased and perforated completion

Here casing is run through the reservoir and cemented in place. A **perforating gun** with shaped explosive charges is then fired to create tunnels through the casing, the cement and a short distance into the rock. Shot density is usually stated in shots per foot (spf), with typical designs of 4 to 12 spf, and phasing (the angle between charges, such as 60 or 90 degrees) spreads the holes around the casing.

This is the most common well completion. Cement isolates zones, perforations can be placed only where logs show good pay, and the casing supports the hole for its whole life. The price is extra cost and a pressure drop through the perforations and the crushed zone around each tunnel. Log interpretation from [well logging methods](/petroleum-engineering/well-logging-methods/) is what tells the engineer where to shoot.

## Liner completions

A **liner** is a casing string that hangs from a liner hanger inside the previous casing instead of running all the way to surface. It saves steel and allows a larger casing size higher up.

- **Cemented liner:** cemented and perforated like full casing, common in deeper wells.
- **Slotted or pre-drilled liner:** an uncemented liner with slots or holes, used in open hole to stop large scale hole collapse. It gives little control of fine sand.
- **Screened liner:** wire wrapped or premium mesh screens that hold back formation sand.

## Sand control and gravel pack completions

Weak, poorly consolidated sandstones can produce sand that erodes chokes and pipework and fills the well. Sand control options include:

- **Standalone screens**, sized to the formation grain distribution.
- **Gravel pack:** a screen is placed across the interval and the annulus between screen and casing (cased hole gravel pack) or screen and rock (open hole gravel pack) is filled with carefully sized gravel. A classic sizing rule chooses a median gravel size about 5 to 6 times the median formation grain size, so the gravel holds back the formation sand while the screen holds back the gravel.
- **Frac pack:** a short, wide hydraulic fracture is created and packed with proppant, then the screen annulus is packed, which both bypasses damage and controls sand.

The rock properties in [porosity and permeability](/petroleum-engineering/porosity-and-permeability/) decide whether sand control is needed and how much productivity it may cost.

## Single zone and multiple zone completions

A **single zone completion** produces one reservoir through one tubing string, with a packer sealing the annulus above the perforations. It is the simplest and most reliable arrangement.

When a well penetrates several reservoirs, a **multiple zone completion** can produce them separately or together:

- **Dual completion:** two tubing strings and two packers, each zone producing up its own string so pressures and fluids stay separate.
- **Selective single string:** one tubing string with sliding sleeves or nipples so zones can be opened in turn.
- **Commingled completion:** zones flow together into one string, simpler but harder to allocate and manage when pressures differ.

The choice depends on regulations for commingling, the [drive mechanism](/petroleum-engineering/reservoir-drive-mechanisms/) and pressure of each zone, and the cost of the extra hardware.

## Conventional vs tubingless completions

A **conventional completion** runs production tubing inside the casing, normally with a packer. The tubing can be pulled and replaced, the annulus is protected from well fluids, and the packer and safety valve form barriers. It also gives a place for [artificial lift methods](/petroleum-engineering/artificial-lift-methods/) such as gas lift valves or pumps.

A **tubingless completion** produces through small diameter casing with no separate tubing, sometimes with several small casing strings cemented in one hole. It costs less and is used for low rate gas wells, but there is no second barrier, corrosion acts directly on the casing and repairs are limited. API Spec 5CT covers the steel grades and dimensions of both casing and tubing.

## Key formulas for well completion

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrostatic pressure (SI) | P = ρ g h | ρ in kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s², h = true vertical depth in m, P in Pa |
| Hydrostatic pressure (field) | P = 0.052 × MW × TVD | MW in ppg, TVD in ft, P in psi |
| Overbalance | ΔP = P_brine − P_res | pressures in MPa or psi |
| Perforation count | N = SPF × L | SPF in shots per foot, L in ft |

## Worked example: completion brine pressure and shot count

Before perforating, the well is filled with completion brine heavy enough to hold back the reservoir. The hydrostatic pressure of a fluid column is:

$$
P = \rho \, g \, h
$$

In oilfield units this becomes $P = 0.052 \times MW \times TVD$. The derivation of the hydrostatic relation is covered in this [hydrostatic pressure formula](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/hydrostatic-pressure-formula/) guide.

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>Top of perforations at 2500 m TVD. Reservoir pressure 27.0 MPa. Calcium chloride brine density 1200 kg/m³ (about 10.0 ppg). Perforated interval 10 m long, shot density 6 spf.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>Brine hydrostatic pressure at the perforations, the overbalance, and the number of perforations.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Static, solids free brine of uniform density; g = 9.81 m/s²; 1 m = 3.281 ft.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>P = 1200 × 9.81 × 2500 = 29 430 000 Pa = 29.43 MPa<br />Overbalance = 29.43 − 27.0 = 2.43 MPa<br />Field check: TVD = 2500 × 3.281 = 8202 ft, P = 0.052 × 10.0 × 8202 = 4265 psi, about 29.4 MPa<br />Interval = 10 × 3.281 = 32.8 ft, N = 6 × 32.8 = 197 shots</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>Brine pressure about 29.4 MPa, overbalance about 2.4 MPa, and about 197 perforations</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Brine density falls with temperature and rises with pressure, so a deep hot well needs a downhole density correction. Many wells are perforated underbalanced instead to clean the tunnels, which needs a different pressure design.</dd>
</dl>
</Example>

## Choosing a well completion type

Selection starts from reservoir data and works up:

1. **Rock strength:** strong rock allows open hole; weak sand needs screens, gravel or frac packs.
2. **Number of zones and fluid contacts:** multiple layers or nearby water and gas favour cemented casing and selective perforating.
3. **Expected rates and lift:** tubing size, and whether pumps or gas lift will be needed later.
4. **Stimulation plans:** cased and cemented wells allow staged fracturing and acidizing.
5. **Life cycle cost and access:** offshore and subsea wells justify more robust, low intervention designs.

The drilling program, including the [drilling fluid](/petroleum-engineering/drilling-fluid-types/) used across the pay zone, also matters, because mud filtrate and solids damage the near wellbore rock that the completion must produce through.

## Well completion interview and viva questions

1. **What is the main advantage of an open hole completion?** The whole reservoir face is exposed, so there is no perforation pressure drop and cost is low, but there is no zonal isolation in the open section.
2. **Why is a cased and perforated completion the most common?** Cement and casing give zonal isolation and hole support, and perforations can be placed selectively where logs show productive rock.
3. **What does a gravel pack do?** Sized gravel placed around a screen holds back formation sand while letting fluids flow, and the screen holds back the gravel.
4. **What is the purpose of a production packer?** It seals the annulus between tubing and casing, forcing flow up the tubing and protecting the casing from well fluids and pressure.
5. **How do you calculate the hydrostatic pressure of completion brine?** Use P = ρgh in SI units, or P = 0.052 × MW × TVD with mud weight in ppg and depth in feet to get psi.
