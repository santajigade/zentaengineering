<Key title="Short answer">

**Offshore platform types** fall into two groups. **Bottom founded (fixed)** structures stand on the seabed: **steel jackets** (up to about 400 m water depth), **gravity based structures** of concrete, **compliant towers**, and **jack-ups**. **Floating** systems are moored or tethered: **tension leg platforms (TLPs)**, **spars**, **semi-submersibles** and **FPSOs** (floating production, storage and offloading vessels), used in deep water from a few hundred to over 3000 m. Water depth, field size, environment and export route decide the choice.

</Key>

Around a quarter of the world's oil and gas comes from offshore fields, from shallow coastal waters to ultra deep water far from land. An offshore platform must support drilling, processing, living quarters and export equipment while resisting waves, wind, currents and sometimes ice for 20 to 40 years.

## What a production platform does

A production platform's **topsides** typically include:

- Wellheads and manifolds, or connections to subsea wells through flexible **risers**.
- Separation of oil, gas and water in [pressure vessels](/chemical-engineering/pressure-vessels-basics/).
- Gas compression, dehydration and sometimes reinjection.
- Water treatment and injection for pressure support.
- Power generation, utilities, safety systems and accommodation.
- Export by pipeline or, for floaters, to shuttle tankers.

The **substructure** or **hull** carries the topsides and transfers loads to the seabed or to moorings.

## Bottom founded platforms

### Steel jacket platforms

A **jacket** is a welded tubular steel space frame fixed to the seabed by **piles** driven through its legs. It is the most common offshore structure, used from a few metres to about 400 m water depth. Jackets are fabricated onshore, transported on barges and lifted or launched into place. Topsides are installed in one lift or several modules. Tubular members follow principles similar to land based [steel sections](/civil-engineering/steel-sections-types/), designed for wave fatigue and corrosion.

### Gravity based structures (GBS)

Massive concrete bases sit on the seabed and are held in place by their own weight, needing no piles. Their hollow cells can store crude oil. GBS platforms suit harsh environments such as the North Sea and iceberg prone waters off Canada, in depths up to about 300 m.

### Compliant towers

Slender towers that flex with wave forces instead of resisting them rigidly, used in about 300 to 900 m of water. Few have been built, as floaters have become more economical at these depths.

### Jack-up platforms

A floating hull with three or four legs that are lowered to the seabed, then the hull is jacked up above the waves. Jack-ups are mostly mobile drilling rigs in up to about 120 to 150 m of water, but some serve as production or accommodation units.

## Floating platforms

### Tension leg platforms (TLP)

A buoyant hull is held down by vertical steel **tendons** anchored to the seabed and kept in high tension by excess buoyancy. Heave (vertical motion) is almost eliminated, allowing **dry trees**, wellheads on deck, which simplifies well access. Used in about 300 to 1500 m.

### Spar platforms

A very deep draft vertical cylinder, often 150 to 200 m long, ballasted at the bottom and held by catenary or taut **mooring lines**. The deep draft gives small motions, also allowing dry trees in water depths up to about 3000 m.

### Semi-submersibles

A deck supported by columns on submerged pontoons. The small waterplane area reduces wave motion. Semi-submersibles serve as drilling rigs and production platforms, usually with subsea wells, in depths from about 100 m to over 3000 m.

### FPSO

A ship shaped vessel, new built or converted from a tanker, with process plant on deck and crude storage in its hull. It is moored by a **turret** that lets it weathervane with the wind and waves, or spread moored in calm regions. **Shuttle tankers** offload the oil. FPSOs dominate deep water developments offshore Brazil and West Africa and are widely used elsewhere. Gas fields can use FLNG vessels that also liquefy gas, using the [LNG process](/petroleum-engineering/lng-process/).

## Offshore platform types compared

| Type | Typical water depth | Support | Oil storage | Wells |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Steel jacket | Up to about 400 m | Piles | No | Dry trees |
| Gravity based | Up to about 300 m | Own weight | Often | Dry trees |
| Jack-up | Up to about 150 m | Legs on seabed | No | Dry trees |
| TLP | About 300 to 1500 m | Vertical tendons | No | Dry or wet |
| Spar | About 500 to 3000 m | Moorings | Limited | Dry or wet |
| Semi-submersible | About 100 to 3000 m | Moorings | No | Wet (subsea) |
| FPSO | About 20 to 3000 m | Turret or spread mooring | Yes | Wet (subsea) |

## Buoyancy in floating platforms

A floater's hull displaces water equal to the total weight it supports (Archimedes' principle):

$$
\rho_w\,g\,V_\text{displaced} = W
$$

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>A TLP has a total weight (hull, topsides and risers) of 40 000 tonnes. Its submerged hull displaces 52 000 m³ of seawater with density 1025 kg/m³.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>The total tendon tension.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Still water; tendons carry all excess buoyancy.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>Buoyancy = 52 000 × 1025 = 53.3 million kg = 53 300 tonnes force<br />Excess buoyancy = 53 300 − 40 000 = 13 300 tonnes<br />Tension = 13 300 × 1000 × 9.81 ≈ 130 MN</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>The tendons carry about 13 300 tonnes (130 MN) of pretension, which keeps the hull from heaving</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Waves, currents and payload changes vary the tension; designs ensure tendons never go slack in storms.</dd>
</dl>

## Decommissioning

At the end of field life, platforms must be removed or made safe. Topsides are usually lifted off in modules or in one piece by heavy lift vessels, jackets are cut and removed, and wells are permanently plugged with cement barriers. Regulations in many regions require full removal of structures in shallow water, while some large concrete bases may stay in place under agreed conditions. Some regions allow parts of jackets to remain as artificial reefs.

## Choosing a concept

Selection weighs water depth, reservoir size and life, distance to shore and existing infrastructure, environmental loads, need for dry trees, oil storage and export route, schedule, and cost. Small fields near existing hubs are often developed with subsea wells tied back to a host platform. The wells themselves are drilled by mobile [rotary drilling rigs](/petroleum-engineering/rotary-drilling-rig-components/) and maintained with [pipeline pigging](/petroleum-engineering/pipeline-pigging/) and inspection programmes across the life of the field.
