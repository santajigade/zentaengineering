<Key title="Short answer">

An **energy audit** is a systematic study of how a building, plant or process uses energy, to find where it is wasted and how to save it. The main types are a **walk-through (preliminary) audit**, a **detailed (general) audit** and an **investment grade audit**. The steps are: **plan and collect data**, **analyse bills and baseline**, **site survey and measurement**, **identify savings measures**, **evaluate costs, savings and payback**, **report and recommend**, then **implement and verify** savings.

</Key>

Energy is one of the largest controllable costs in industry and commercial buildings. An energy audit turns vague concern about bills into a clear list of actions with costs, savings and paybacks. It is also the first step toward an energy management system such as ISO 50001, and for large consumers in many countries it is a legal requirement.

## Types of energy audit

### Walk-through (preliminary) audit

A short visit, often one or two days, with a review of energy bills and a tour of the site. It identifies obvious waste and low cost measures and estimates savings roughly. ASHRAE calls this Level 1.

### Detailed (general) audit

A thorough study of major energy systems with measurements, load profiles and an energy balance. It quantifies savings and costs for each measure with reasonable accuracy, typically within about ±20%. ASHRAE Level 2.

### Investment grade (comprehensive) audit

An in depth engineering analysis for capital intensive projects, with detailed modelling, quotes from suppliers and risk analysis, suitable for financing decisions and energy performance contracts. ASHRAE Level 3.

| Type | Duration | Depth | Accuracy of savings | Typical output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Walk-through | Hours to days | Bills and visual survey | Rough | Quick wins, need for further study |
| Detailed | Weeks | Measurements, energy balance | About ±20% | Ranked list of measures with payback |
| Investment grade | Weeks to months | Engineering design, modelling | About ±10% | Bankable project proposals |

### 1. Plan and collect data

Agree scope, objectives and boundaries with management. Collect at least 12 to 36 months of electricity, fuel and water bills, production data, equipment lists, drawings, operating hours and any existing meter data.

### 2. Analyse bills and set a baseline

Break down consumption by energy type and month; study tariff structure, maximum demand charges and [power factor](/electrical-engineering/power-factor/) penalties. Relate energy use to production or floor area to form **energy performance indicators**, such as kWh per tonne or kWh per m² per year. Regression against production or degree days gives a **baseline** for measuring future savings.

### 3. Site survey and measurement

Inspect major energy users and measure their actual performance with portable instruments:

- Power and harmonics analysers on motors, transformers and feeders.
- Ultrasonic or clamp on [flow meters](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/flow-measurement/) on water, steam and compressed air lines.
- Thermal cameras for insulation losses, hot spots and steam traps.
- Flue gas analysers for boiler and furnace combustion efficiency.
- Lux meters for lighting, data loggers for temperature and humidity.
- Ultrasonic leak detectors for compressed air systems.

### 4. Identify energy saving measures

Typical opportunities include:

- **Motors and drives:** high efficiency motors and variable speed drives on pumps and fans, where affinity laws give large savings at reduced flow. See [induction motor working principle](/electrical-engineering/induction-motor-working-principle/).
- **Compressed air:** fixing leaks, lowering pressure, recovering compressor heat.
- **Pumps and fans:** right sizing, trimming impellers, removing throttling, as in [centrifugal pump](/mechanical-engineering/centrifugal-pump-working-principle/) systems.
- **Boilers and steam:** combustion tuning, condensate return, steam trap repair, insulation.
- **HVAC and refrigeration:** setpoint changes, free cooling, efficient chillers and controls.
- **Lighting:** LED retrofits, daylight and occupancy controls.
- **Heat recovery** between hot and cold streams, as in [pinch analysis](/chemical-engineering/pinch-analysis/).
- **Renewable supply:** rooftop [solar PV systems](/renewable-energy/how-solar-pv-systems-work/) and storage.

### 5. Evaluate savings and payback

For each measure, estimate energy and cost savings, implementation cost and financial indicators:

$$
\text{Simple payback (years)} = \frac{\text{Investment}}{\text{Annual savings}}
$$

Larger projects use net present value and internal rate of return.

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>A 55 kW cooling water pump runs 8000 h per year at full speed, with flow throttled by a valve. Measurements show the process needs only 75% of design flow. A variable speed drive costs 6000 (in local currency units, with electricity at 0.12 per kWh).</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>Annual savings and simple payback when the pump runs at 75% speed instead of throttling.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Measured input power is 55 kW; affinity law power ∝ speed³; 5% drive losses; static head negligible.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>Power at 75% speed = 55 × 0.75³ = 55 × 0.422 = 23.2 kW; with drive losses ≈ 24.4 kW<br />Savings = (55 − 24.4) × 8000 = 244 800 kWh per year<br />Cost saving = 244 800 × 0.12 = 29 376 per year<br />Payback = 6000 ÷ 29 376 = 0.20 years ≈ 2.5 months</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>About 245 MWh and 29 400 per year saved, with a payback of under three months</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Systems with high static head save less than the pure cube law suggests; measure before and after to verify.</dd>
</dl>
</Example>

### 6. Report and recommend

The audit report summarises the energy baseline, findings and a prioritised list of measures, grouped as no cost, low cost and capital projects, with savings, costs, paybacks, risks and an implementation plan. A clear executive summary helps management approve actions.

### 7. Implement and verify

Install measures, then **measure and verify** savings against the baseline, often following the IPMVP protocol, adjusting for production and weather. Results feed the next audit cycle and the organisation's energy management system.

## Common energy audit mistakes

- Relying on nameplate ratings instead of measured loads, which often overstates savings.
- Ignoring how equipment interacts, for example counting savings twice from both a new chiller and reduced cooling load.
- Recommending measures without checking production constraints, maintenance capability or safety.
- Skipping measurement and verification, so savings are never proven and momentum is lost.

## Making the energy audit count

The best audits combine accurate measurements with practical knowledge of how the site really runs, involve operators and maintenance staff, and lead to an ongoing programme of metering, targets and reviews under ISO 50001. Many sites also pair efficiency with on-site generation and storage, such as [battery energy storage](/renewable-energy/battery-energy-storage-systems/) to cut peak demand charges and a [microgrid](/renewable-energy/microgrid-explained/) for resilience.
