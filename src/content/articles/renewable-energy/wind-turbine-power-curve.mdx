<Key title="Short answer">

A **wind turbine power curve** is the graph of a turbine's electrical output against hub height wind speed. Output is zero below the **cut-in speed**, rises roughly with the cube of wind speed up to the **rated speed**, stays at **rated power** under pitch control, and drops to zero at the **cut-out speed**.

</Key>

The wind turbine power curve is the single most important performance document for a wind turbine. Combined with the wind speed distribution at a site, it tells you how much energy the machine will produce in a year, and from that its **capacity factor** and earnings. This article explains each part of the curve, how it is measured under IEC 61400-12-1, and how to turn it into annual energy. For the machine itself (blades, gearbox, generator, yaw and pitch systems), see [how wind turbines generate electricity](/renewable-energy/how-wind-turbines-work/).

## What a wind turbine power curve shows

A power curve plots the steady electrical power at the turbine terminals on the vertical axis against the ten-minute average wind speed at hub height on the horizontal axis. It is not a curve of instantaneous values: each point is an average over many ten-minute periods with similar wind speed. Manufacturers publish it as a table of power in 0.5 m/s or 1 m/s steps, referred to a standard air density of 1.225 kg/m³.

## The four regions of the power curve

**Region 1, below cut-in.** At very low wind speed the aerodynamic torque is too small to cover drivetrain losses, so the turbine idles or stays parked. Cut-in is typically about 3 to 4 m/s for large turbines.

**Region 2, partial load.** Between cut-in and rated speed the turbine tries to capture as much energy as possible. The power extracted is

$$
P = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho\,A\,v^{3}\,C_p
$$

where ρ is air density, A the swept area, v the wind speed and C_p the power coefficient. C_p can never exceed the **Betz limit** of 16/27, about 0.593. A variable speed turbine changes rotor speed in step with the wind to hold the tip speed ratio, and therefore C_p, near its optimum, so output rises roughly with the cube of wind speed.

**Region 3, full load.** At the rated wind speed, commonly about 11 to 13 m/s, the generator reaches its rated power. Above this, the blades are pitched to shed the extra aerodynamic power, so the curve is flat. Older stall regulated turbines used blade stall instead, which gives a rounded peak and some overshoot.

**Region 4, above cut-out.** At about 25 m/s (ten-minute average) the controller pitches the blades to feather and the rotor idles to protect the structure. Some modern turbines ramp power down gradually instead of stopping abruptly, which reduces lost energy in storms.

The sharp rise in Region 2 is why small changes in mean wind speed matter so much. A site with slightly higher average winds spends many more hours in the upper part of Region 2 and in Region 3.

## How a power curve is measured under IEC 61400-12-1

IEC 61400-12-1 defines how a power curve is measured on a real turbine so that results from different sites can be compared:

- **Wind measurement:** a calibrated cup or sonic anemometer on a met mast placed 2 to 4 rotor diameters upwind, 2.5 diameters being recommended, at hub height. The 2017 edition also allows rotor equivalent wind speed and nacelle lidar methods.
- **Data:** ten-minute averages of wind speed, wind direction, power, air temperature and pressure. Periods with the turbine unavailable, or with wind from sectors disturbed by obstacles or other turbines, are excluded.
- **Density normalisation:** for a pitch regulated turbine the wind speed is corrected to the reference density as v_n = v (ρ/ρ0)^(1/3), where ρ0 is 1.225 kg/m³.
- **Method of bins:** data are sorted into 0.5 m/s wind speed bins and averaged. The database must contain at least 180 hours in total and at least 30 minutes in every bin, covering from 1 m/s below cut-in up to 1.5 times the wind speed at 85% of rated power.
- **Uncertainty:** the report states the measurement uncertainty bin by bin, which feeds into energy yield risk.

Turbulence intensity and wind shear also change the measured curve. High turbulence tends to round off the knee near rated speed, which is why site specific power curves are often adjusted for local conditions.

## From power curve to annual energy

Annual energy production (AEP) combines the power curve with the frequency of each wind speed at the site. If the hours in each bin are known, AEP is simply the sum of power times hours. When only the mean wind speed is known, IEC 61400-12-1 uses a **Rayleigh distribution**, a Weibull distribution with shape factor 2:

$$
\text{AEP} = N_h \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[F(V_i) - F(V_{i-1})\right] \frac{P_{i-1} + P_i}{2}
$$

$$
F(V) = 1 - \exp\left[-\frac{\pi}{4}\left(\frac{V}{V_{ave}}\right)^{2}\right]
$$

where N_h is 8760 hours, P_i the power in bin i, F the cumulative probability and V_ave the annual mean wind speed at hub height. Real sites are better described with a measured Weibull shape factor, which Manwell and Burton both treat in detail.

## Capacity factor

The **capacity factor** compares the actual annual energy with what the turbine would produce at rated power for every hour of the year:

$$
CF = \frac{E_{year}}{P_{rated} \times 8760}
$$

Capacity factor is not an efficiency. It depends mostly on the site wind resource and on how large the rotor is relative to the generator rating, called **specific power** (rated power divided by swept area, in W/m²). A low specific power turbine reaches rated output at a lower wind speed, so it spends more hours in Region 3 and achieves a higher capacity factor, at the cost of a larger rotor. Onshore wind farms typically reach about 25 to 40%, and modern offshore farms about 40 to 50%.

## What changes the capacity factor in practice

- **Wind class:** IEC 61400-1 groups sites by reference wind speed, 50, 42.5 and 37.5 m/s for Classes I, II and III, which correspond to annual mean wind speeds of 10, 8.5 and 7.5 m/s at hub height. Low wind Class III turbines use larger rotors and lower specific power.
- **Hub height:** wind speed usually increases with height, so a taller tower moves the operating point to the right on the power curve.
- **Air density:** hot or high altitude sites have lower density, which lowers Region 2 output and delays rated power.
- **Availability and losses:** downtime for faults and maintenance, wake losses inside the wind farm, blade soiling or icing, and electrical losses in cables and transformers.
- **Curtailment:** grid operators may limit output when the network is congested. Storage such as [battery energy storage systems](/renewable-energy/battery-energy-storage-systems/) and local control in [microgrids](/renewable-energy/microgrid-explained/) help use energy that would otherwise be curtailed.

Comparing technologies on capacity factor alone can mislead. A [solar PV system](/renewable-energy/how-solar-pv-systems-work/) has a lower capacity factor than wind at a good site, yet produces at times of day that can complement wind output.

## Key formulas for wind turbine power curve

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Turbine power (Region 2) | P = ½ ρ A v³ C_p | ρ in kg/m³, A = πR² in m², v in m/s, P in W |
| Betz limit | C_p,max = 16/27 ≈ 0.593 | dimensionless |
| Density normalised wind speed | v_n = v (ρ/ρ0)^(1/3) | ρ0 = 1.225 kg/m³, pitch regulated turbines |
| Annual energy from bins | AEP = Σ P_i × h_i | P_i in kW, h_i = hours per year in bin i, AEP in kWh |
| Rayleigh cumulative distribution | F(V) = 1 − exp[−(π/4)(V/V_ave)²] | V_ave = annual mean hub height wind speed, m/s |
| Capacity factor | CF = E_year ÷ (P_rated × 8760) | E_year in kWh, P_rated in kW |
| Specific power | P_rated ÷ A | W/m² |

## Wind turbine power curve interview and viva questions

1. **What are the cut-in, rated and cut-out wind speeds?** Cut-in is the speed at which the turbine starts producing power, rated is the speed at which it reaches rated power, and cut-out is the speed at which it shuts down for protection, typically about 25 m/s.
2. **Why is the power curve flat above rated speed?** The generator and drivetrain are sized for rated power, so the pitch system turns the blades to reduce aerodynamic efficiency and hold output constant.
3. **Why does IEC 61400-12-1 normalise the wind speed for air density?** Power is proportional to density, so measurements at different sites and seasons would not be comparable; normalising to 1.225 kg/m³ gives a standard curve.
4. **What is the method of bins?** Ten-minute data are grouped into 0.5 m/s wind speed bins and averaged in each bin to give one power value per bin, which forms the measured power curve.
5. **How does specific power affect capacity factor?** A lower specific power, meaning a larger rotor for the same generator, reaches rated power at lower wind speed and spends more hours at full output, which raises the capacity factor.
