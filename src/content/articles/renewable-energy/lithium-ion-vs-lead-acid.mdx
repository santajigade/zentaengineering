<Key title="Short answer">

**Lithium-ion vs lead acid** is a comparison of two rechargeable battery chemistries. Lithium-ion cells shuttle lithium ions between a graphite anode and a metal oxide or phosphate cathode, giving higher energy density, deeper usable discharge, higher efficiency and longer cycle life. Lead acid cells cost less upfront, are simple to charge and recycle well.

</Key>

The **lithium-ion vs lead acid** choice comes up in every solar home system, UPS, telecom site, forklift and grid storage project. Both store energy chemically and both work, but they behave very differently in weight, usable capacity, lifetime, maintenance and safety. This article compares them side by side, explains why they differ, and shows how the differences change the size of a battery bank.

## How each battery works

Both batteries are electrochemical cells: two electrodes in an electrolyte, with a separator between them. During discharge, the negative electrode is oxidised and releases electrons into the external circuit, while ions move through the electrolyte to keep charge balanced.

### Lithium-ion

In a lithium-ion cell, lithium is stored between the graphite layers of the anode. On discharge, lithium ions leave the graphite, cross the organic electrolyte and separator, and slot into the cathode crystal structure, which may be a layered oxide such as NMC or LCO, or lithium iron phosphate (LFP). Electrons take the external path through the load. Charging reverses both flows. Because lithium simply moves in and out of host structures (intercalation), the electrodes change little and the cell survives many cycles.

### Lead acid

In a lead acid cell, the negative plate is sponge lead (Pb), the positive plate is lead dioxide (PbO₂) and the electrolyte is dilute sulfuric acid. On discharge, both plates turn into lead sulfate and the acid is consumed, so the electrolyte specific gravity falls. The overall reaction is:

$$
\text{Pb} + \text{PbO}_2 + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow 2\,\text{PbSO}_4 + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}
$$

Lead sulfate is bulkier than the original active materials, and if it is left on the plates it hardens (sulfation). This is the main reason lead acid life depends so strongly on depth of discharge and on recharging promptly.

The values below are typical ranges from Linden's Handbook of Batteries. Exact figures depend on cell design, chemistry variant and test conditions, so always use the manufacturer datasheet for design.

| Property | Lithium-ion (LFP, NMC) | Lead acid (flooded, VRLA) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nominal cell voltage | About 3.2 V (LFP) to 3.7 V (NMC) | 2.0 V |
| Specific energy | Roughly 100 to 250 Wh/kg | Roughly 30 to 50 Wh/kg |
| Cycle life | Thousands of cycles (LFP highest) | Hundreds to about 1000+ cycles, strongly DoD dependent |
| Usable depth of discharge | Typically 80 to 90%+ | Often limited to about 50% for long life |
| Round trip efficiency | About 90 to 95% | About 70 to 85% |
| Upfront cost | Higher | Lower |
| Cost per stored and delivered kWh over life | Often lower in daily cycling duty | Often higher in daily cycling, competitive in standby |
| Safety | Risk of thermal runaway; needs BMS and IEC 62619 type safety design | Hydrogen gassing on charge, corrosive acid; needs ventilation |
| Maintenance | Essentially none for the cells; BMS monitoring | Flooded: water top up and specific gravity checks; VRLA: periodic inspection and testing |

| Self-discharge | Low | Higher; needs float or periodic charge in storage |
| Recycling | Growing, more complex | Very well established |

## Differences explained

### Energy density and weight

Lithium is the lightest metal and lithium-ion cells run at about 3.2 to 3.7 V, nearly twice the 2.0 V of a lead acid cell. Higher voltage and lighter materials give several times the specific energy. That is why every modern electric vehicle and phone uses lithium-ion, while lead acid remains for applications where weight matters little, such as stationary standby or forklift counterweight.

### Cycle life and depth of discharge

Depth of discharge (DoD) is the fraction of rated capacity removed in a cycle. Lead acid plates suffer from sulfation and active material shedding during deep cycles, so designers usually limit regular DoD to around 50% to reach a useful cycle life. Lithium-ion intercalation causes far less mechanical stress, so cells routinely run at 80 to 90% DoD while still giving thousands of cycles. This alone means a lead acid bank needs roughly twice the nameplate capacity for the same usable energy.

### Efficiency

Lead acid batteries lose energy to gassing and to higher internal resistance, especially near full charge. Lithium-ion cells have high coulombic efficiency and low resistance, so more of the charging energy comes back out. In a solar system this means fewer panels are needed to refill the battery each day.

### Safety and maintenance

Lithium-ion cells contain a flammable organic electrolyte. Overcharge, internal short circuits, crushing or overheating can trigger **thermal runaway**, so packs need a battery management system that monitors cell voltage and temperature, and cells for industrial use are tested to IEC 62619. Lead acid cells cannot burn in the same way, but they release hydrogen and oxygen on charge, so battery rooms need ventilation, and the acid is corrosive. Stationary lead acid batteries are tested to IEC 60896-21 and IEC 60896-22, and maintained following IEEE 450 (vented) and IEEE 1188 (VRLA), which cover inspections, capacity tests and replacement criteria.

## Sizing a battery bank: worked example

The nominal capacity needed to deliver a given daily energy is:

$$
E_\text{nom} = \frac{E_\text{load} \times N}{\text{DoD} \times \eta}
$$

$$
C_\text{Ah} = \frac{E_\text{nom}}{V_\text{sys}}
$$

where E_load is the daily load energy, N the days of autonomy, DoD the allowed depth of discharge, η the discharge path efficiency and V_sys the battery bank voltage.

<dt>Find</dt><dd>The nominal energy and amp hour rating of each battery bank.</dd>

## When to use which

Choose **lithium-ion** (usually LFP for stationary use) when the battery cycles daily, weight or space is limited, efficiency matters, or maintenance access is poor. Typical cases are solar self consumption, [battery energy storage systems](/renewable-energy/battery-energy-storage-systems/), [microgrids](/renewable-energy/microgrid-explained/) and electric vehicles, as described in [EV charging levels and connectors](/renewable-energy/ev-charging-levels-and-connectors/).

Choose **lead acid** when the battery mostly floats and rarely discharges, upfront cost is the main constraint, or the site already has trained staff and chargers for it. UPS systems, substation and telecom DC supplies, and engine starting are common examples. Note that a [solar PV system](/renewable-energy/how-solar-pv-systems-work/) inverter or charge controller must be set for the chemistry used. The IEA notes that lithium-ion now dominates new battery storage deployments, driven by falling costs and the shift towards LFP.

| Required nominal energy | E_nom = E_load × N ÷ (DoD × η) | E_load in kWh per day, N = days of autonomy, η = efficiency |
| Amp hour rating | C_Ah = E_nom ÷ V_sys | E_nom in Wh, V_sys in V |

## Lithium-ion vs lead acid interview and viva questions

1. **Why does a lithium-ion cell have a higher voltage than a lead acid cell?** The electrode potential difference between lithiated graphite and a lithium metal oxide or phosphate cathode is larger, giving about 3.2 to 3.7 V versus 2.0 V for lead acid.
2. **What happens to the electrolyte of a lead acid battery during discharge?** Sulfuric acid is consumed and water is produced, so specific gravity falls, which is why it can indicate state of charge in flooded cells.
3. **Why is lead acid DoD usually limited to about 50%?** Deep discharge accelerates sulfation and active material shedding, which shortens cycle life sharply.
4. **What is thermal runaway?** It is a self heating chain in a lithium-ion cell where rising temperature triggers exothermic reactions that raise temperature further, which the BMS and cell design must prevent.
5. **Why does a lead acid bank need about twice the nameplate capacity of a lithium-ion bank for the same load?** Lead acid allows roughly half the DoD and has lower efficiency, so more nominal energy must be installed to deliver the same usable energy.
