<Key title="Short answer">

**EV charging levels** describe how fast an electric vehicle charges. **Level 1** uses a household socket (about 1.4 to 2.3 kW AC) and adds only a few km of range per hour. **Level 2** uses a dedicated AC charger (about 3.7 to 22 kW), ideal for homes and workplaces. **DC fast charging** (often called Level 3, 50 to 350 kW or more) bypasses the car's onboard charger and feeds the battery directly. Connectors differ by region: **Type 1** and **NACS** in North America, **Type 2** and **CCS2** in Europe and India, **CHAdeMO** in Japan and **GB/T** in China.

</Key>

Charging an electric vehicle can take anything from 15 minutes to a whole day, depending on the charger, the car and the battery. Understanding charging levels, AC versus DC, and the plug standards helps drivers, fleet managers and engineers plan charging infrastructure.

## AC charging and DC charging

An EV battery stores DC energy. The question is where the AC from the grid is converted to DC.

- **AC charging:** the charging station supplies AC, and the car's **onboard charger** converts it to DC. Onboard chargers are limited by size, weight and cost, typically 7.4 to 11 kW, sometimes 22 kW.
- **DC charging:** the station contains a large rectifier and supplies DC directly to the battery, controlled by the car's battery management system. Power is limited by the station and the battery's acceptance, not the onboard charger.

## EV charging levels in detail

### Level 1 (slow AC)

Uses a standard household socket through a portable cable with an in-cable control and protection device (IEC Mode 2). Power is about 1.4 to 1.9 kW at 120 V in North America and up to about 2.3 kW at 230 V. Adds roughly 8 to 15 km of range per hour. Suitable for overnight top ups, plug in hybrids or emergency use.

### Level 2 (AC)

A fixed wallbox or post on a dedicated circuit (IEC Mode 3), at 208 to 240 V single phase or 400 V three phase. Common ratings are 7.4 kW (32 A single phase), 11 kW (16 A three phase) and 22 kW (32 A three phase). Adds about 30 to 100 km of range per hour depending on the car. The standard for homes, workplaces, hotels and car parks.

### DC fast charging (Level 3)

Off-board DC chargers (IEC Mode 4) of 50, 150, 350 kW or more, with liquid cooled cables at the high end. Many cars can add 200 to 300 km of range in 20 to 30 minutes. Used along highways, in cities and for fleets. Megawatt charging systems (MCS) above 1 MW are being introduced for heavy trucks.

| Level | Supply | Typical power | Range added per hour | Typical location |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Level 1 | AC socket | 1.4 to 2.3 kW | 8 to 15 km | Home, backup |
| Level 2 | AC wallbox | 3.7 to 22 kW | 30 to 100 km | Home, work, public car parks |
| DC fast | Off-board DC | 50 to 350+ kW | 300 to 1500+ km | Highways, hubs, fleets |

- **Type 1 (SAE J1772):** single phase AC up to about 7.4 kW; North America (now being replaced by NACS) and older Japanese cars.
- **Type 2 (IEC 62196-2, Mennekes):** single or three phase AC up to 22 kW (43 kW in rare cases); standard in Europe and India.
- **CCS (Combined Charging System):** adds two large DC pins under a Type 1 (CCS1) or Type 2 (CCS2) inlet, so one inlet supports both AC and DC fast charging up to 350 kW or more.
- **CHAdeMO:** Japanese DC standard, up to about 100 kW in common use, declining outside Japan.
- **NACS (SAE J3400):** Tesla's compact connector, carrying both AC and DC through the same pins; adopted by most North American carmakers.
- **GB/T:** Chinese national standard with separate AC and DC connectors.

## Communication and safety

Before power flows, the car and charger communicate. AC charging uses a **control pilot** PWM signal (IEC 61851) that tells the car the maximum current available. DC charging uses digital communication (power line communication under ISO 15118 or DIN 70121 for CCS, CAN for CHAdeMO and GB/T) to agree voltage and current limits. **Plug and Charge** under ISO 15118 lets the car authenticate itself without an app or card. Chargers include residual current protection, ground monitoring, cable locking and insulation checks.

## Charging time

A simple estimate of charging time is:

$$
t \approx \frac{E_\text{battery} \times \Delta SoC}{P \times \eta}
$$

## Planning charging infrastructure

- Assess the **supply capacity**: a few 22 kW points or a DC hub may need a new transformer or upgraded service.
- Use **load management** to share available power between chargers and avoid costly peaks; add [battery energy storage](/renewable-energy/battery-energy-storage-systems/) where grid capacity is limited.
- Combine with **solar PV** for lower cost, cleaner charging, as in [how solar PV systems work](/renewable-energy/how-solar-pv-systems-work/).
- Follow wiring rules for dedicated circuits, RCD types, [earthing](/electrical-engineering/electrical-earthing/) and [cable sizing](/electrical-engineering/cable-sizing/).
- Plan for **bidirectional charging** (V2H, V2G), which lets EV batteries support homes and the grid and fits naturally into [microgrids](/renewable-energy/microgrid-explained/).

Choosing the right EV charging levels for each location, fast for travel and slow for long stays, gives drivers convenience while keeping grid impact and costs under control.
