<Key title="Short answer">

**Sampling theorem**, also called the Nyquist-Shannon theorem, states that a signal containing no frequencies above fmax can be reconstructed exactly from samples taken at a rate fs greater than 2 × fmax. Frequencies above half the sampling rate reappear as false lower frequencies called aliases, so an analog **[anti-aliasing filter](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/active-filter-sallen-and-key/)** is placed before the ADC.

</Key>

## Why sampling needs a rule

Digital systems such as [microcontrollers](/electronics-engineering/microcontroller-vs-microprocessor/), data loggers, digital oscilloscopes and audio recorders measure analog signals at discrete instants. Between samples, nothing is recorded. The question is how often samples must be taken so that the original signal can be recovered without ambiguity. The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem answers it.

## The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem

If a continuous signal is **band-limited**, meaning it contains no frequency components above fmax, it is completely described by samples taken at:

$$
f_s > 2\,f_{max}
$$

Two related terms are used:

- The **Nyquist rate** is 2 × fmax, the minimum sampling rate for a signal with highest frequency fmax.
- The **Nyquist frequency** is fs / 2, the highest frequency that a system sampling at fs can represent without ambiguity.

The theorem assumes ideal conditions: an exactly band-limited signal, perfectly timed samples and an ideal reconstruction filter. Real systems sample with a margin above 2 × fmax to make filtering practical.

## What aliasing is

When a sine wave above the Nyquist frequency is sampled, the samples are identical to those of a sine wave at a lower frequency. The system cannot tell them apart, so the high frequency appears in the data as a false low frequency. This is **aliasing**.

A sampled frequency f appears at the alias frequency:

$$
f_{alias} = \left| f - k\,f_s \right|
$$

where k is the integer that brings the result into the range 0 to fs / 2.

## Preventing aliasing

### Anti-aliasing filter

The only reliable protection is an analog **low-pass filter** placed before the ADC. It attenuates all frequencies above the Nyquist frequency to a level below the ADC's resolution, so nothing significant is left to alias.

A simple [RC filter](/electronics-engineering/pcb-design-basics/) rolls off at only 20 dB per decade, which is rarely enough on its own. Practical anti-aliasing filters are often second order or higher, such as Sallen-Key or multiple feedback active filters, or a combination of an RC filter with a high sampling rate.

### Choosing the sampling rate

Because real filters cannot cut off instantly, the sampling rate is chosen well above 2 × fmax, leaving a **transition band** for the filter:

| Application | Signal bandwidth | Typical sampling rate | Ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Telephone speech | About 3.4 kHz | 8 kHz | About 2.4 |
| CD audio | 20 kHz | 44.1 kHz | About 2.2 |
| Professional audio | 20 kHz | 48 kHz or 96 kHz | 2.4 or 4.8 |
| Industrial process signals | A few hertz | 10 Hz to 1 kHz | Often much more than 10 |

### Oversampling

Sampling much faster than needed, then filtering and reducing the rate digitally (**decimation**), relaxes the analog filter. Sigma-delta ADCs use this technique: they sample at a very high rate, so a simple RC filter is enough, and a digital filter inside the converter removes the out-of-band content. Oversampling also spreads quantisation noise over a wider band, which improves resolution after filtering.

## Quantisation: the other half of digitising

Sampling discretises time; **quantisation** discretises amplitude. An ideal N-bit ADC sampling a full-scale sine wave has a signal to quantisation noise ratio of:

$$
\text{SNR} \approx 6.02\,N + 1.76 \;\text{dB}
$$

A 12-bit ADC therefore has an ideal SNR of about 74 dB, and a 16-bit ADC about 98 dB. Real converters fall short of these figures because of their own noise and distortion.

## Sampling jitter

The theorem assumes samples are taken at exactly equal intervals. Variation in the sampling instants, called **jitter**, adds noise that grows with signal frequency. For high-frequency, high-resolution converters, a low-jitter clock is as important as the ADC itself.

## Reconstruction

To turn samples back into a continuous signal, a digital to analog converter (DAC) outputs a stepped waveform, and a **reconstruction filter** removes the high-frequency images around multiples of fs. In theory an ideal low-pass filter recovers the original signal exactly; in practice, oversampling and digital interpolation filters make the analog reconstruction filter simple.

## A practical example: measuring a 50 Hz power signal

Consider a power monitor that must measure mains voltage and current up to the 25th harmonic on a 50 Hz system. The highest frequency of interest is 25 × 50 = 1250 Hz, so the Nyquist rate is 2500 Hz. Sampling at, for example, 6.4 kHz (128 samples per 50 Hz cycle) leaves a wide transition band. An anti-aliasing filter with a cut-off around 2 kHz then attenuates switching noise from drives and inverters, which can contain components of several kilohertz, before it can alias into the harmonic measurement range. Sampling synchronously with the mains, an integer number of samples per cycle, also makes the later Fourier analysis more accurate.

## Key formulas for the sampling theorem

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sampling condition | fs > 2 × fmax | fs = sampling rate in Hz, fmax = highest signal frequency in Hz |
| Nyquist rate | 2 × fmax | Minimum sampling rate for the signal, in Hz |
| Nyquist frequency | fs ÷ 2 | Highest frequency represented without ambiguity, in Hz |
| Alias frequency | f<sub>alias</sub> = abs(f − k × fs) | k = integer that brings the result into 0 to fs ÷ 2 |
| Ideal quantisation SNR | SNR ≈ 6.02 N + 1.76 dB | N = ADC resolution in bits, full-scale sine input |

## Practical checklist

- Find the highest frequency of interest and of any interference that could reach the ADC, including switching noise and mains harmonics.
- Choose fs with a comfortable margin above twice the bandwidth of interest.
- Design an anti-aliasing filter that attenuates everything above fs / 2 below the ADC's noise floor.
- Consider oversampling with digital filtering where a sharp analog filter is hard to build.
- Never rely on software to remove aliasing after sampling.

## Sampling theorem interview and viva questions

1. **What does the sampling theorem state?** A band-limited signal with no components above fmax is completely described by samples taken at a rate fs greater than 2 × fmax.
2. **What is the difference between the Nyquist rate and the Nyquist frequency?** The Nyquist rate is 2 × fmax, the minimum sampling rate for a given signal. The Nyquist frequency is fs / 2, the highest frequency a system sampling at fs can represent without ambiguity.
3. **Where does a 700 Hz signal appear when sampled at 1000 Hz?** It aliases to 1000 − 700 = 300 Hz, because it lies above the 500 Hz Nyquist frequency.
4. **Why can aliasing not be removed in software after sampling?** An alias is indistinguishable from a real signal at the alias frequency, so it must be prevented by an analog anti-aliasing filter before the ADC.
5. **How does oversampling help an ADC design?** Sampling much faster than needed and then decimating digitally relaxes the analog filter, and it spreads quantisation noise over a wider band, which improves resolution after filtering.
