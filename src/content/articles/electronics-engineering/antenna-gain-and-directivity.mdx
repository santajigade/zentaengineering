<Key title="Short answer">

Antenna gain and directivity describe how well an antenna focuses energy in a chosen direction. An antenna converts electrical signals in a transmission line into radio waves, and back again on receive. Its **radiation pattern** shows how strongly it radiates in each direction. **Directivity** measures how concentrated that pattern is compared with an antenna radiating equally in all directions, and **gain** is directivity reduced by the antenna's losses. Gain is usually quoted in **[dBi](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/decibel-in-electronics-db-dbm/)**.

</Key>

## How an antenna radiates

Radio waves are produced by accelerating electric charges. When an alternating current flows in a conductor whose length is a significant fraction of a wavelength, the changing electric and magnetic fields detach from the conductor and travel outwards at the speed of light. The wavelength λ is set by the frequency f:

$$
\lambda = \frac{c}{f}
$$

where c is about 3 × 10⁸ m/s in free space. At 100 MHz the wavelength is 3 m; at 2.4 GHz it is 12.5 cm.

Antennas are **reciprocal**: an antenna has the same pattern, gain and [impedance](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/what-is-impedance/) whether it transmits or receives.

## Radiation pattern

The radiation pattern is a plot of the radiated field or power against direction, usually shown as two cuts: the **E-plane** (containing the electric field) and the **H-plane** (containing the magnetic field). Key features:

- **Main lobe:** the direction of maximum radiation.
- **Side lobes and back lobe:** smaller lobes in other directions, usually unwanted.
- **Half-power beamwidth (HPBW):** the angle between the points where the power falls to half (−3 dB) of the maximum.
- **Front-to-back ratio:** main lobe level compared with the back lobe, in dB.

An **isotropic** antenna, which radiates equally in all directions, is a theoretical reference. An **omnidirectional** antenna, such as a vertical dipole, radiates equally in all horizontal directions but not upwards or downwards.

## Antenna gain and directivity

**Directivity** D is the ratio of the radiation intensity in the direction of maximum radiation to the average radiation intensity over all directions. A larger D means a narrower, more focused beam.

**Gain** G includes the antenna's efficiency η, which accounts for losses in conductors, dielectrics and mismatch:

$$
G = \eta\,D
$$

Gain is usually expressed in decibels relative to an isotropic antenna:

$$
G_{dBi} = 10\log_{10} G
$$

A half-wave dipole has a directivity of about 1.64, or 2.15 dBi. Gain relative to a dipole, in dBd, is 2.15 dB lower than the dBi figure.

## Impedance, matching and bandwidth

An antenna presents an impedance at its feed point. A half-wave dipole in free space has a radiation resistance of about 73 Ω at resonance. Maximum power transfers when the antenna impedance matches the transmission line, typically 50 Ω or 75 Ω. Mismatch reflects part of the power back, which is measured as:

- **Return loss:** how far the reflected power is below the incident power, in dB. Higher is better.
- **[VSWR](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/impedance-matching-explained/):** voltage standing wave ratio. A VSWR of 1:1 is a perfect match; 2:1 or better is a common acceptance level.

The **bandwidth** of an antenna is the frequency range over which its impedance match, gain and pattern stay within specified limits.

## Polarisation

Polarisation is the orientation of the electric field of the radiated wave. It can be linear (vertical or horizontal) or circular (right- or left-hand). Transmit and receive antennas must have matching polarisation; a vertically polarised antenna receiving a horizontally polarised signal suffers a large loss in theory. Circular polarisation is common for satellite links because it is not affected by the orientation of the satellite.

## Common antenna types

| Antenna | Typical gain | Pattern | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Half-wave dipole | 2.15 dBi | Omnidirectional around its axis | Reference antenna, FM and VHF |
| Quarter-wave monopole | Depends on ground plane | Omnidirectional | Vehicles, handheld radios |
| Yagi-Uda | Moderate to high, rising with element count | Directional | TV reception, point-to-point VHF and UHF |
| Microstrip patch | Moderate | Broad beam on one side | GPS, Wi-Fi, mobile devices |
| Horn | Moderate to high | Directional | Microwave test, feeds for dishes |
| Parabolic dish | Very high | Narrow beam | Satellite and microwave links |

## Antennas in a radio link

The Friis transmission equation relates received power to the antenna gains and the distance d between them in free space:

$$
P_r = P_t\,G_t\,G_r\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2
$$

The term (4πd / λ)² is the **free-space path loss**. In decibels, path loss for d in metres and f in hertz is 20 log₁₀(d) + 20 log₁₀(f) − 147.55. At 2.4 GHz over 100 m this gives about 80 dB. Adding antenna gain at either end directly reduces the transmit power needed for the same received signal.

## Effective aperture

A receiving antenna collects power from the wave passing it as if it had a capture area called the **effective aperture** Ae:

$$
A_e = \frac{G\,\lambda^2}{4\pi}
$$

For the same gain, the effective aperture falls with the square of frequency. This is one reason why higher-frequency links need high-gain antennas to receive enough power, and why dish antennas grow more effective as frequency rises for a fixed physical size.

## Choosing an antenna

1. Start from the **frequency band** and required **bandwidth**.
2. Decide the **coverage**: all directions (omnidirectional) or a point-to-point link (directional).
3. Match the **polarisation** at both ends of the link.
4. Check **gain** against the link budget, including path loss, cable loss and fade margin.
5. Consider size, mounting, weather exposure and the effect of nearby metal, which can detune the antenna and distort its pattern.

## Near field and far field

Close to an antenna, the fields are complex and the pattern is not yet formed. The far field, where the radiation pattern is valid, begins at roughly 2D² / λ for an antenna of largest dimension D. Antenna measurements must be made in the far field or corrected with near-field techniques.
