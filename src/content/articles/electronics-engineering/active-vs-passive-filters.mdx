  - q: "Can a passive filter have gain?"
    a: "A passive filter cannot give power gain, because it has no energy source. An RLC circuit can show a voltage peak near resonance when Q is high, but the output power is still less than the input power. Any real gain above unity needs an active device such as an op amp or transistor."

<Key title="Short answer">

**Active vs passive filters** differ in the amplifying device. A passive filter uses only resistors, capacitors and inductors, needs no supply and cannot give power gain. An active filter adds an op amp or transistor, giving gain and a low output impedance unaffected by the load. Passive suits RF and high power; active suits low frequencies.

</Key>

Filters pass the frequencies you want and attenuate the rest. They appear in audio equipment, sensor interfaces, power supplies, radio receivers and in front of every analog to digital converter to prevent aliasing, as explained in the [sampling theorem](/electronics-engineering/sampling-theorem/). The comparison below follows Horowitz and Hill, Sedra and Smith, and the Texas Instruments book Op Amps for Everyone.

## Comparison table: active vs passive filters

| Feature | Passive filter | Active filter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Components | R, L, C only | R, C plus op amp or transistor |
| Power supply | Not needed | Needed |
| Gain | Cannot exceed unity power gain | Can provide voltage and power gain |
| Output impedance | Depends on the components; can be high | Very low (op amp output) |
| Effect of load | Load changes gain and cutoff | Little effect when buffered |
| Cascading stages | Stages load each other | Stages can be cascaded independently |
| Inductors | Often needed for sharp responses | Not needed |
| Frequency range | Up to RF and microwave | Limited by op amp bandwidth and slew rate |
| Signal level | Handles high voltage and power | Limited by supply rails and output current |
| Noise | Thermal noise of resistors only | Adds op amp voltage and current noise |
| Tuning | Fixed by component values | Gain and Q set easily by resistor ratios |

## Passive filters explained

The simplest passive filter is an RC low pass: a series resistor and a shunt capacitor. Its cutoff frequency, where the output has fallen by 3 dB (to about 70.7 percent of the input voltage), is:

$$
f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R C}
$$

Swapping R and C gives a high pass filter with the same cutoff formula. A single RC stage is **first order**: above cutoff the gain falls at 20 dB per decade. Sharper roll off needs more reactive components. LC ladder filters, for example, give second order or higher responses with low loss, because ideal inductors and capacitors dissipate no power. That is why LC filters dominate in radio frequency circuits and in power supply and mains filters, where large currents and voltages must be handled.

The weakness of passive filters is **loading**. The source impedance driving the filter and the load impedance at its output both become part of the circuit. Connecting a low impedance load shifts the cutoff and reduces the passband gain, as the worked example below shows. Cascading two RC stages to get a second order response also gives a poor result, because the second stage loads the first.

## Active filters explained

An active filter places an amplifier inside or after the RC network. The amplifier provides a high input impedance and a very low output impedance, so the filter response is set by its own components and does not change with the load. The two most common second order topologies, described in Op Amps for Everyone and TI application report SLOA049, are:

- **Sallen-Key:** a unity gain or non-inverting amplifier with two resistors and two capacitors. It is simple, needs few parts and has low sensitivity to op amp gain at unity gain.
- **Multiple feedback (MFB):** an inverting amplifier with the filter components in its feedback paths. It gives an inverted output and is less sensitive to component tolerances at higher Q.

Each second order stage gives a roll off of 40 dB per decade. Higher orders are built by cascading second order stages and, for odd orders, one first order stage. The response shape is chosen from classic approximations: **Butterworth** for the flattest passband, **Chebyshev** for a steeper transition with passband ripple, and **Bessel** for the best phase linearity and step response. The basic amplifier circuits used are those in [inverting and non-inverting amplifiers](/electronics-engineering/inverting-and-non-inverting-amplifier/), and a detailed Sallen-Key design walk-through is available on [InstrumentationBlog](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/active-filter-sallen-and-key/).

## Worked example: why loading matters

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>Passive RC low pass with R = 10 kΩ and C = 10 nF, driven from an ideal voltage source. The output is connected to a load of RL = 10 kΩ.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>Cutoff frequency unloaded, then the passband gain and cutoff frequency with the load connected, and the effect of adding a unity gain op amp buffer.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Ideal source with zero output impedance; ideal op amp buffer with infinite input impedance and zero output impedance.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>Unloaded: fc = 1 / (2π × 10 000 × 10 × 10⁻⁹) = 1592 Hz, passband gain = 1 (0 dB)<br />Loaded: the source sees R in series with C parallel RL. Using Thevenin's theorem at the capacitor, the source becomes a divider gain RL / (R + RL) = 10 / 20 = 0.5 behind a resistance R parallel RL = 5 kΩ<br />Passband gain = 0.5, which is 20 log10(0.5) = -6.0 dB<br />fc = 1 / (2π × 5000 × 10 × 10⁻⁹) = 3183 Hz<br />With a buffer between the capacitor and the load, the RC network sees no load, so fc = 1592 Hz and gain = 1 are restored</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>Unloaded fc = 1592 Hz at 0 dB; loaded fc = 3183 Hz at -6.0 dB; buffered (active) fc = 1592 Hz at 0 dB</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>A real op amp has finite bandwidth and output impedance, which matter at high frequencies, and a real source has some output resistance that adds to R.</dd>
</dl>
</Example>

The loaded filter has its cutoff doubled and its signal halved. This is the loading problem in one line, and it is why active filters are preferred when the next stage has a low or unknown impedance. The divider step uses the method shown in [Thevenin's theorem](/electrical-engineering/thevenins-theorem/).

## When to use which

Choose a **passive filter** when:

- The frequency is high, from tens of megahertz into RF, where op amps run out of bandwidth.
- Large voltages or currents are involved, as in mains EMI filters, power converter output filters and loudspeaker crossovers.
- No power supply is available, or the lowest possible noise and distortion are needed.
- A simple first order RC is enough, for example decoupling or an ADC input network.

Choose an **active filter** when:

- The frequency is low, such as audio, biomedical and sensor signals, where inductors would be large.
- Gain, buffering or a sharp, precisely defined response is needed.
- Several stages must be cascaded without loading each other.
- The filter must be adjustable or easily tuned through resistor values.

## Design tips

- Keep the op amp gain bandwidth product well above the product of the stage gain, Q and cutoff frequency; TI SLOA049 gives guidance for each topology.
- Use stable capacitor types such as C0G/NP0 ceramic or film capacitors in the frequency setting positions.
- Place high Q stages last in a cascade to reduce the risk of clipping inside the filter.
- Lay out the filter close to the ADC with a solid ground return, as covered in [PCB design basics](/electronics-engineering/pcb-design-basics/).

## Key formulas for active vs passive filters

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RC cutoff frequency | fc = 1 / (2π R C) | fc in Hz, R in Ω, C in F |
| Loaded RC passband gain | Gain = RL / (R + RL) | RL = load resistance in Ω |
| Loaded RC cutoff | fc = 1 / (2π (R ∥ RL) C) | R ∥ RL = R × RL / (R + RL), in Ω |
| Gain in decibels | Gain (dB) = 20 log10(Vout / Vin) | Vout and Vin in V |
| Roll off above cutoff | 20 dB per decade per order | First order 20, second order 40 dB per decade |

## Active vs passive filters interview and viva questions

1. **What is the main difference between active and passive filters?** A passive filter uses only R, L and C and needs no power supply, while an active filter adds an op amp or transistor that provides gain and a low output impedance.
2. **Why is loading a problem for passive filters?** The load becomes part of the circuit, so a low impedance load changes both the passband gain and the cutoff frequency, as the worked example shows with gain falling to 0.5 and fc doubling.
3. **Why are active filters preferred at audio and low frequencies?** At low frequencies the inductors a passive filter would need become large, heavy and lossy, while an active filter reaches the same response with only R, C and an op amp.
4. **Name two common second order active filter topologies.** Sallen-Key, a unity gain or non-inverting stage with two resistors and two capacitors, and multiple feedback, an inverting stage with the components in its feedback paths.
5. **When would you choose a Butterworth, Chebyshev or Bessel response?** Butterworth gives the flattest passband, Chebyshev a steeper transition with passband ripple, and Bessel the best phase linearity and step response.
