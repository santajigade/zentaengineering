<Key title="Short answer">

The **transistor working principle** of a bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is that a small current into the base controls a much larger current from collector to emitter. With the base-emitter junction forward biased and the base-collector junction reverse biased, carriers injected from the emitter cross a thin base and are swept into the collector, so I<sub>C</sub> ≈ β I<sub>B</sub>.

</Key>

The transistor is the building block of amplifiers, switches, oscillators and digital logic. The bipolar junction transistor was the first type to be mass produced, and it is still widely used for discrete switching, analog amplifiers and power stages. This guide explains its structure, how it works inside, the equations that describe it, its operating regions, and how to design a simple transistor switch.

## Construction of a BJT

A BJT is made of three doped semiconductor regions forming two PN junctions. In an **NPN** transistor a thin P-type base sits between an N-type emitter and an N-type collector. A **PNP** transistor has the opposite arrangement. The three regions are not alike:

- **Emitter:** heavily doped, so it can inject a large number of carriers into the base.
- **Base:** very thin and lightly doped, so most injected carriers pass through without recombining.
- **Collector:** moderately doped and physically the largest region, because it must collect the carriers and dissipate most of the heat.

The circuit symbol has an arrow on the emitter showing the direction of conventional current: out of the emitter for NPN, into the emitter for PNP. If you are new to PN junctions, the article [what is a diode](/electronics-engineering/what-is-a-diode/) explains forward and reverse bias first.

## Transistor working principle step by step

The description below is for an NPN device in the forward-active mode. For PNP the roles of electrons and holes, and all polarities, are reversed.

1. **Bias the junctions.** The base-emitter (BE) junction is forward biased, about 0.6 to 0.7 V for silicon. The base-collector (BC) junction is reverse biased by making the collector more positive than the base.
2. **Emitter injects electrons.** Forward bias lowers the BE barrier, and the heavily doped emitter injects a large flow of electrons into the base.
3. **Electrons diffuse across the base.** Because the base is thin and lightly doped, few electrons find holes to recombine with. The few that do, plus holes injected from base into emitter, make up the small base current I<sub>B</sub>.
4. **Collector sweeps them up.** Electrons reaching the edge of the reverse biased BC depletion region are pulled across by its electric field into the collector. This forms the collector current I<sub>C</sub>.
5. **Control.** Raising V<sub>BE</sub> slightly raises the injection exponentially, so the base terminal controls the collector current while drawing only a small current itself.

## BJT current equations

Kirchhoff's current law at the device gives:

$$
I_E = I_C + I_B
$$

The common-emitter current gain β (also written h<sub>FE</sub> for the DC value) and the common-base gain α are:

$$
\beta = \frac{I_C}{I_B} \qquad \alpha = \frac{I_C}{I_E} = \frac{\beta}{\beta + 1}
$$

β for small signal transistors is typically in the range of about 50 to 300, and it varies widely between devices of the same type and with temperature and collector current. Good designs therefore never depend on an exact value of β. α is slightly less than 1.

In the active region, the collector current depends exponentially on the base-emitter voltage:

$$
I_C = I_S \, e^{V_{BE}/V_T}
$$

where I<sub>S</sub> is the saturation current and V<sub>T</sub> is the thermal voltage, about 25.9 mV at 300 K. This exponential law is why V<sub>BE</sub> stays near 0.7 V over a wide range of currents: a tenfold change in I<sub>C</sub> changes V<sub>BE</sub> by only about 60 mV.

## Operating regions

| Region | BE junction | BC junction | Behaviour | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cutoff | Reverse or zero bias | Reverse bias | Almost no current flows | Switch OFF |
| Forward-active | Forward bias | Reverse bias | I<sub>C</sub> ≈ β I<sub>B</sub> | Amplifier |
| Saturation | Forward bias | Forward bias | V<sub>CE</sub> small, about 0.1 to 0.3 V | Switch ON |
| Reverse-active | Reverse bias | Forward bias | Low gain | Rarely used |

In saturation the collector current is limited by the external circuit, not by β. Logic families such as TTL use transistors switching between cutoff and saturation; the [logic gates](/electronics-engineering/logic-gates/) article shows the gate functions they build.

## Transistor configurations

- **Common emitter:** input at base, output at collector. Gives both voltage and current gain with phase inversion; the most common amplifier stage.
- **Common collector (emitter follower):** output at emitter. Voltage gain just below 1, high input resistance, low output resistance; used as a buffer.
- **Common base:** input at emitter. Current gain just below 1 but good high frequency response.

## Using a BJT as a switch

To switch a load such as a relay, LED string or small motor from a microcontroller pin, the transistor is driven hard into saturation. Horowitz and Hill recommend supplying more base current than the minimum I<sub>C</sub> / β, commonly designing for a ratio of collector to base current of about 10 (a "forced beta" of 10), so that the transistor stays saturated even with a low-gain device. An inductive load also needs a flyback diode across it.

## Advantages and limitations of the BJT

- **Advantages:** high transconductance for a given current, predictable V<sub>BE</sub>, low noise in many analog stages, simple to drive at low voltages.
- **Limitations:** needs continuous base current, which wastes power in switching; slower to turn off from saturation because stored charge must be removed; current gain varies with temperature and between devices; risk of thermal runaway in power stages without emitter resistance.

For switching at higher power and speed, MOSFETs are often preferred. The [CMOS inverter](/electronics-engineering/cmos-inverter/) article shows how MOSFETs build digital logic, and [TTL vs CMOS logic families](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/ttl-vs-cmos-logic-families/) on InstrumentationBlog.in compares the two technologies.
