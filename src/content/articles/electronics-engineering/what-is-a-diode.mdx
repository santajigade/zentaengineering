<Key title="Short answer">

What is a diode? A **diode** is a two-terminal semiconductor device, made from a **PN junction**, that lets current flow easily from **anode** to **cathode** and blocks it in the reverse direction. A silicon diode starts conducting at a forward voltage of roughly 0.6 to 0.7 V and blocks reverse voltage until its breakdown rating is reached.

</Key>

## What is a diode made of? The PN junction

A diode is formed by joining P-type semiconductor, which has an excess of holes, to N-type semiconductor, which has an excess of free electrons. At the junction, electrons and holes diffuse across and recombine. This leaves a thin **depletion region** with no free carriers, containing fixed charged ions. The ions create an electric field and a built-in potential barrier that stops further diffusion.

- **Forward bias:** making the P side (anode) positive with respect to the N side (cathode) reduces the barrier. Once the applied voltage approaches the barrier, carriers cross freely and current rises steeply.
- **Reverse bias:** making the cathode positive widens the depletion region. Only a very small leakage current flows, until the reverse voltage is high enough to cause breakdown.

## The diode equation and V-I characteristic

The current through an ideal junction diode follows the Shockley equation:

$$
I = I_S \left( e^{V / (n V_T)} - 1 \right)
$$

Here IS is the saturation current (very small, often nanoamperes or less), n is an ideality factor between about 1 and 2, and VT is the thermal voltage kT/q, about 26 mV at room temperature. Because of the exponential, the current increases about ten times for every 60 mV or so of extra forward voltage when n = 1. This is why the forward voltage changes only slightly over a wide range of currents, and why "0.7 V" is a useful approximation for silicon.

The forward voltage also falls with temperature, by roughly 2 mV per °C for a silicon diode at constant current. Diodes are sometimes used as simple temperature sensors for this reason.

## Main types of diode

| Type | Key feature | Typical forward voltage | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rectifier diode | High current and reverse voltage ratings | About 0.7 to 1.1 V | Power supply rectification |
| Fast and ultrafast recovery | Short reverse recovery time | About 1 V or more | Switching power supplies, inverters |
| Schottky diode | Metal to semiconductor junction, no minority carrier storage | About 0.15 to 0.45 V | Low-loss rectifiers, reverse polarity protection |
| Zener diode | Precise, stable reverse breakdown voltage | Operated in reverse | Voltage references and simple regulators |
| LED | Emits light when forward biased | About 1.8 V (red) to 3.3 V (blue, white) | Indicators, lighting |
| Photodiode | Current generated by incident light | Operated reverse biased | Light sensors, optical receivers |
| TVS diode | Fast clamping of voltage surges | Clamps above its rated voltage | Surge and ESD protection |
| Varactor | Junction capacitance varies with reverse voltage | Operated reverse biased | Voltage-controlled oscillators, tuning |

## Key datasheet ratings

- **VRRM, repetitive peak reverse voltage:** the maximum reverse voltage the diode can block repeatedly.
- **IF(AV), average forward current:** the maximum continuous average current under stated cooling conditions.
- **IFSM, surge forward current:** the maximum non-repetitive current for a short pulse, important at power-up when filter capacitors charge.
- **VF, forward voltage:** given at a stated current and temperature; sets the conduction loss P = VF × IF.
- **IR, reverse leakage current:** rises steeply with temperature, especially in Schottky diodes.
- **trr, reverse recovery time:** how long the diode takes to stop conducting when switched from forward to reverse; important in fast switching circuits.

Always choose a reverse voltage rating with margin above the highest voltage the circuit can produce, including spikes.

## Common diode circuits

### Rectifiers

A **half-wave [rectifier](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/precision-rectifier-op-amp-circuit/)** uses one diode and passes only one half of each AC cycle. A **bridge rectifier** uses four diodes to pass both halves, giving full-wave rectified DC with two diode drops in the current path. A smoothing capacitor after the rectifier stores charge to reduce ripple.

### Flyback (freewheeling) diode

A diode connected in reverse across an inductive load, such as a relay coil or solenoid, provides a path for the coil current when the driving transistor switches off. Without it, the collapsing magnetic field produces a high voltage spike that can destroy the transistor.

### Reverse polarity protection

A series diode in the supply line blocks current if the battery or supply is connected the wrong way round. A Schottky diode is often used to reduce the voltage lost in the protection.

### Clamping and protection

Diodes connected from a signal line to the supply rails clamp voltages that try to go above or below the rails. TVS diodes absorb fast surges on supply and data lines.

## Worked example: series resistor for an LED

An LED must have its current limited, normally by a [series resistor](/electrical-engineering/ohms-law-kirchhoffs-laws/). The resistor drops the difference between the supply voltage and the LED forward voltage:

$$
R = \frac{V_S - V_F}{I_F}
$$

## Key formulas for diodes

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diode current (Shockley equation) | I = IS × (e^(V / (n VT)) − 1) | IS = saturation current in A, n = ideality factor (about 1 to 2), V = junction voltage in V |
| Thermal voltage | VT = kT / q | k = Boltzmann constant, T = temperature in K, q = electron charge; about 26 mV at room temperature |
| Conduction loss | P = VF × IF | VF = forward voltage in V, IF = forward current in A, P in W |
| LED series resistor | R = (VS − VF) / IF | VS = supply voltage in V, R in Ω |
| Resistor power | P = I² × R | I in A, R in Ω, P in W |

## Reverse recovery

When a conducting PN junction diode is suddenly reverse biased, charge stored in the junction must be removed before it can block. For a short time, called the reverse recovery time trr, current flows in the reverse direction. In switching power supplies this causes extra loss and electrical noise, which is why fast recovery, ultrafast or Schottky diodes are used at high switching frequencies.

## Testing a diode

Most digital multimeters have a diode test mode that passes a small current and displays the forward voltage. A healthy silicon diode reads roughly 0.5 to 0.7 V in the forward direction and shows an open circuit in reverse. A reading near zero in both directions indicates a shorted diode; an open reading in both directions indicates an open diode. Test diodes out of circuit, because parallel components can give misleading readings.

## Diode interview and viva questions

1. **What is the depletion region of a PN junction?** It is the thin layer at the junction left without free carriers after electrons and holes diffuse across and recombine. Its fixed charged ions create the electric field and built-in potential barrier that stop further diffusion.
2. **Why is 0.7 V used as the forward voltage of a silicon diode?** Diode current rises exponentially with voltage, about ten times for every 60 mV or so when n = 1, so over a wide range of currents the forward voltage of a small silicon diode stays near 0.6 to 0.7 V.
3. **What is reverse recovery time?** It is the short time, trr, for which a conducting PN junction diode carries reverse current after being reverse biased, while its stored charge is removed. It causes extra loss and noise in switching power supplies.
4. **Why are Schottky diodes used for low-loss rectifiers?** A Schottky diode uses a metal to semiconductor junction with no minority carrier storage, so it has a low forward voltage of about 0.15 to 0.45 V and switches quickly. Its reverse leakage rises steeply with temperature.
5. **How do you test a diode with a multimeter?** In diode test mode, a healthy silicon diode reads roughly 0.5 to 0.7 V forward and open circuit in reverse. A reading near zero both ways means a shorted diode, and open both ways means an open diode.
