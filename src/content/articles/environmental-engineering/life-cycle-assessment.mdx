<Key title="Short answer">

**Life cycle assessment** (LCA) measures the environmental impacts of a product, process or service over its whole life, from raw material extraction through manufacturing, transport and use to end of life. Under ISO 14040 and 14044 it has four phases: goal and scope definition, life cycle inventory, life cycle impact assessment, and interpretation of the results.

</Key>

A product that looks green at the point of use may cause large impacts elsewhere: in mining, energy intensive production, long supply chains or disposal. LCA gives a complete, quantitative picture so that engineers and decision makers can compare options fairly, find hotspots and avoid simply moving a problem from one stage or place to another.

### Phase 1: goal and scope definition

- **Goal:** why the study is done, for whom, and whether results will be used for public comparative claims (which need critical review).
- **Functional unit:** the quantified function on which all comparisons are based, for example "lighting 10 m² at 500 lux for 10 years" or "transporting one tonne of goods 1 km". Comparing per product rather than per function can mislead.
- **System boundary:** which life cycle stages and processes are included: cradle to gate, cradle to grave or cradle to cradle.
- **Allocation rules** for shared processes and recycling, **cut-off criteria**, data quality requirements and chosen impact categories.

### Phase 2: life cycle inventory (LCI)

All flows crossing the system boundary are quantified per functional unit: raw materials, energy, water, land use, and emissions to air, water and soil, plus waste. **Foreground data** for processes the study owner controls are collected directly (meter readings, bills of materials, supplier data). **Background data** for electricity, materials and transport come from databases such as ecoinvent or national inventories. Building the inventory is usually the most time consuming phase, similar in spirit to a detailed [material balance](/chemical-engineering/material-balance/).

### Phase 3: life cycle impact assessment (LCIA)

Inventory flows are assigned to **impact categories** and multiplied by **characterisation factors**:

$$
I_c = \sum_i m_i \times CF_{i,c}
$$

where m_i is the mass of substance i emitted and CF its characterisation factor for category c. For **climate change**, factors are global warming potentials (GWP100): CO₂ = 1, methane about 27 to 30 and nitrous oxide about 273 kg CO₂e per kg (IPCC AR6).

Common categories include climate change, ozone depletion, acidification, eutrophication, photochemical smog, particulate matter, human and ecotoxicity, water use, land use and resource depletion. Methods such as ReCiPe and the EU Environmental Footprint can also normalise and weight results into single scores, which is optional and value based.

### Phase 4: interpretation

Results are checked for completeness, consistency and sensitivity to key assumptions, such as electricity mix, product lifetime or end of life route. **Hotspots** are identified, conclusions and limitations stated, and recommendations made.

## Key formulas for life cycle assessment

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Category indicator result | I_c = Σ m_i × CF_i,c | m_i = mass of substance i in kg, CF_i,c = characterisation factor, for climate change in kg CO₂e per kg |
| Use phase emissions | E_use = P × t × EF | P = power in kW, t = time in h, EF = grid emission factor in kg CO₂e per kWh |
| Allocated manufacturing impact | E_mfg = E_unit × t ÷ L | E_unit = impact per item, t = hours in the functional unit, L = rated life in h |

## Types and uses of LCA

- **Attributional LCA:** describes the impacts attributable to a product system as it is.
- **Consequential LCA:** estimates how impacts change in the wider economy as a result of a decision.
- **Streamlined or screening LCA:** quick, lower data versions for early design choices.
- **Social LCA and life cycle costing** extend the approach to social and economic aspects.

Applications include eco-design, choosing materials and suppliers, comparing technologies such as [solar PV systems](/renewable-energy/how-solar-pv-systems-work/) against fossil generation, Environmental Product Declarations, carbon footprint labels and policy making.

## LCA software and data

Practitioners use dedicated software such as SimaPro, GaBi (now LCA for Experts), openLCA and Brightway to model product systems and link them to inventory databases. Data quality is assessed for time, geography and technology relevance, and uncertainty can be explored with Monte Carlo analysis. For engineering firms, a practical approach is to start with a screening LCA in early design to find hotspots, then refine the model with primary supplier and process data for the options that matter most. Reporting should state the functional unit, boundary, data sources, allocation choices and limitations clearly.

## Life cycle assessment in buildings and infrastructure

In construction, LCA covers embodied carbon in materials such as cement, steel and glass as well as operational energy over decades. Standards like EN 15978 define the life cycle modules, and many green building rating systems now award credits for whole building LCA and EPDs.

## Common pitfalls in life cycle assessment

- Using an unfair functional unit or system boundary.
- Ignoring the use phase or end of life when they dominate.
- Over-relying on generic database data for key processes.
- Reporting a single score without showing trade-offs between categories.
- Drawing comparative public claims without critical review.

LCA is the quantitative backbone of [circular economy principles](/environmental-engineering/circular-economy-principles/) and complements site specific [environmental impact assessment](/environmental-engineering/environmental-impact-assessment/). Used well, it helps engineers make choices that genuinely reduce environmental impact rather than just moving it.

## Life cycle assessment interview and viva questions

1. **What are the four phases of LCA under ISO 14040?** Goal and scope definition, life cycle inventory, life cycle impact assessment and interpretation, carried out iteratively.
2. **What is a functional unit?** It is the quantified function on which all comparisons are based, such as lighting a given area at a given level for a set time.
3. **What is the difference between cradle to gate and cradle to grave?** Cradle to gate stops when the product leaves the factory, while cradle to grave also includes use and end of life.
4. **How is climate change impact calculated in LCIA?** Each greenhouse gas mass is multiplied by its global warming potential and the results are summed in kg CO₂e.
5. **What is the difference between attributional and consequential LCA?** Attributional LCA describes the impacts of a product system as it is, while consequential LCA estimates how impacts change in the wider economy because of a decision.
