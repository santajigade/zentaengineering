<Key title="Short answer">

A conventional **water treatment process** turns raw surface water into safe drinking water in a sequence of steps: **intake and screening** to remove debris, **coagulation** with chemicals such as alum to destabilise fine particles, **flocculation** to grow them into larger flocs, **sedimentation** (clarification) to settle the flocs, **filtration** through sand or membranes to remove remaining particles and pathogens, and **disinfection**, usually with chlorine, to kill microorganisms and leave a protective residual. Extra steps such as aeration, softening, activated carbon or pH correction are added for specific problems.

</Key>

Rivers, lakes and reservoirs contain silt, algae, bacteria, viruses, organic matter and sometimes chemicals. A treatment plant removes these to meet drinking water standards such as the WHO Guidelines or national standards like IS 10500. The multiple barrier approach, where several steps each remove contaminants, protects public health even if one step underperforms.

## 1. Intake, screening and pre-treatment

Water is drawn from a river, reservoir or well. **Bar screens** and **fine screens** remove leaves, fish, plastics and floating debris. Some plants add:

- **Pre-sedimentation** basins to settle heavy silt in monsoon conditions.
- **Aeration** to remove dissolved gases such as hydrogen sulphide and carbon dioxide, and to oxidise iron and manganese so they can be removed as solids.
- **Pre-chlorination or pre-ozonation** to control algae and oxidise contaminants, used carefully because of disinfection by-products.

## 2. Coagulation

Most turbidity comes from tiny colloidal particles of clay, silt and organic matter, often under 1 µm. They carry negative surface charges that repel each other, so they stay suspended indefinitely. A **coagulant** neutralises these charges:

- **Aluminium sulphate (alum)**, the most common.
- **Ferric chloride** or **ferric sulphate**.
- **Polyaluminium chloride (PAC)**, effective over a wider pH range and at lower temperatures.

The coagulant is added in a **rapid mix** tank or inline mixer with intense mixing for a few seconds to a minute, so it disperses quickly through all the water. The optimum chemical and dose are found with **jar tests** and depend on raw water turbidity, pH, alkalinity and temperature.

## 3. Flocculation

Destabilised particles collide and stick together to form visible **flocs**. Flocculation tanks use **gentle mixing** from slow paddles or baffles over about 20 to 40 minutes, so particles meet without breaking the fragile flocs. **Polymer flocculant aids** can strengthen flocs. Mixing intensity is expressed by the velocity gradient G, typically about 20 to 80 s⁻¹ in flocculators.

## 4. Sedimentation (clarification)

Water flows slowly through large tanks where the heavy flocs settle under gravity, typically over 2 to 4 hours in conventional basins. Settled **sludge** is removed from the bottom by scrapers. Design is based on the **surface overflow rate**:

$$
SOR = \frac{Q}{A}
$$

where Q is flow and A is the tank surface area. Typical values are about 1 to 2.5 m/h for conventional basins. **Tube or plate settlers** and **dissolved air flotation** (for light algal flocs) allow much more compact clarifiers.

## 5. Filtration

**Rapid sand filters** or **dual media filters** (anthracite over sand) remove the remaining fine flocs, particles and many microorganisms, bringing turbidity below about 0.3 to 1 NTU. Typical filtration rates are 5 to 15 m/h. As the bed clogs, head loss rises, and the filter is cleaned by **backwashing** with water and air. **Slow sand filters** rely on a biological layer and suit small communities. **Membrane filtration** (microfiltration and ultrafiltration) provides a physical barrier against bacteria and protozoa such as Cryptosporidium.

## 6. Disinfection

Disinfection inactivates bacteria, viruses and protozoa:

- **Chlorine** (gas or sodium hypochlorite) is cheap and leaves a **residual** that protects the distribution network. Effectiveness depends on concentration and contact time, expressed as **CT** (mg·min/L).
- **Chloramine** gives a longer lasting residual with fewer by-products.
- **Ultraviolet (UV)** light is highly effective against Cryptosporidium and Giardia, with no chemicals but no residual.
- **Ozone** is a strong oxidant and disinfectant that also improves taste and odour.

A typical target is a free chlorine residual of about 0.2 to 0.5 mg/L at the consumer's tap.

## Additional treatment steps

- **Softening** with lime or ion exchange to reduce hardness.
- **Activated carbon** (powdered or granular) to remove taste, odour, pesticides and organic micropollutants.
- **Iron and manganese removal** by oxidation and filtration.
- **Fluoride and arsenic removal** with activated alumina or specialised adsorbents where groundwater is affected.
- **Reverse osmosis** for desalination of seawater and brackish water.
- **pH and corrosion control** to protect pipes and prevent lead or copper leaching.

## Groundwater treatment

Groundwater is usually clear and free of most pathogens, so treatment is often simpler: aeration and filtration for iron and manganese, softening where hardness is high, removal of specific contaminants such as arsenic, fluoride or nitrate, and disinfection.

## Sludge and residuals

Sedimentation sludge and filter backwash water contain the removed solids and coagulant. They are thickened, dewatered with belt presses, centrifuges or drying beds, and disposed of or reused, while recovered water is often recycled to the plant inlet.

## Monitoring and control of the water treatment process

Plants monitor turbidity, pH, chlorine residual, flow and levels continuously, with laboratory tests for bacteria, metals and other parameters. Accurate [flow measurement](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/flow-measurement/) paces chemical dosing pumps to the plant flow, and level measurement controls filter levels and storage tanks. Automation through [SCADA systems](/automation-robotics/scada-system-architecture/) helps operators respond quickly to changes in raw water quality.

Used water returning from homes and industry needs a different set of processes, described in the [activated sludge process](/environmental-engineering/activated-sludge-process/).
