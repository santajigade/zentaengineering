<Key title="Short answer">

An **electrostatic precipitator** (ESP) removes dust and smoke from gas streams using electric forces. A high negative DC voltage of 30 to 100 kV on discharge electrodes creates a corona that charges the particles. The charged particles migrate to grounded collection plates, and rappers knock the dust into hoppers. Removal is commonly 99 to 99.9% with very low pressure drop.

</Key>

ESPs clean flue gas at coal and biomass power plants, cement kilns, steel works, pulp mills, smelters and incinerators. They can treat millions of cubic metres of hot gas per hour while adding only a few hundred pascals of pressure drop, which saves a great deal of fan power compared with other collectors.

1. **Corona generation:** a transformer rectifier set supplies high voltage DC to thin discharge electrodes (wires, rigid spiked tubes or frames). Near their sharp surfaces the field is strong enough to ionise gas molecules, creating a glowing **corona** and producing free electrons.
2. **Particle charging:** electrons attach to gas molecules such as oxygen, forming negative ions that drift towards the plates. They collide with and attach to dust particles. Larger particles (above about 1 µm) are charged mainly by **field charging**; very fine ones by **diffusion charging**.
3. **Migration and collection:** the electric field pulls the charged particles sideways across the gas flow to the grounded plates, where they lose most of their charge and build up a dust layer.
4. **Removal:** mechanical or pneumatic **rappers** strike the plates and electrodes at intervals, so the layer slides into **hoppers** in sheets, minimising re-entrainment. Dust is conveyed away for disposal or reuse, for example fly ash for [cement](/civil-engineering/types-of-cement/).

## Main components

- **Casing** with inlet and outlet transitions and **gas distribution screens** for even flow across the passages.
- **Collection plates:** vertical steel plates spaced about 300 to 400 mm apart, forming many parallel gas passages.
- **Discharge electrode system** suspended on insulators.
- **Transformer rectifier (TR) sets** with automatic voltage controllers, one per field. Modern **high frequency switched mode** power supplies give smoother voltage and higher collection.
- **Rappers**, **hoppers** with heaters and level detectors, and **insulator compartments** kept warm and clean to avoid tracking.

Large ESPs have three to five **fields** in series along the gas flow, each with its own power supply, so that a fault in one field does not stop collection.

## The Deutsch-Anderson equation

Collection efficiency η is estimated by the Deutsch-Anderson equation:

$$
\eta = 1 - e^{-wA/Q}
$$

where w is the effective particle **migration velocity**, A the total collection plate area and Q the gas flow rate. The ratio A/Q is the **specific collection area (SCA)**. Typical w values are about 0.02 to 0.2 m/s, depending on particle size, dust resistivity and electrical conditions. The exponential form shows that each extra percent of efficiency at high levels needs a disproportionate increase in plate area.

<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Deutsch-Anderson equation valid; uniform gas distribution.</dd>

<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>About 19 900 m² for 99.5%, rising by about 30% to 25 900 m² for 99.9%</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>The effective migration velocity comes from pilot tests or experience with similar dust; sneakage and rapping losses reduce real efficiency.</dd>

## Dust resistivity

The electrical resistivity of the collected dust strongly affects performance:

- **Low resistivity** (below about 10⁸ Ω·cm), such as carbon rich dust: particles lose charge instantly on the plate and can be re-entrained.
- **Ideal range** (about 10⁸ to 10¹¹ Ω·cm): good collection.
- **High resistivity** (above about 10¹¹ Ω·cm), such as fly ash from low sulphur coal: charge builds up in the dust layer, causing **back corona**, which emits positive ions and sharply reduces efficiency.

Remedies include **flue gas conditioning** with SO₃ or ammonia, changing gas temperature, humidification, intermittent energisation, or switching to a fabric filter.

## Types of electrostatic precipitators

- **Dry plate type:** the most common, for large industrial gas flows.
- **Wet ESP:** plates are washed with water, collecting sticky particles, acid mists, and very fine particles with no resistivity problems. Used after wet scrubbers and in sulphuric acid plants.
- **Tubular ESP:** discharge wires in the centre of round or hexagonal tubes, often wet type.
- **Two stage ESP:** separate charging and collection sections, used in indoor air cleaners and oil mist collectors.

## Operation and maintenance

Operators watch secondary voltage and current on each field, spark rate, rapping performance, hopper levels and opacity. Rising spark rates or falling current can show electrode misalignment, broken wires, dust build up or air leakage. Hoppers must be emptied reliably: overfilled hoppers can short the electrodes and even distort plates. Hopper heaters prevent dust from caking at low temperatures. During outages, inspections check plate alignment, electrode condition, insulator cleanliness and rapper function. Gas distribution tests ensure even velocity across the inlet face, because one fast lane can let dust bypass collection.

## Advantages and limitations of an electrostatic precipitator

**Advantages:** very high efficiency, low pressure drop and fan power, handles high temperatures (up to about 400 °C or more) and large flows, long life, low operating cost.

**Limitations:** high capital cost and large size, sensitivity to dust resistivity and gas conditions, not suitable for explosive gases without special design, and efficiency loss if electrical fields trip.

Performance is confirmed by continuous particulate monitors that form part of a [continuous emissions monitoring system](/environmental-engineering/cems-overview/), and ESP design is a core part of meeting the limits set during [environmental impact assessment](/environmental-engineering/environmental-impact-assessment/) and plant permitting.

## Electrostatic precipitator interview and viva questions

1. **What is the working principle of an electrostatic precipitator?** A corona at high voltage discharge electrodes charges the dust particles, and the electric field drives them to grounded collection plates, from which rappers drop the dust into hoppers.
2. **Why is negative corona normally used?** Negative corona gives a higher sparkover voltage and more stable operation in flue gas, so more voltage and current can be applied before sparking.
3. **What does the Deutsch-Anderson equation show about plate area?** Efficiency rises exponentially with wA/Q, so each extra percent at high efficiency needs a disproportionate increase in collection area.
4. **What is back corona?** With high resistivity dust, charge builds up in the dust layer on the plates until it emits positive ions, which sharply reduces collection efficiency.
5. **How does a wet ESP differ from a dry ESP?** A wet ESP washes its plates with water, so it can collect sticky particles, acid mists and very fine particles without resistivity problems.
