<Key title="Short answer">

In **router vs switch** comparisons, a **switch** connects devices **within one network** (LAN) and forwards frames by **MAC address** at layer 2 of the OSI model. A **router** connects **different networks** and forwards packets by **IP address** at layer 3, choosing a path using a routing table. Switches build local networks; routers link those networks to each other and to the internet.

</Key>

Every network, from a home to a factory, uses both. Knowing what each device does, and where its job ends, is essential for designing, segmenting and troubleshooting networks. The layers involved are explained in [OSI vs TCP/IP model](/telecommunication-engineering/osi-vs-tcp-ip-model/).

## What a switch does

A switch has many Ethernet ports and connects PCs, PLCs, cameras, servers and access points in a local area network.

- **MAC learning:** when a frame arrives, the switch records the source MAC address and the port it came in on, building a **MAC address table**.
- **Forwarding:** it looks up the destination MAC address and sends the frame only out of the matching port.
- **Flooding:** if the destination is unknown, or the frame is a broadcast, it sends the frame out of all ports except the one it arrived on.
- **Full duplex:** each port sends and receives at the same time without collisions.

All devices on a switch, without VLANs, are in the same **broadcast domain**: a broadcast such as an ARP request reaches every one of them.

### VLANs

A **virtual LAN** (IEEE 802.1Q) splits one physical switch into several logical networks. Ports are assigned to VLANs, and traffic between VLANs needs a router. VLANs are widely used to separate office, control system, CCTV and guest traffic for security and performance. **Managed switches** support VLANs, port mirroring, quality of service and monitoring; **unmanaged switches** simply forward frames.

## What a router does

A router connects two or more IP networks, such as a plant LAN and the internet, or several subnets inside a company.

- **Routing table:** a list of destination networks and the next hop or interface to reach each. Routes are configured manually (**static routing**) or learned from neighbouring routers using protocols such as OSPF and BGP (**dynamic routing**).
- **Forwarding:** for each packet, the router reads the destination IP address, finds the most specific matching route, decrements the time to live, and sends the packet on in a new layer 2 frame.
- **Boundary:** a router does not forward broadcasts, so each interface is a separate broadcast domain.
- **Edge functions:** network address translation (NAT), [firewall](/telecommunication-engineering/firewalls-explained/) rules, VPN termination and DHCP.

## Router vs switch: comparison

| Feature | Switch | Router |
| --- | --- | --- |
| OSI layer | Layer 2 (data link) | Layer 3 (network) |
| Address used | MAC address | IP address |
| Table | MAC address table | Routing table |
| Connects | Devices within one LAN | Different networks (LANs, WAN, internet) |
| Broadcasts | Forwarded within the VLAN | Not forwarded; separates broadcast domains |
| Typical ports | 8 to 48 Ethernet ports | Few LAN ports plus WAN interfaces |
| Extra functions | VLANs, PoE, QoS, port mirroring | NAT, firewall, VPN, DHCP, dynamic routing |
| Speed | Hardware forwarding at line rate | Slower per port, more processing per packet |

## Layer 3 switches

A **layer 3 switch** is a switch with built in routing between VLANs, done in hardware at full speed. It is common in the core of enterprise and plant networks to route between internal subnets, while a router or firewall at the edge handles the internet connection, NAT and security policy.

## Redundancy and loops

Switched networks are often built with redundant links so a single cable fault does not cut off an area. Redundant links create loops, and a loop would make broadcast frames circulate forever. The **Spanning Tree Protocol** (STP, and the faster RSTP) blocks redundant paths and reopens them within seconds if the active path fails. Industrial rings use faster protocols such as MRP or vendor ring protocols that recover in tens of milliseconds. Routers handle redundancy differently: they run routing protocols that find alternative paths, and gateway redundancy protocols such as VRRP let two routers share one virtual gateway address, so hosts keep working if one router fails.

## Choosing and placing devices

- Use **switches** to connect devices in each area, with enough ports and speed (1 Gbit/s or more for uplinks).
- Use **managed switches** where you need VLANs, monitoring or redundancy protocols such as RSTP or industrial ring protocols.
- Use **PoE switches** to power cameras, access points and some sensors over the Ethernet cable.
- Use a **router or firewall** at every boundary between trust zones, such as between the control network and the business network, and at the internet edge.
- Choose **industrial grade switches** in plants for wide temperature ranges, DIN rail mounting and redundant power.

## Industrial networks

In automation systems, segmentation with VLANs and routed zones limits the spread of faults and cyber attacks, following the zones and conduits approach of IEC 62443. Many plants use managed industrial Ethernet switches in a ring at the control level, connected through a firewall to the plant IT network. See [SCADA system architecture](/automation-robotics/scada-system-architecture/) and the [industrial communication articles on Instrumentation Blog](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/automation/communication/) for more.

In short, switches make local networks fast and efficient, while routers give them structure, connect them to the wider world and enforce boundaries between them.
