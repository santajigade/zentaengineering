<Key title="Short answer">

**Bluetooth vs WiFi vs Zigbee** compares three short range radios. Bluetooth links personal devices at 1 to 3 Mbit/s in 2.4 GHz. Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11) is the wireless LAN, using 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz for high throughput through an access point. Zigbee builds low power mesh networks on IEEE 802.15.4 at 250 kbit/s.

</Key>

You face Bluetooth vs WiFi vs Zigbee in almost any smart home, wearable or factory sensor project. All three can run in the 2.4 GHz ISM band (2400 to 2483.5 MHz), but each was designed for a different job. Bluetooth replaces the cable between a phone and its accessories, Wi-Fi extends an IP network to laptops and phones, and Zigbee connects many small battery powered devices that send a few bytes at a time. Each standard fixes the data rate, topology and power budget, so start from what the device has to do.

## Differences explained

### Radio and data rate

Bluetooth Classic (BR/EDR) hops across 79 channels of 1 MHz at 1600 hops per second. Basic rate uses GFSK at 1 Mbit/s, and Enhanced Data Rate adds π/4-DQPSK at 2 Mbit/s and 8DPSK at 3 Mbit/s. Bluetooth Low Energy uses 40 channels of 2 MHz, three of which are primary advertising channels. Since version 5.0 it offers the LE 1M and LE 2M PHYs and the LE Coded PHY at 500 or 125 kbit/s, which trades rate for range.

Wi-Fi uses OFDM on channels of 20 MHz or wider, with higher order QAM and several spatial streams, so its throughput is far above the other two. Its standards, bands and generations are covered in [Wi-Fi standards and bands](/telecommunication-engineering/wi-fi-standards-and-bands/), and the modulation schemes in [modulation techniques](/telecommunication-engineering/modulation-techniques/).

The IEEE 802.15.4 2.4 GHz PHY that Zigbee uses sends O-QPSK at 62.5 ksymbol/s with 4 bits per symbol, which gives 250 kbit/s. Its 16 channels sit at 2405 + 5(k − 11) MHz for channel numbers k = 11 to 26. A PHY frame carries at most 127 bytes, so Zigbee suits short messages, not streams.

### Topology

A Bluetooth Classic piconet has one central device that sets the hopping sequence and timing, with up to seven active peripherals. Bluetooth LE links are also central to peripheral, and the Bluetooth SIG publishes a separate Mesh Protocol specification for many node networks built on LE.

Wi-Fi normally works as an infrastructure basic service set: every station associates with one access point, which bridges the radio to the wired LAN. How that LAN forwards frames is explained in [router vs switch](/telecommunication-engineering/router-vs-switch/).

Zigbee is built for mesh. One coordinator forms the network, routers relay traffic for others, and end devices talk only to their parent router, so they can sleep most of the time. If a router fails, the network finds another route. That is why Zigbee is used for lighting and building systems spread across many rooms.

### Power

Power use follows how long the radio stays on. Wi-Fi radios move a lot of data quickly but usually stay associated with the access point, so they draw more average power; target wake time in 802.11ax lets IoT clients schedule when they wake. Bluetooth LE and Zigbee end devices sleep between short transmissions, which lets small sensors run from coin cells. Bluetooth Classic defines power classes of 100 mW (Class 1), 2.5 mW (Class 2) and 1 mW (Class 3).

### Coexistence in 2.4 GHz

All three share 2.4 GHz with microwave ovens and other devices. Bluetooth uses adaptive frequency hopping to avoid busy channels. Wi-Fi channels 1, 6 and 11 are centred at 2412, 2437 and 2462 MHz and are about 20 MHz wide, so Zigbee channels 15 (2425 MHz), 20 (2450 MHz), 25 (2475 MHz) and 26 (2480 MHz) fall in the gaps between them. Pick one of those when Zigbee and Wi-Fi share a building.

## Key formulas for Bluetooth, Wi-Fi and Zigbee links

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Free space path loss | FSPL = 20 log10(4πdf ÷ c) | d in m, f in Hz, c = 3 × 10^8 m/s, FSPL in dB |
| 802.15.4 channel centre (2.4 GHz) | f_c = 2405 + 5(k − 11) | f_c in MHz, k = 11 to 26 |
| 802.15.4 bit rate | R_b = R_s × bits per symbol | 62.5 ksymbol/s × 4 bit = 250 kbit/s |
| Minimum transfer time | t = 8N ÷ R | N in bytes, R in bit/s, t in s |

Free space loss comes from the Friis equation in Rappaport:

$$
\text{FSPL} = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d f}{c}\right)\ \text{dB}
$$

## When to use which

Use Bluetooth for headsets, speakers, fitness bands, medical wearables and any accessory paired to a phone, because every phone already has the radio. Bluetooth LE also suits beacons and battery sensors that one phone or gateway reads.

Use Wi-Fi for anything that needs IP and real throughput, such as laptops, cameras, displays and HMI tablets. It works through existing access points and reaches the internet directly, but draws more power.

Use Zigbee for many low rate nodes spread over a building, such as lights, switches, door sensors and thermostats, where a self healing mesh and long battery life matter more than speed. A gateway connects it to the IP network.

Many products use more than one. A smart home hub often has Wi-Fi or Ethernet towards the router and a Zigbee radio towards the devices, while a phone sets it up over Bluetooth LE. For wired field networks, see [Modbus RTU vs TCP](/automation-robotics/modbus-rtu-vs-tcp/).

## Bluetooth vs Wi-Fi vs Zigbee interview and viva questions

1. **Which standard defines the Zigbee physical layer?** IEEE 802.15.4 defines the PHY and MAC. The Connectivity Standards Alliance Zigbee specification adds the network, security and application layers on top.
2. **How is the 250 kbit/s rate of 802.15.4 at 2.4 GHz obtained?** The O-QPSK PHY sends 62.5 ksymbol/s and each symbol carries 4 bits, so 62.5 × 4 = 250 kbit/s.
3. **What is a Bluetooth piconet?** It is a network of one central device and up to seven active peripherals in Bluetooth Classic, all following the hopping sequence and clock of the central.
4. **Why can Zigbee and Wi-Fi share 2.4 GHz?** Zigbee channels 15, 20, 25 and 26 fall in the gaps between Wi-Fi channels 1, 6 and 11, and both use listen before talk access with retries.
5. **Why does path loss rise when Wi-Fi moves from 2.4 GHz to 5 GHz?** Free space path loss grows with 20 log10(f), so at the same distance the 5.5 GHz link loses about 7 dB more than a 2.44 GHz link.
