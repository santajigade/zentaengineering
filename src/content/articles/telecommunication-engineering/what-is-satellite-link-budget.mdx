<Key title="Short answer">

A **satellite link budget** is a decibel accounting of every gain and loss between transmitter and receiver. Start from the transmitter EIRP, subtract free space path loss and atmospheric losses, add the receive antenna's figure of merit G/T, and get the carrier to noise ratio. The margin above the modem's required value shows whether the link works.

</Key>

A satellite link budget answers one practical question: will enough signal reach the receiver, compared with the noise, for the modem to decode it? A geostationary satellite is more than 35 786 km away, so the received carrier is extremely weak. Engineers therefore list every gain and loss in decibels and add them up, one line at a time, the same way an accountant balances a budget.

## What a satellite link budget includes

Uplink or downlink, a link budget has the same parts:

- Transmit side: transmitter power Pt, feed and waveguide losses, and transmit antenna gain Gt. Together they give the **EIRP** (effective isotropic radiated power).
- Path: free space path loss Lp, which is by far the largest term, plus atmospheric gas absorption, rain attenuation and small losses such as antenna pointing error and polarisation mismatch.
- Receive side: receive antenna gain Gr and the system noise temperature Tsys, combined into the figure of merit **G/T**.
- Noise and data rate: Boltzmann's constant, the noise bandwidth B and the bit rate Rb turn the received carrier into C/N0, C/N and Eb/N0.
- Required value and margin: the modem needs a minimum C/N or Eb/N0 for its modulation and coding. The difference between what arrives and what is needed is the **link margin**.

Working in decibels turns all the multiplications into additions. If decibel arithmetic is new to you, this [guide to dB and dBm](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/decibel-in-electronics-db-dbm/) covers the basics; here dBW means decibels relative to 1 W.

## EIRP: transmitter power plus antenna gain

$$
EIRP = P_t + G_t - L_t \quad \text{(dBW)}
$$

where Pt is transmitter output in dBW, Gt the transmit antenna gain in dBi and Lt the feed loss in dB. A parabolic dish of diameter D has gain

$$
G = \eta \left( \frac{\pi D}{\lambda} \right)^2
$$

with η the aperture efficiency and λ the wavelength. Gain rises with the square of both diameter and frequency, which is why small Ku band dishes can do the work of much larger C band ones. The same relationship is covered in [antenna gain and directivity](/electronics-engineering/antenna-gain-and-directivity/). Satellite operators publish EIRP contour maps, so a ground station designer usually reads the downlink EIRP for their location straight from the map.

## Free space path loss

Free space path loss is the spreading of power over a growing sphere, expressed for two isotropic antennas (ITU-R P.525):

$$
L_p = \left( \frac{4 \pi d}{\lambda} \right)^2
$$

In decibels, with d in km and f in GHz:

$$
L_p\,(\text{dB}) = 20\log_{10} d + 20\log_{10} f + 92.45
$$

For a geostationary satellite the distance is 35 786 km for a station directly below it and about 41 679 km for a station at 0° elevation, so the slant range depends on where the earth station is. At Ku band frequencies the path loss is about 205 to 206 dB. Because loss rises 20 dB per decade of distance, a GEO link loses about 36 dB more than a LEO link at 550 km at the same frequency, as compared in [GEO vs MEO vs LEO satellites](/telecommunication-engineering/geo-meo-leo-satellites/).

## Atmospheric and rain losses

The atmosphere adds loss on top of free space loss. Oxygen and water vapour absorb a small amount (ITU-R P.676). Rain is the big variable above about 10 GHz: drops absorb and scatter the signal, so Ku and especially Ka band links suffer **rain fade**. ITU-R P.618 predicts the attenuation exceeded for a given percentage of the year from local rain rate, elevation angle, frequency and polarisation. That prediction sets the margin the link budget must hold back. Rain also raises the antenna noise temperature, so the carrier falls and the noise rises at the same time.

## G/T and the noise side

The receive station is judged by G/T:

$$
G/T = G_r - 10\log_{10} T_{sys} \quad \text{(dB/K)}
$$

Tsys combines the antenna noise temperature (sky, ground and rain seen by the dish) with the noise added by the LNB and the cable. Noise power in bandwidth B is N = kTB, where k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K, which is −228.6 dBW/K/Hz.

## Carrier to noise ratio and margin

The full chain, in decibels:

$$
C/N_0 = EIRP - L_p - L_a + G/T - k \quad \text{(dBHz)}
$$

$$
C/N = C/N_0 - 10\log_{10} B, \qquad E_b/N_0 = C/N_0 - 10\log_{10} R_b
$$

Margin = received C/N (or Eb/N0) minus the value the modem needs. The required value depends on the [modulation](/telecommunication-engineering/modulation-techniques/) and error correction code: higher order schemes carry more bits per hertz but need a higher C/N. A complete system has an uplink and a downlink, and their noise adds, so the overall C/N is always a little worse than the weaker of the two.

## Key formulas for satellite link budget

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EIRP | EIRP = Pt + Gt − Lt | dBW; Pt in dBW, Gt in dBi, Lt in dB |
| Dish gain | G = η(πD/λ)² | η = efficiency, D = diameter (m), λ = wavelength (m) |
| Free space path loss | Lp = 20 log d + 20 log f + 92.45 | dB; d in km, f in GHz |
| Figure of merit | G/T = Gr − 10 log Tsys | dB/K; Tsys in K |
| Noise power | N = kTB | k = −228.6 dBW/K/Hz, T in K, B in Hz |
| Carrier to noise density | C/N0 = EIRP − Lp − La + G/T − k | dBHz |
| Carrier to noise ratio | C/N = C/N0 − 10 log B | dB |
| Energy per bit to noise density | Eb/N0 = C/N0 − 10 log Rb | dB; Rb in bit/s |

## Worked example: Ku band GEO downlink

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>Downlink frequency 12 GHz, slant range 38 000 km, satellite EIRP 50 dBW toward the station, receive dish 1.2 m diameter with aperture efficiency 0.6, system noise temperature 150 K, atmospheric loss 0.5 dB, transponder bandwidth 36 MHz.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>Path loss, dish gain, G/T, C/N0, C/N and the clear sky margin if the modem needs C/N of 10 dB.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Clear sky, no pointing or polarisation loss, downlink only (uplink noise ignored), the 10 dB threshold is assumed for illustration.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>λ = c ÷ f = 3 × 10⁸ ÷ 12 × 10⁹ = 0.025 m<br />Lp = 20 log 38 000 + 20 log 12 + 92.45 = 91.6 + 21.6 + 92.45 = 205.6 dB<br />Gr = 10 log [0.6 × (π × 1.2 ÷ 0.025)²] = 41.4 dBi<br />G/T = 41.4 − 10 log 150 = 41.4 − 21.8 = 19.6 dB/K<br />C/N0 = 50 − 205.6 − 0.5 + 19.6 + 228.6 = 92.1 dBHz<br />C/N = 92.1 − 10 log (36 × 10⁶) = 92.1 − 75.6 = 16.5 dB<br />Margin = 16.5 − 10 = 6.5 dB</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>C/N = 16.5 dB in clear sky, leaving a margin of about 6.5 dB for rain fade and other impairments</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Whether 6.5 dB is enough depends on the rain attenuation predicted with ITU-R P.618 for the site and the availability required. Including uplink noise and interference would lower the overall C/N.</dd>
</dl>
</Example>

The level diagram above shows the same steps: received carrier C = 50 − 205.6 − 0.5 + 41.4 = −114.7 dBW and noise N = −228.6 + 21.8 + 75.6 = −131.2 dBW, which differ by the same 16.5 dB.

## How to improve a weak satellite link budget

- Bigger receive dish: doubling the diameter adds about 6 dB of gain.
- Quieter LNB: a lower noise temperature lowers Tsys and raises G/T.
- More EIRP: a higher power amplifier on the uplink, or a spot beam on the satellite.
- Lower data rate or more robust coding: reduces the required Eb/N0 or C/N.
- Lower frequency band: C band suffers much less rain fade than Ku or Ka band.

Satellite power and antenna size are limited by the spacecraft bus, as described in [satellite subsystems](/aerospace-engineering/satellite-subsystems/), which is why ground stations usually carry most of the burden.

## Satellite link budget interview and viva questions

1. **What is the largest loss in a satellite link budget?** Free space path loss, about 205 to 206 dB for a GEO link at Ku band, far larger than atmospheric or equipment losses in clear sky.
2. **What is EIRP?** The transmitter power plus transmit antenna gain minus feed loss, in dBW: the power an isotropic antenna would need to radiate to give the same signal in the beam direction.
3. **Why is G/T called the figure of merit of an earth station?** Because C/N0 rises one for one with it, it captures both the dish gain and the system noise temperature in one number.
4. **How does doubling the frequency affect free space path loss?** Loss rises by 20 log 2, about 6 dB, although a dish of fixed size also gains about 6 dB at the higher frequency.
5. **Why do Ku and Ka band links need larger margins than C band links?** Rain attenuation increases strongly with frequency above about 10 GHz, so the margin predicted with ITU-R P.618 for the same availability is larger.
