<Key title="Short answer">

**Bioreactor types** are vessel designs that give cells or enzymes controlled conditions to grow and make products. The main ones are stirred tank, bubble column, airlift, packed and fluidised bed, membrane or hollow fibre, photobioreactor and single use bag reactors, run in batch, fed-batch or continuous mode with control of temperature, pH and dissolved oxygen.

</Key>

Living cells are sensitive: they need the right temperature, pH, nutrients and oxygen, must be protected from contamination, and some (especially animal cells) are damaged by strong shear. Bioreactor design balances these needs with efficient mixing and mass transfer at scales from millilitres in the lab to hundreds of cubic metres in industry.

The **stirred tank reactor (STR)** is used for most microbial fermentations and mammalian cell cultures.

- A motor drives one or more **impellers**: Rushton turbines for high gas dispersion in microbial cultures, or pitched blade and marine impellers for gentle mixing of shear sensitive cells.
- **Baffles** prevent vortexing and improve mixing.
- A **sparger** releases sterile air or oxygen as bubbles.
- A **jacket** or internal coils remove the heat generated by metabolism and agitation.
- **Probes** measure temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen (DO), and often foam level, pressure and off-gas composition.
- **Sterile design:** steam sterilisable stainless steel, aseptic seals, sterile filters on gas lines, clean in place (CIP) and steam in place (SIP).

Its behaviour approximates an ideal well mixed reactor, the [CSTR](/chemical-engineering/cstr-vs-pfr/) of chemical engineering.

## Other bioreactor types

- **Bubble column:** a tall vessel where gas sparged at the bottom provides mixing; simple, low shear, low energy, but less control of mixing.
- **Airlift:** a bubble column with a draft tube or external loop; the riser contains gas and the downcomer degassed liquid, creating a defined circulation. Used for single cell protein, some cell cultures and wastewater.
- **Packed bed:** cells or enzymes immobilised on particles in a column through which medium flows; high cell density, used for enzyme conversions and some cell cultures.
- **Fluidised bed:** immobilised cells on particles kept suspended by upward flow; good mass transfer.
- **Membrane and hollow fibre:** cells grow on one side of semi-permeable fibres while medium flows on the other; very high cell densities, used for antibodies and cell therapy.
- **Photobioreactors:** tubular or flat panel transparent reactors that provide light for algae and cyanobacteria.
- **Wave (rocking) and single use stirred bag reactors:** disposable bags agitated by rocking or internal impellers, widely used in biopharmaceutical production.
- **Bioreactors for tissue engineering:** perfusion and rotating wall vessels that grow 3D tissues.

| Type | Mixing | Shear | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stirred tank | Impeller | Medium to high | Microbial fermentation, antibodies |
| Bubble column | Gas | Low | Simple aerobic processes, yeast |
| Airlift | Gas, defined loop | Low | Single cell protein, cell culture |
| Packed / fluidised bed | Liquid flow | Low | Immobilised enzymes and cells |
| Hollow fibre | Perfusion | Very low | High density cell culture, cell therapy |
| Single use | Rocking or impeller | Low to medium | Biopharmaceuticals, clinical supplies |

## Operating modes

- **Batch:** all nutrients added at the start; cells grow through lag, exponential, stationary and death phases. Simple, but product titre is limited.
- **Fed-batch:** nutrients fed during the run to extend growth and production while avoiding inhibition; the most common industrial mode.
- **Continuous (chemostat):** fresh medium flows in and culture flows out at the same rate, keeping cells growing at a steady state; the dilution rate D equals the specific growth rate μ at steady state.
- **Perfusion:** cells retained by filters while medium is exchanged continuously, giving very high cell densities.

## Growth kinetics

During exponential growth:

$$
X = X_0\,e^{\mu t} \qquad t_d = \frac{\ln 2}{\mu}
$$

The **Monod equation** relates specific growth rate to limiting substrate concentration S:

$$
\mu = \frac{\mu_{max}\,S}{K_S + S}
$$

## Oxygen transfer

For aerobic cultures, oxygen transfer rate is:

$$
OTR = k_L a\,(C^* - C_L)
$$

where k_La is the volumetric mass transfer coefficient, C* the saturation concentration and C_L the actual dissolved oxygen. The oxygen uptake rate of the cells must not exceed the OTR.

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>An E. coli fermentation reaches 20 g/L of cells with a specific oxygen uptake of 15 mmol O₂ per g cells per hour. Oxygen saturation with air is 0.21 mmol/L, and dissolved oxygen must be kept at 30% of saturation.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>The oxygen uptake rate and the minimum k_La required.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Steady state, OTR equals OUR; air sparging only.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>OUR = 20 × 15 = 300 mmol/L·h<br />C* − C_L = 0.21 − 0.3 × 0.21 = 0.147 mmol/L<br />k_La = 300 ÷ 0.147 = 2041 h⁻¹ ≈ 0.57 s⁻¹</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>The culture needs about 300 mmol O₂/L·h, requiring k_La of about 2000 h⁻¹, which is at the upper limit of large stirred tanks, so oxygen enrichment or lower cell density would be needed</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Using pure oxygen raises C* about fivefold, greatly easing the requirement; heat removal also becomes limiting at high rates.</dd>
</dl>
</Example>

## Key formulas for bioreactor types

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exponential growth | X = X_0 e^(μt) | X = cell concentration in g/L, μ = specific growth rate in h⁻¹, t = time in h |
| Doubling time | t_d = ln 2 ÷ μ | t_d in h |
| Monod equation | μ = μ_max S ÷ (K_S + S) | S = limiting substrate concentration, K_S = saturation constant in the same units as S |
| Chemostat steady state | D = μ | D = dilution rate in h⁻¹ |
| Oxygen transfer rate | OTR = k_La (C\* − C_L) | k_La in h⁻¹, C\* = saturation and C_L = actual dissolved oxygen in mmol/L |
| Oxygen uptake rate | OUR = X × q_O2 | q_O2 = specific oxygen uptake in mmol O₂ per g cells per h |

## Control and monitoring

Contamination control is as important as process control: every connection, sample port and gas line must keep the culture sterile for the whole run, sometimes weeks long.


Typical control loops: temperature by jacket water, pH by acid and base addition, dissolved oxygen by cascading agitation speed, gas flow and oxygen enrichment, foam by antifoam addition, and feed rate by profiles or feedback from off-gas or glucose sensors. Accurate [temperature](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/temperature-measurements/) and flow measurement and well tuned controllers keep conditions within tight ranges. Process analytical technology adds online sensors for biomass, metabolites and product quality.

## Bioreactor types interview and viva questions

1. **Why is the stirred tank the most widely used bioreactor type?** Impellers, baffles and a sparger give good mixing and oxygen transfer, and its behaviour approximates an ideal well mixed reactor suited to most microbial fermentations and mammalian cell cultures.
2. **How does an airlift bioreactor differ from a bubble column?** An airlift adds a draft tube or external loop, so gassed liquid rises in the riser and degassed liquid falls in the downcomer, giving a defined, low shear circulation.
3. **Why is fed-batch the most common industrial operating mode?** Feeding nutrients during the run extends growth and production while avoiding inhibition, overcoming the limited product titre of simple batch operation.
4. **What holds in a chemostat at steady state?** The dilution rate D equals the specific growth rate μ, so the cells keep growing at a steady state.
5. **How is dissolved oxygen usually controlled?** By cascading agitation speed, gas flow and oxygen enrichment so that the oxygen transfer rate keeps up with the oxygen uptake rate of the cells.

## Scale-up of bioreactor types

Scaling from lab to production is challenging because mixing time, oxygen transfer, shear and heat removal do not scale equally. Engineers use criteria such as constant power per volume, constant k_La, constant impeller tip speed or constant mixing time, together with pilot runs and computational fluid dynamics. Downstream, product recovery begins, and many final products, such as vaccines and biologics, are then regulated as medicines or used with devices covered under [medical device classification](/biomedical-engineering/medical-device-classification/).
