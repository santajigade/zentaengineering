  - q: "What is the difference between wet and dry electrodes?"
    a: "Wet electrodes, usually silver/silver chloride with conductive gel, give low, stable skin contact impedance and low noise, so they are standard for clinical ECG. Dry electrodes make direct contact without gel, which is convenient for wearables and long term monitoring, but they have higher and more variable impedance and are more sensitive to motion artefacts."
  - q: "Why is a high common mode rejection ratio important?"
    a: "The body picks up large mains interference (50 or 60 Hz) that appears almost equally on all electrodes as a common mode voltage, often much larger than the millivolt ECG signal. An amplifier with high CMRR, typically above 100 dB, amplifies only the difference between electrodes and rejects the common interference."

<Key title="Short answer">

**Biopotential measurement** is the recording of the small electrical signals produced by the heart (ECG), brain (EEG), muscles (EMG) and eyes (EOG). Silver/silver chloride electrodes convert ionic body currents into electronic currents, and a high CMRR instrumentation amplifier amplifies the millivolt or microvolt difference before filtering, isolation and digitisation under IEC 60601 safety rules.

</Key>

Measuring the body's electrical activity is one of the oldest and most important tools in medicine. Willem Einthoven recorded the first practical ECG in 1903; today ECG, EEG and EMG are used everywhere from intensive care units to smartwatches. The engineering challenge is to capture very small signals reliably and safely.

## Where biopotentials come from

Nerve and muscle cells maintain a resting potential of about −70 to −90 mV across their membranes. When they fire, ion channels open and the membrane depolarises in an **action potential**. The coordinated activity of many cells produces electric fields that spread through body tissues and can be measured at the skin surface.

| Signal | Source | Typical amplitude | Frequency range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ECG | Heart | 0.5 to 4 mV | 0.05 to 150 Hz |
| EEG | Brain cortex | 5 to 300 µV | 0.5 to 100 Hz |
| EMG (surface) | Skeletal muscle | 0.1 to 5 mV | 10 to 500 Hz |
| EOG | Eye movement | 50 to 3500 µV | DC to 50 Hz |

## Electrodes and the skin interface

Current in the body is carried by ions; current in wires by electrons. The **electrode electrolyte interface** converts between them through chemical reactions.

- **Silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl)** electrodes are almost **non-polarisable**: they pass current with small, stable half-cell potential and low noise, which is why they are standard for ECG.
- **Conductive gel** wets the skin, lowering impedance through the outer dead skin layer (stratum corneum).
- **Skin preparation** (light abrasion, cleaning) reduces impedance and **motion artefact**.
- Electrodes have a **half-cell potential** (DC offset of tens to hundreds of mV) that the amplifier must tolerate without saturating.
- **Dry and textile electrodes** suit wearables; **needle electrodes** are used for intramuscular EMG; **cup electrodes** with paste for EEG.

## The amplifier

### Instrumentation amplifier

A **differential instrumentation amplifier** amplifies the difference between two electrodes while rejecting voltages common to both. Requirements:

- **High input impedance** (well above 10 MΩ) so that unequal electrode impedances do not convert common mode voltage into a differential error.
- **High CMRR**, typically 100 dB or more at mains frequency.
- **Low noise**, a few µV peak to peak or less in the signal band.
- **DC offset tolerance** for electrode half-cell potentials.

The classic three op-amp design, described in [inverting and non-inverting amplifiers](/electronics-engineering/inverting-and-non-inverting-amplifier/), sets gain with a single resistor:

$$
G = 1 + \frac{2R_1}{R_G}
$$

### CMRR

$$
CMRR_{dB} = 20\log_{10}\frac{A_d}{A_{cm}}
$$

### Right leg drive

A **driven right leg** circuit senses the common mode voltage and feeds an inverted version back to the body through a third electrode, actively reducing interference further.

## Filtering and digitisation

- **High pass filter** (0.05 Hz for diagnostic ECG, 0.5 Hz for monitoring) removes baseline wander from breathing and electrode drift.
- **Low pass filter** (40 Hz for monitoring, 150 Hz or more for diagnostic ECG) removes high frequency noise and prevents aliasing before sampling, following the [sampling theorem](/electronics-engineering/sampling-theorem/).
- **Notch filter** at 50 or 60 Hz suppresses remaining mains interference.
- **ADC:** modern front ends use 16 to 24 bit sigma-delta converters sampling at 250 to 1000 samples per second or more.

Digital processing then detects QRS complexes, calculates heart rate and classifies rhythms.

## Key formulas for biopotential measurement

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Three op-amp in-amp gain | G = 1 + 2R_1 ÷ R_G | R_1 = matched first stage resistors, R_G = gain setting resistor, both in Ω |
| Common mode rejection ratio | CMRR_dB = 20 log10(A_d ÷ A_cm) | A_d = differential gain, A_cm = common mode gain (dimensionless) |
| Common mode gain | A_cm = A_d ÷ 10^(CMRR_dB ÷ 20) | CMRR_dB in dB |
| Required total gain | G = V_out ÷ V_in | V_out = target ADC level in V, V_in = signal amplitude in V |
| Two stage gain | G = G_1 × G_2 | G_1 = in-amp stage gain, G_2 = second stage gain after the high pass filter |

## Patient safety

Electrodes create a direct path into the body, so medical equipment must limit leakage current to microamp levels under **IEC 60601-1**. Measures include **isolated power supplies** and **isolation amplifiers or digital isolators** between the patient side and mains powered parts, **defibrillation protection** so the input survives shocks of several kV, and classification of applied parts (B, BF and CF, with CF for direct cardiac contact having the strictest limits). Good [electrical earthing](/electrical-engineering/electrical-earthing/) practice in hospitals adds further protection.

## Biopotential measurement interview and viva questions

1. **Why are Ag/AgCl electrodes standard for ECG?** They are almost non-polarisable, passing current with a small, stable half-cell potential and low noise.
2. **Why must a biopotential amplifier have a very high input impedance?** So that unequal electrode impedances do not convert the common mode voltage into a differential error.
3. **Why is the first stage gain of an ECG front end kept low?** Electrode half-cell offsets of hundreds of mV would saturate a high gain first stage, so a low gain in-amp is followed by a high pass filter and then most of the gain.
4. **What does a driven right leg circuit do?** It senses the common mode voltage and feeds an inverted version back to the body through a third electrode, actively reducing mains interference.
5. **What is a CF applied part?** It is the IEC 60601-1 classification for applied parts in direct cardiac contact, which have the strictest leakage current limits.

## Common problems and solutions in biopotential measurement

- **Baseline wander:** improve skin preparation, use appropriate high pass filtering.
- **Mains interference:** check electrode contact and lead dress, use shielded cables and right leg drive.
- **Motion artefacts:** secure electrodes and cables, use good quality Ag/AgCl electrodes.
- **EMG noise on ECG:** keep the patient relaxed and warm, use low pass filtering.

Biopotential measurement links physiology with electronics, and the same front end principles apply in wearables, sleep labs, brain computer interfaces and the myoelectric [prosthetic limbs](/biomedical-engineering/prosthetic-limbs/) that use EMG to control motors.
