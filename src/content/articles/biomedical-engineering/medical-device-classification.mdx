<Key title="Short answer">

**Medical device classification** groups devices by **risk** to patients and users, which decides how strictly they are regulated before and after sale. The **EU MDR** uses **Class I, IIa, IIb and III**; the **US FDA** uses **Class I, II and III**; and **India's Medical Devices Rules 2017** use **Class A, B, C and D**, all rising in risk. Classification depends on **intended purpose**, **duration of contact**, **invasiveness**, whether the device is **active** or **implantable**, and the **body part** affected. Higher classes need more evidence, such as clinical data and third party review by a **notified body** or regulator, under quality systems such as **ISO 13485** and risk management to **ISO 14971**.

</Key>

Medical devices range from tongue depressors and bandages to pacemakers, MRI scanners, insulin pumps and diagnostic software. Regulating them all identically would either leave high risk devices under-controlled or bury simple products in paperwork. Risk based classification solves this, and it is the first regulatory decision any device developer must make, because it determines the whole route to market.

## What counts as a medical device

A medical device is any instrument, apparatus, software, implant, material or other article intended by the manufacturer for a medical purpose, such as diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, treatment or alleviation of disease or injury, or for investigating or replacing anatomy or a physiological process, and which does not achieve its main effect by pharmacological, immunological or metabolic means (which would make it a drug). The **intended purpose**, as stated in labelling and marketing, is central.

In vitro diagnostic devices (IVDs), such as blood glucose meters and test kits, have separate rules, for example the EU IVDR with Classes A to D.

## How medical device classification works in the EU

The EU MDR Annex VIII contains **22 rules** grouped by device type:

- **Non-invasive devices** (Rules 1 to 4): mostly Class I unless they channel or store fluids for infusion, modify blood, or contact injured skin.
- **Invasive devices** (Rules 5 to 8): depends on whether invasion is through a body orifice or surgical, and on duration: **transient** (under 60 minutes), **short term** (60 minutes to 30 days) or **long term** (over 30 days). Implantable and long term devices are generally IIb or III; devices in contact with the heart, central circulatory system or central nervous system are Class III.
- **Active devices** (Rules 9 to 13): therapeutic devices delivering energy (IIa, IIb if potentially hazardous), diagnostic and monitoring devices, and **software (Rule 11)**.
- **Special rules** (Rules 14 to 22): devices incorporating medicines, contraception, disinfection, nanomaterials, devices made with animal tissues, and others.

When several rules apply, the **highest class** applies.

## United States

The FDA classifies devices into about 1700 generic types in 21 CFR. Routes to market:

- **Class I:** general controls; most are exempt from premarket notification.
- **Class II:** general and special controls; usually a **510(k)** showing substantial equivalence to a legally marketed predicate device.
- **Class III:** **premarket approval (PMA)** with clinical evidence of safety and effectiveness.
- **De Novo** pathway for novel low to moderate risk devices with no predicate.

## India

Under the **Medical Devices Rules 2017**, administered by **CDSCO**, devices are Classes A to D. Class A and B manufacturing licences are granted by state licensing authorities, while Classes C and D are licensed centrally, with notified bodies auditing quality systems for lower classes. Classification lists for each device category are published by CDSCO.

## Conformity assessment and requirements

Higher classes need more:

- **Quality management system** to **ISO 13485**, including design controls.
- **Risk management** to **ISO 14971**, identifying hazards, estimating and controlling risks and monitoring after sale. Tools such as [FMEA](/safety-engineering/fmea-explained/) are widely used.
- **Technical documentation:** design, verification and validation, labelling.
- **Electrical safety and EMC** to the **IEC 60601** series for medical electrical equipment, and **IEC 62304** for software life cycle.
- **Biocompatibility** to **ISO 10993** for materials contacting the body, see [biomaterials](/biomedical-engineering/biomaterials-types/).
- **Clinical evaluation** with clinical data, especially for Class IIb and III.
- **Notified body or regulator review** for all but the lowest classes.
- **Post-market surveillance**, vigilance reporting and periodic safety updates.

## Practical tips for engineers

Define the intended purpose and indications precisely early in design, since changing them later can change the class. Build regulatory requirements into the design process from the start, keep a living risk management file, plan clinical evidence needs, and design for usability (IEC 62366), because many incidents come from use errors. Choosing a slightly narrower intended purpose can sometimes lower the class and shorten time to market, but claims must then match the labelling. Medical device classification is not paperwork at the end of a project: it shapes the whole development, from [biopotential measurement](/biomedical-engineering/biopotential-measurement-basics/) front ends to [prosthetic limbs](/biomedical-engineering/prosthetic-limbs/) and imaging systems.
