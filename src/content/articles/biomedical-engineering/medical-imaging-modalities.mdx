<Key title="Short answer">

The main **medical imaging modalities** are X-ray radiography, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), ultrasound and nuclear medicine (SPECT and PET). X-ray and CT use X-ray attenuation, MRI uses magnetic fields and radio waves, ultrasound uses sound echoes and nuclear medicine uses radioactive tracers. They differ in contrast, resolution, speed, radiation dose and cost.

</Key>

Medical imaging lets clinicians see inside the body without surgery, diagnose disease earlier, guide procedures and monitor treatment. Choosing the right modality depends on what needs to be seen (bone, soft tissue, blood flow, metabolism), how quickly, at what risk and cost. Biomedical engineers design, maintain and optimise these complex systems.

## X-ray radiography

X-rays are produced in an **X-ray tube** when electrons accelerated by tens of kilovolts strike a tungsten target. As X-rays pass through the body, they are absorbed more by dense, high atomic number tissues (bone, contrast agents) than by soft tissue and air. The transmitted intensity follows exponential attenuation:

$$
I = I_0\,e^{-\mu x}
$$

where μ is the linear attenuation coefficient and x the thickness. A digital detector records the transmitted pattern as a 2D projection image.

- **Uses:** fractures, chest imaging, dental imaging, mammography, fluoroscopy for real time guidance.
- **Strengths:** fast, cheap, widely available, excellent for bone and lungs.
- **Limits:** overlapping structures in 2D, poor soft tissue contrast, ionising radiation (low dose for a chest X-ray, about 0.02 to 0.1 mSv).

## Computed tomography (CT)

An X-ray tube and detector array rotate around the patient while the table moves (helical scanning). Hundreds of projections from different angles are mathematically reconstructed, using filtered back projection or iterative methods, into cross-sectional slices and 3D volumes. Pixel values are expressed in **Hounsfield units** (water = 0, air = −1000, bone about +1000).

- **Uses:** trauma, stroke, cancer staging, chest, abdomen, angiography, guiding biopsies.
- **Strengths:** fast (whole body in seconds), 3D, good for bone, lungs and bleeding.
- **Limits:** higher radiation dose (a few to about 10 mSv), soft tissue contrast lower than MRI, iodine contrast agent risks.

## Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

The patient lies in a strong static magnetic field, typically **1.5 or 3 tesla**, which aligns hydrogen nuclei (protons) in water and fat. Radiofrequency pulses at the **Larmor frequency** tip the protons; as they relax, they emit signals detected by receiver coils. **Gradient coils** encode position, and Fourier transforms reconstruct images. Different pulse sequences give **T1, T2, diffusion** and other contrasts.

$$
f = \gamma\,B_0
$$

where γ for hydrogen is about 42.58 MHz per tesla.

- **Uses:** brain, spinal cord, joints, muscles, liver, prostate, heart, tumours.
- **Strengths:** excellent soft tissue contrast, no ionising radiation, functional imaging.
- **Limits:** slow (tens of minutes), expensive, noisy, claustrophobic for some, contraindicated for some implants; strict safety zones because the magnet is always on.

## Ultrasound

A **transducer** with piezoelectric elements sends short pulses of high frequency sound (typically 2 to 15 MHz) into the body and receives echoes from tissue boundaries. The time delay gives depth (using about 1540 m/s in soft tissue), and echo strength gives brightness (B-mode). **Doppler** ultrasound measures blood flow velocity from the frequency shift of echoes from moving blood cells. Higher frequencies give better resolution but less penetration.

- **Uses:** obstetrics, abdomen, heart (echocardiography), blood vessels, guiding needles, point of care.
- **Strengths:** real time, portable, no ionising radiation, low cost.
- **Limits:** cannot see through bone or gas, operator dependent, limited depth at high frequency.

## Nuclear medicine: SPECT and PET

A radioactive tracer is injected, swallowed or inhaled and accumulates in tissues according to their function. **Gamma cameras** (planar and SPECT) detect single gamma photons; **PET** detects pairs of 511 keV photons from positron annihilation, often using fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) to show glucose metabolism. PET is usually combined with CT (PET/CT) or MRI for anatomical reference.

- **Uses:** cancer staging, heart perfusion, bone scans, thyroid, brain disorders.
- **Strengths:** functional and molecular information.
- **Limits:** lower resolution, radiation dose, tracer production logistics.

## Medical imaging modalities compared

| Feature | X-ray | CT | MRI | Ultrasound | PET |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Physical basis | X-ray attenuation | X-ray attenuation, tomography | Nuclear magnetic resonance | Acoustic reflection | Positron emitting tracer |
| Ionising radiation | Yes, low | Yes, moderate | No | No | Yes |
| Best for | Bone, chest | Trauma, bone, lungs, bleeding | Soft tissue, brain, joints | Fetus, heart, vessels, abdomen | Metabolism, cancer |
| Typical scan time | Seconds | Seconds | 15 to 60 min | Minutes, real time | 20 to 40 min |
| Spatial resolution | High (about 0.1 mm) | About 0.5 mm | About 1 mm | 0.2 to 2 mm | 4 to 6 mm |
| Relative cost | Low | Medium | High | Low | High |
| Portability | Mobile units | Fixed | Fixed | Handheld possible | Fixed |

## Key formulas for medical imaging modalities

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| X-ray attenuation | I = I₀ e^(−μx) | I₀ = incident intensity, μ = linear attenuation coefficient in 1/cm, x = thickness in cm |
| Larmor frequency | f = γ B₀ | γ = 42.58 MHz/T for hydrogen, B₀ = static field in T, f in MHz |
| Ultrasound echo depth | d = c t ÷ 2 | c = about 1540 m/s in soft tissue, t = echo return time in s, d in m |

## Image quality basics

All modalities trade off three things: spatial resolution (smallest detail), contrast (difference between tissues) and noise. Reducing dose or scan time usually raises noise, so engineers develop better detectors and reconstruction to keep images diagnostic at lower dose.

## Choosing among medical imaging modalities

Clinicians follow appropriateness guidelines: start with the safest, cheapest test that answers the question, such as X-ray for a suspected fracture or ultrasound for gallstones, and escalate to CT or MRI when needed. Radiation protection follows the **ALARA** principle (as low as reasonably achievable). Engineers contribute through detector physics, image reconstruction, AI image analysis ([classification, detection and segmentation](/ai-data-engineering/classification-vs-detection-vs-segmentation/)), quality assurance, equipment safety under [medical device regulation](/biomedical-engineering/medical-device-classification/), and maintenance. Together, these medical imaging modalities form the backbone of modern diagnosis.

## Medical imaging modalities interview and viva questions

1. **Which medical imaging modalities avoid ionising radiation?** MRI and ultrasound do not use ionising radiation. X-ray radiography, CT and nuclear medicine all involve ionising radiation.
2. **Why does bone appear bright on an X-ray image?** Dense, high atomic number tissue such as bone has a higher attenuation coefficient, so it absorbs more X-rays and less radiation reaches the detector behind it.
3. **What are Hounsfield units?** They are the CT pixel value scale in which water is 0, air is about −1000 and bone is about +1000.
4. **Why is the ultrasound echo time divided by two to find depth?** The pulse travels to the reflecting boundary and back, so the measured time covers twice the depth.
5. **What does the Larmor frequency of an MRI scanner depend on?** It is proportional to the static field strength, so a 3 T scanner imaging hydrogen works at about 128 MHz, twice the frequency of a 1.5 T scanner.
