    a: "Biocompatibility is the ability of a material to perform with an appropriate host response in a specific application. It is not a fixed property of the material: the same polymer may be biocompatible in a short term catheter but not in a permanent vascular graft.   - q: "Why is titanium widely used for implants?"
    a: "Titanium and its alloys, especially Ti-6Al-4V, combine high strength, low density, a lower elastic modulus than steel or cobalt chromium, excellent corrosion resistance from a stable oxide film, and good integration with bone (osseointegration). These properties suit dental implants, hip stems, bone plates and screws."

Designers reduce stress shielding by using lower modulus materials, porous structures and implant shapes that share load with bone."

<Key title="Short answer">

**Biomaterials** are natural or synthetic materials designed to interact with biological systems in medical devices and implants. The main types are metals, ceramics, polymers, composites and natural materials, chosen for biocompatibility, suitable stiffness and strength, corrosion and wear resistance, sterilisability and, where needed, controlled degradation, with biological safety evaluated under ISO 10993.

</Key>

From dental fillings and contact lenses to hip replacements, heart valves and stents, biomaterials improve and extend millions of lives every year. Choosing a biomaterial is a classic materials engineering problem with an extra, demanding constraint: the material must work in the warm, wet, salty, chemically active and immunologically watchful environment of the human body, often for decades.

## Generations of biomaterials

- **First generation (bioinert):** materials chosen to cause minimal reaction, such as titanium, alumina and UHMWPE.
- **Second generation (bioactive and bioresorbable):** materials that bond to tissue (hydroxyapatite, bioactive glass) or dissolve as tissue heals (PLA, PLGA sutures and screws).
- **Third generation (bioinstructive):** materials that stimulate specific cell responses, such as scaffolds with growth factors for tissue engineering.

### Metals

- **Titanium and Ti-6Al-4V:** strong, light, corrosion resistant, osseointegrating; dental implants, hip stems, bone plates, spinal cages.
- **Cobalt chromium alloys:** very wear and corrosion resistant; femoral heads, knee components, heart valve frames.
- **316L stainless steel:** low cost, strong; temporary fracture fixation, surgical instruments.
- **Nitinol (nickel titanium):** shape memory and superelasticity; self expanding stents and orthodontic wires.

Metals are stiff and strong but may release ions, corrode and cause stress shielding. Corrosion mechanisms are discussed in [types of corrosion](/materials-engineering/types-of-corrosion/).

### Ceramics

- **Alumina and zirconia toughened alumina:** very hard, low wear femoral heads and dental crowns.
- **Hydroxyapatite and calcium phosphates:** similar to bone mineral; coatings on implants and bone graft substitutes.
- **Bioactive glasses:** bond to bone and soft tissue, used in bone fillers and dental products.

Ceramics are hard and wear resistant but brittle.

### Polymers

- **UHMWPE:** low friction bearings in hip and knee joints; highly cross-linked versions reduce wear.
- **PEEK:** spinal cages and trauma devices; modulus close to bone and radiolucent.
- **Silicone:** catheters, breast implants, shunts.
- **PMMA:** bone cement, intraocular lenses.
- **Hydrogels:** contact lenses, wound dressings, drug delivery.
- **Biodegradable PLA, PGA, PLGA, PCL:** sutures, resorbable screws, drug delivery, tissue scaffolds.

Polymer families are compared in [thermoplastics vs thermosets](/materials-engineering/thermoplastics-vs-thermosets/).

### Composites and natural materials

Carbon fibre reinforced PEEK for implants, dental composites of resin and glass filler, and natural materials such as collagen, gelatin, alginate, chitosan, silk and decellularised tissue matrices for tissue engineering.

## Key properties

| Material | Elastic modulus (GPa) | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cortical bone | About 15 to 20 | Reference |
| Ti-6Al-4V | About 110 | Stems, plates, dental implants |
| CoCrMo | About 210 to 240 | Joint bearings |
| 316L stainless steel | About 190 to 200 | Fracture fixation |
| Alumina | About 380 | Femoral heads |
| PEEK | About 3 to 4 | Spinal cages |
| UHMWPE | About 0.5 to 1 | Joint liners |

The mismatch between implant and bone stiffness drives **stress shielding**: when two materials share a load in parallel, each carries load in proportion to its stiffness (modulus times area):

$$
\frac{F_\text{implant}}{F_\text{bone}} = \frac{E_i A_i}{E_b A_b}
$$

<dt>Find</dt><dd>The load carried by bone in each case.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Equal strain in bone and implant (perfect bonding); linear elastic behaviour.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>Bone stiffness term: 17 × 300 = 5100<br />Titanium: 110 × 150 = 16 500; bone share = 5100 ÷ (5100 + 16 500) = 23.6%, so bone carries 472 N<br />CoCr: 220 × 150 = 33 000; bone share = 5100 ÷ 38 100 = 13.4%, so bone carries 268 N</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>With titanium, bone carries about 470 N (24%); with CoCr only about 270 N (13%), so the stiffer stem shields bone more</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Real hip mechanics involve bending, interfaces and complex geometry; finite element models are used in design.</dd>

## Biocompatibility and testing

Biocompatibility depends on the whole device and its use. **ISO 10993-1** sets out a risk based biological evaluation, considering the nature and duration of body contact. Typical endpoints include **cytotoxicity**, **sensitisation**, **irritation**, **acute and chronic systemic toxicity**, **genotoxicity**, **implantation** effects, **haemocompatibility** for blood contacting devices, and **chemical characterisation** of leachables. Sterilisation (steam, ethylene oxide, gamma or electron beam) must not degrade the material, and surface finish, cleanliness and manufacturing residues matter as much as the bulk material.

## Host responses

When a material is implanted, proteins adsorb within seconds, followed by an inflammatory response. Bioinert materials end up encapsulated in a thin fibrous layer; bioactive materials bond directly to tissue; degradable materials are gradually replaced. Problems include chronic inflammation, infection on implant surfaces (biofilms), wear particle induced bone loss (osteolysis) around joints, and metal ion release. Surface engineering, such as porous coatings, antibacterial treatments and drug eluting layers, improves outcomes.

## Manufacturing biomaterials

Processing changes properties. Titanium implants may be machined, forged or 3D printed by powder bed fusion, which allows porous lattice structures that encourage bone ingrowth and lower stiffness. UHMWPE is cross-linked by radiation and then heat treated to improve wear resistance while controlling oxidation. Ceramics are pressed and sintered with tight control of grain size and porosity, since small flaws cause brittle fracture. Surface treatments such as grit blasting, acid etching, plasma spraying of hydroxyapatite and anodising tune how cells and proteins interact with the implant.

## Key formulas for biomaterials

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Load sharing ratio | F_implant ÷ F_bone = (E_i × A_i) ÷ (E_b × A_b) | E = elastic modulus in GPa, A = cross-sectional area in mm² |
| Bone share of load | F_bone ÷ F = E_b A_b ÷ (E_b A_b + E_i A_i) | F = total axial load in N shared in parallel |
| Load carried by bone | F_bone = F × bone share | F_bone in N |

## Biomaterials interview and viva questions

1. **What is biocompatibility?** It is the ability of a material to perform with an appropriate host response in a specific application, so it depends on the material, device design, site and duration of contact rather than on the material alone.
2. **What is stress shielding and how is it reduced?** A much stiffer implant carries most of the load, leaving surrounding bone under-loaded so it can lose density; lower modulus materials, porous structures and load sharing implant shapes reduce it.
3. **Why is UHMWPE cross-linked for joint liners?** Radiation cross-linking improves wear resistance, and the heat treatment that follows controls oxidation.
4. **How do bioinert, bioactive and bioresorbable materials differ?** Bioinert materials such as titanium and alumina cause minimal reaction and end up encapsulated in a thin fibrous layer, bioactive materials such as hydroxyapatite bond to tissue, and bioresorbable materials such as PLA dissolve as tissue heals.
5. **Which standard guides the biological evaluation of a medical device?** ISO 10993-1, which sets out a risk based evaluation according to the nature and duration of body contact, with endpoints such as cytotoxicity, sensitisation, irritation and genotoxicity.

## Choosing biomaterials

Selection balances mechanical needs, biological response, degradation behaviour, imaging compatibility (MRI, X-ray), manufacturability, sterilisation and cost, within the regulatory framework for the device's class, as described in [medical device classification](/biomedical-engineering/medical-device-classification/). Engineers also test mechanical performance, such as fatigue of hip stems, using methods from [fatigue failure](/materials-engineering/fatigue-failure/) analysis. The same principles guide the design of [prosthetic limbs](/biomedical-engineering/prosthetic-limbs/) and sockets that sit against skin rather than inside the body.
