<Key title="Short answer">

**Ship auxiliary machinery** is all the engine room equipment, other than the main propulsion engine, that keeps a ship running. It includes centrifugal purifiers that clean fuel and lubricating oil, freshwater generators, starting air compressors at about 25 to 30 bar, pumps, boilers, oily water separators, steering gear, refrigeration and generators.

</Key>

While the main engine gets most attention, a ship cannot sail without dozens of auxiliary systems working reliably around the clock. Marine engineers spend much of their time operating and maintaining this machinery. This article covers three key systems in detail and summarises the rest.

## Centrifugal purifiers and clarifiers

Heavy fuel oil and lubricating oil contain water, catalytic fines from refining, sludge and other contaminants that would damage fuel pumps, injectors, piston rings, liners and bearings. Gravity settling in tanks is too slow, so ships use **disc stack centrifugal separators**.

The bowl spins at about 6000 to 10 000 rpm, creating forces thousands of times gravity. Oil flows through the thin gaps between conical **discs**, where heavier water and solids move outward and lighter clean oil moves inward. Settling velocity in a centrifuge follows Stokes' law with the centrifugal acceleration:

$$
v = \frac{d^2\,(\rho_p - \rho_o)\,\omega^2 r}{18\,\mu}
$$

Here v is the settling velocity of a particle or droplet, d its diameter, ρ_p its density, ρ_o the oil density, ω the bowl angular speed, r the radius and μ the oil viscosity.

- **Purifier:** separates oil, water and solids; a **gravity disc** sized for oil density positions the oil and water interface.
- **Clarifier:** removes solids only, more efficient for fine particles.
- **Self-cleaning bowls** open periodically to discharge sludge without stopping.
- **Heating** the oil, typically to about 98 °C for heavy fuel, lowers viscosity and density, improving separation.
- Modern **alcap or flexible separators** handle fuels up to 1010 kg/m³ at 15 °C using water sensors instead of fixed gravity discs.

## Freshwater generators

Ships need fresh water for boilers, engine cooling, drinking (after treatment) and washing. Most produce it on board.

### Vacuum evaporator type

A **plate or shell and tube evaporator** works under vacuum and uses heat from the main engine **jacket cooling water**. Output on large ships is typically 20 to 50 tonnes per day, with very low energy cost because the heat is waste heat.

<Steps>

1. A seawater driven **ejector** creates the vacuum, so seawater boils at about 40 to 60 °C.
2. The vapour passes a **demister** that removes droplets.
3. The vapour condenses in a **condenser** cooled by seawater.
4. A **distillate pump** delivers fresh water through a **salinometer**, which dumps water back to the evaporator if salinity exceeds about 2 to 10 ppm.
5. The **brine ejector** removes the concentrated brine.

</Steps>

Heat exchanger principles are explained in [heat exchanger types](/chemical-engineering/heat-exchanger-types/).

### Reverse osmosis

High pressure pumps force seawater through semi-permeable membranes that reject salts. RO plants are common on passenger ships and offshore units, where demand is high, and need pre-filtration and periodic membrane cleaning.

## Air compressors

Ships have several compressed air systems:

<Tabs><div data-tab="Starting air">

Two or more **two stage reciprocating compressors** with intercooling and aftercooling charge **air receivers** to about 25 to 30 bar for starting main and auxiliary engines. Rules require enough air for a set number of starts.

</div><div data-tab="Control and service air">

About 7 bar, reduced from starting air or produced by screw compressors, then dried and filtered for pneumatic controls and tools.

</div><div data-tab="Emergency air">

A hand or diesel driven emergency compressor starts an auxiliary engine from a dead ship condition.

</div></Tabs>

Safety features include relief valves on each stage and receiver, fusible plugs, drain valves for condensate and oil, and non return valves. Multistage compression with intercooling reduces the work needed, a direct application of the [laws of thermodynamics](/mechanical-engineering/laws-of-thermodynamics/).

## Other ship auxiliary machinery

- **Pumps:** centrifugal pumps for seawater and freshwater cooling, ballast, fire and general service; positive displacement pumps for fuel, lube oil and sludge, as described in [centrifugal pump working principle](/mechanical-engineering/centrifugal-pump-working-principle/).
- **Boilers and exhaust gas economisers:** steam for heating heavy fuel, cargo, accommodation and sometimes turbine driven pumps.
- **Oily water separators:** treat bilge water to below **15 ppm** oil before discharge under MARPOL Annex I, with an oil content meter that stops overboard discharge if exceeded.
- **Sewage treatment plants** and **incinerators.**
- **Steering gear:** electro-hydraulic rams or rotary vanes moving the rudder, with duplicated power units.
- **Refrigeration and air conditioning** for provisions, cargo and accommodation.
- **Generators:** diesel generators, shaft generators and emergency generator, covered in [ship electrical power system](/marine-engineering/ship-electrical-power-system/).

<dt>Given</dt><dd>A ship's main engine burns 40 t of heavy fuel per day. The fuel contains 0.5% water by mass. The purifier must handle the daily consumption in 20 hours of operation, and the manufacturer recommends running at about 70% of rated throughput for good separation.</dd>

<dt>Find</dt><dd>Required throughput and water removed per day.</dd>

<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Fuel density about 0.97 t/m³ at separation temperature; all water removed.</dd>

<dt>Solution</dt><dd>Volume per day = 40 ÷ 0.97 = 41.2 m³; flow = 41.2 ÷ 20 = 2.06 m³/h = 2060 L/h<br />Rated capacity needed = 2060 ÷ 0.70 ≈ 2940 L/h<br />Water removed = 0.005 × 40 = 0.2 t = 200 kg per day</dd>

<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>Run the purifier at about 2060 L/h, using a unit rated for about 3000 L/h; it removes about 200 kg of water per day</strong></dd>

<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Lower flow improves separation of catalytic fines; fuel quality reports may require slower rates or series operation.</dd>

## Maintaining ship auxiliary machinery

Many auxiliary systems are duplicated, with one running and one on standby, so that a single failure does not stop the ship.

Planned maintenance systems schedule overhauls of purifier bowls, compressor valves, evaporator plate cleaning and pump seals by running hours, supported by condition monitoring of vibration, temperatures and pressures.

Engineers must also comply with MARPOL record keeping for oil, sludge and bilge water. Well maintained ship auxiliary machinery keeps the main [two-stroke marine diesel engine](/marine-engineering/two-stroke-marine-diesel-engine/) supplied with clean fuel, cooling water and starting air, and keeps the ship safe and compliant.

## Ship auxiliary machinery interview and viva questions

1. **What is the difference between a purifier and a clarifier?** A purifier separates oil, water and solids and discharges water through an outlet set by a gravity disc or water transducer, while a clarifier removes only solids and has no water outlet.
2. **Why is heavy fuel heated to about 98 °C before separation?** Heating lowers the oil's viscosity and density, which increases the settling velocity of water and solids and improves separation.
3. **How can a freshwater generator boil seawater with engine jacket water?** It runs under vacuum, so seawater boils at about 40 to 60 °C, which waste heat from the jacket cooling water can supply.
4. **What does the salinometer in a freshwater generator do?** It checks the distillate and dumps it back to the evaporator if salinity exceeds about 2 to 10 ppm.
5. **Why are starting air compressors two stage with intercooling?** Multistage compression with intercooling reduces the work needed to reach the 25 to 30 bar starting air pressure.
