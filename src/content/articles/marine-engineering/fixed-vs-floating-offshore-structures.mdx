<Key title="Short answer">

**Fixed vs floating offshore structures** differ in how they are supported. Fixed structures, such as monopiles, jackets, gravity bases and jack-ups, carry loads to the seabed and suit shallow to moderate depths. Floating structures, such as semi-submersibles, spars, TLPs and FPSOs, rely on buoyancy plus moorings or tendons, suit deep water and move with waves.

</Key>

The ocean hosts oil and gas platforms, offshore wind farms, aquaculture, bridges, ports and research stations. Whether a structure stands on the seabed or floats is the first and most important design decision. It depends on water depth, seabed soil, environment, function, installation method and cost.

Specific platform types used in oil and gas are covered in [offshore platform types](/petroleum-engineering/offshore-platform-types/); this article compares the two families more broadly, including offshore wind.

## Fixed offshore structures

<Tabs><div data-tab="Monopiles">

A single large steel tube, now often 8 to 11 m in diameter, driven or drilled into the seabed. The most common foundation for offshore wind turbines in water up to about 40 to 60 m. Simple and quick to install, but very heavy and sensitive to soil conditions and fatigue from waves and turbine loads.

</div><div data-tab="Jackets">

Welded tubular steel frames pinned to the seabed by piles. Used for oil and gas platforms up to about 400 m and for offshore wind substations and turbines in deeper or difficult sites. Jackets are stiff, relatively transparent to waves and efficient in steel use. Their members behave like the [steel sections](/civil-engineering/steel-sections-types/) of onshore structures, but are designed for wave fatigue and corrosion.

</div><div data-tab="Gravity based">

Large concrete or steel bases that rely on their weight to resist sliding and overturning, needing no piles. Suitable for hard seabeds, harsh environments and sites with ice. Concrete GBS can include oil storage cells.

</div><div data-tab="Jack-ups">

Self elevating mobile units with legs that stand on the seabed, used for drilling, accommodation and wind turbine installation.

</div></Tabs>

**Advantages of fixed structures:** very small motions, simple dry access to wells or equipment, well proven, no mooring systems. **Limitations:** cost and steel weight rise steeply with depth; installation needs heavy lift vessels and calm weather; removal at end of life is complex.

## Floating offshore structures

<Tabs><div data-tab="Semi-submersibles">

A deck supported by columns on submerged pontoons. Small waterplane area gives good motion characteristics; moored with spread mooring systems. Used for drilling, production, accommodation and many floating wind designs.

</div><div data-tab="Spars">

A deep draft vertical cylinder with ballast at the bottom, giving very stable motions; moored by catenary or taut lines. Used for oil production and floating wind.

</div><div data-tab="TLPs">

Excess buoyancy is resisted by vertical tendons anchored to the seabed, almost eliminating heave, pitch and roll. Used in oil and gas and proposed for wind.

</div><div data-tab="FPSO and FLNG">

Ship hulls with process plant, storage and offloading, often turret moored to weathervane.

</div></Tabs>

**Advantages of floating structures:** practical in deep water, can be fabricated and integrated at quayside, towed to site, and in some cases towed back for major repairs; less dependent on seabed conditions. **Limitations:** motions affect equipment, risers and people; moorings and dynamic cables are complex; hydrostatic stability and damage stability must be ensured, as in [ship stability](/marine-engineering/ship-stability-basics/).

## Fixed vs floating offshore structures: comparison

| Factor | Fixed | Floating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Support | Seabed via piles or gravity | Buoyancy plus moorings or tendons |
| Typical water depth | Shallow to moderate (wind up to about 60 to 80 m; oil and gas up to about 400 m) | Deep (wind beyond about 60 m; oil and gas to over 3000 m) |
| Motions | Very small | Six degrees of freedom; must be controlled |
| Installation | Heavy lift offshore, piling | Quayside assembly, tow out, mooring hook-up |
| Seabed dependency | High | Lower (anchors only) |
| Key design issues | Wave loads, fatigue, pile capacity, scour | Stability, motions, mooring and cable fatigue |
| Maturity in wind | Mature, widely used | Early commercial |

<More title="Catenary vs taut moorings">

Catenary moorings use long, heavy chain or wire lines that hang in a curve and lie partly on the seabed; their weight provides the restoring force. Taut moorings use lighter synthetic fibre ropes running at an angle to anchors and provide restoring force through stretch. Taut systems have a smaller footprint and are preferred in deep water.

</More>

## Loads and design

Wave forces on slender members of fixed structures are calculated with **Morison's equation**, combining drag and inertia terms:

$$
f = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_D D\,u|u| + \rho C_M \frac{\pi D^2}{4}\,\dot{u}
$$

Here f is the wave force per unit length of member, ρ the water density, C_D and C_M the drag and inertia coefficients, D the member diameter, u the water particle velocity and u̇ its acceleration.

Floaters are analysed for motions using hydrodynamic software, and their moorings for extreme and fatigue loads. Both must resist corrosion through coatings and cathodic protection, as discussed in [types of corrosion](/materials-engineering/types-of-corrosion/), and are designed for [fatigue failure](/materials-engineering/fatigue-failure/) under millions of wave cycles.

<dt>Find</dt><dd>The displaced volume and the ballast mass needed for equilibrium at the design draft.</dd>

<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Still water; vertical forces only.</dd>

<dt>Solution</dt><dd>Displaced volume = 12 000 ÷ 1.025 = 11 707 m³<br />Buoyancy force (mass) = 12 000 t must equal total weight: 4000 + 300 + ballast<br />Ballast = 12 000 − 4300 = 7700 t</dd>

<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>The hull displaces about 11 700 m³ and needs about 7700 t of water ballast to float at design draft</strong></dd>

<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Stability also depends on the vertical position of ballast and the turbine thrust overturning moment; active ballast systems may adjust trim in operation.</dd>

## Choosing between fixed vs floating offshore structures

Soil investigations, metocean data on waves, wind and currents, and port and vessel availability are gathered early, because they often decide the answer.

Engineers compare total lifecycle cost, including fabrication, transport, installation, operation, maintenance and decommissioning, together with technical risk and supply chain availability.

In offshore wind, most current capacity is fixed bottom in shallow seas, but a large share of the world's wind resource lies over deep water, so floating platforms are expected to grow quickly, as noted in [how wind turbines work](/renewable-energy/how-wind-turbines-work/). The choice is not either or: a region may use monopiles near shore, jackets further out and floating platforms beyond the continental shelf.

## Fixed vs floating offshore structures interview and viva questions

1. **How does a fixed offshore structure differ from a floating one?** A fixed structure transfers loads directly to the seabed through piles or its own weight, while a floating structure is supported by buoyancy and held on station by moorings or tendons.
2. **Up to what depth are monopiles and jackets used for offshore wind?** Monopiles suit water up to about 40 to 60 m and jackets up to about 60 to 80 m; beyond that, floating wind foundations become attractive.
3. **What is Morison's equation used for?** It gives the wave force on slender members of fixed structures as the sum of a drag term and an inertia term.
4. **Why does a tension leg platform have very small heave, pitch and roll?** Its excess buoyancy is resisted by vertical tendons anchored to the seabed, which almost eliminates these motions.
5. **What design issues dominate floating structures?** Hydrostatic and damage stability, wave induced motions, and mooring and dynamic cable fatigue dominate, while wave loads, fatigue, pile capacity and scour dominate fixed structures.
