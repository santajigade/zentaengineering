    a: "No. A positive GM is essential, but a very large GM makes a ship stiff: it rolls quickly and violently back to upright, which is uncomfortable, can damage cargo and stress lashings. <Key title="Short answer">

**Ship stability** is a ship's ability to return upright after being heeled by wind, waves or cargo shifts. Weight acts down through the **centre of gravity (G)** and buoyancy acts up through the **centre of buoyancy (B)**. When the ship heels, B moves towards the low side; for small angles the buoyancy line of action passes through the **metacentre (M)**. The **metacentric height GM = KM − KG** measures initial stability: if M is above G (positive GM), the ship rights itself. The **righting lever GZ ≈ GM sin θ** at small angles, and the **GZ curve** describes stability at large angles. The **free surface effect** of slack tanks reduces effective GM.

</Key>

Stability is a matter of life and death at sea. Many capsizes, from ferries to fishing boats, have resulted from poor loading, flooding, cargo shift or free surface effects. Every ship carries a stability booklet, and officers check stability before each voyage using hydrostatic data and loading computers.

## Key points and terms

- **K (keel):** reference point at the bottom of the hull.
- **G (centre of gravity):** where the total weight of ship, cargo, fuel and ballast acts. Its height above the keel is **KG**.
- **B (centre of buoyancy):** centroid of the underwater volume, where buoyancy acts. Height **KB**.
- **M (metacentre):** for small angles of heel, the point where the vertical through the heeled centre of buoyancy meets the centreline. Height **KM**.
- **BM (metacentric radius):** distance from B to M:

$$
BM = \frac{I_T}{\nabla}
$$

where I_T is the transverse second moment of area of the waterplane and ∇ the displaced volume. Wide ships have large I_T and therefore large BM.

- **GM (metacentric height):**

$$
GM = KB + BM - KG = KM - KG
$$

## Equilibrium conditions

- **Stable:** M above G (GM positive). Heeling creates a righting moment that returns the ship upright.
- **Neutral:** M coincides with G (GM zero). The ship stays at whatever angle it is pushed to.
- **Unstable:** M below G (GM negative). Heeling creates a capsizing moment; the ship lolls to an angle or capsizes.

## Righting lever and righting moment

At small angles (up to about 10 to 15°):

$$
GZ \approx GM\,\sin\theta
$$

The **righting moment** is:

$$
RM = \Delta \times GZ
$$

where Δ is the displacement (weight). At larger angles, the shape of the hull above and below the waterline matters, so GZ is calculated from hydrostatic **cross curves** (KN values): GZ = KN − KG sin θ.

## The GZ curve

The **curve of statical stability** plots GZ against heel angle. Important features:

- **Initial slope** equals GM (per radian).
- **Maximum GZ** and the angle at which it occurs.
- **Angle of vanishing stability**, beyond which GZ becomes negative and the ship capsizes.
- **Area under the curve** represents the energy needed to heel the ship and is a measure of dynamic stability.

Deck edge immersion and bilge emergence change the curve shape; freeboard and superstructure help large angle stability.

## Free surface effect

Liquid in a partly filled tank moves as the ship heels, shifting its centre of gravity sideways. This reduces GZ as if G had risen, a **virtual rise of G**:

$$
FSC = \frac{\rho_\text{liquid}\,i}{\Delta}
$$

where i is the second moment of area of the liquid surface about its centreline (for a rectangular tank i = l b³ ÷ 12) and Δ is the displacement mass. Effective **GM fluid = GM solid − FSC**. Wide tanks have very large effects because of the b³ term, which is why tanks are subdivided with longitudinal bulkheads.

## Inclining experiment

The lightship KG, the starting point for all stability calculations, is found by an **inclining experiment** when the ship is built or significantly modified. Known weights are moved a measured distance across the deck, and the resulting small heel angle is measured with pendulums or electronic inclinometers. From the shifting moment and the heel, GM is calculated: GM = (w × d) ÷ (Δ × tan θ). Knowing KM from hydrostatic tables then gives KG. Every later loading condition builds on this result.

## Longitudinal stability and trim

Ships also need longitudinal stability. Because the waterplane is long, the longitudinal metacentric height is very large, so ships do not capsize end over end, but loading changes **trim**, the difference between forward and aft drafts. Trim affects propeller immersion, speed, fuel consumption and visibility from the bridge, and is controlled with ballast.

## Practical ship stability management

- **Loading:** heavy cargo low, light cargo high; check KG, GM and the GZ curve against the stability booklet before departure.
- **Ballast:** add low ballast to lower G and increase GM; watch free surface during ballasting.
- **Consumables:** fuel and water use changes G and free surface during the voyage.
- **Cargo securing:** prevent shift of bulk cargo, containers and vehicles.
- **Icing and deck cargo** raise G; fishing vessels and offshore supply ships are particularly vulnerable.
- **Damage stability:** watertight subdivision under SOLAS ensures the ship survives specified flooding.

## Related principles

Ship stability is built on Archimedes' principle, the same physics that keeps [offshore platforms](/petroleum-engineering/offshore-platform-types/) afloat and governs [fixed vs floating offshore structures](/marine-engineering/fixed-vs-floating-offshore-structures/). Stability is also part of the safety framework set by [SOLAS](/marine-engineering/solas-lifesaving-firefighting/), and good ship stability, together with reliable propulsion from the [two-stroke marine diesel engine](/marine-engineering/two-stroke-marine-diesel-engine/), keeps ships safe in heavy weather.
