<Key title="Short answer">

The **Bernoulli equation** says that for steady, incompressible, frictionless flow along a streamline, pressure energy plus kinetic energy plus potential energy is constant: p + ½ρv² + ρgz = constant. Where the fluid speeds up, its pressure falls. It is the basis of venturi meters, pitot tubes, orifice plates and many pipe flow calculations.

</Key>

The Bernoulli equation is the most used result in fluid mechanics. It links the pressure, velocity and height of a moving fluid with one simple expression. It explains why water speeds up through a nozzle, how a differential pressure flowmeter works and how much pressure a pump must add to lift water. Used carelessly, it also gives wrong answers, so its assumptions matter as much as the formula.

## The Bernoulli equation

Between two points 1 and 2 on the same streamline:

$$
p_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 + \rho g z_1 = p_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2 + \rho g z_2
$$

where p is static pressure (Pa), ρ is density (kg/m³), v is velocity (m/s), g is 9.81 m/s² and z is elevation (m). Each term is an energy per unit volume, so each has units of pressure.

Dividing by ρg gives the **head form** used by pump and piping engineers, where every term is a height of fluid in metres:

$$
\frac{p}{\rho g} + \frac{v^2}{2g} + z = H = \text{constant}
$$

- **Pressure head** p / ρg: the height of fluid column the pressure could support. The [hydrostatic pressure formula](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/hydrostatic-pressure-formula/) p = ρgh is the same relation at rest.
- **Velocity head** v² / 2g: the kinetic energy per unit weight.
- **Elevation head** z: potential energy per unit weight above a datum.

The sum H is the total head. Static pressure plus ½ρv² is called **stagnation pressure**, the pressure measured where the flow is brought to rest.

## Simple derivation from energy

Consider a small slug of fluid of mass m and volume V moving through a pipe from section 1 to section 2 in steady flow. The pressure at section 1 pushes the fluid in and does work p₁V on it. At section 2 the fluid downstream resists with pressure p₂ and the slug does work p₂V. With no friction and no heat transfer, the net work equals the gain in kinetic and potential energy:

$$
(p_1 - p_2)V = \tfrac{1}{2}m(v_2^2 - v_1^2) + mg(z_2 - z_1)
$$

Dividing by V and using m / V = ρ gives the Bernoulli equation. The same result follows from applying Newton's second law along a streamline (Euler's equation) and integrating, or from the steady flow energy equation of the [first law of thermodynamics](/mechanical-engineering/laws-of-thermodynamics/) for an incompressible fluid with no heat transfer and no shaft work.

## Assumptions and limits

The simple form holds only when all of these are reasonable:

1. **Steady flow:** conditions at a point do not change with time.
2. **Incompressible fluid:** density constant. Liquids qualify; gases qualify below about Mach 0.3.
3. **Frictionless (inviscid) flow:** no viscous losses between the two points.
4. **Along a streamline:** the two points lie on the same streamline (or the flow is irrotational).
5. **No machines or heat transfer:** no pump, fan or turbine between the points, and no heating.

Real pipelines violate assumption 3, so engineers use the **extended energy equation**, adding pump head and subtracting friction and fitting losses:

$$
\frac{p_1}{\rho g} + \frac{v_1^2}{2g} + z_1 + h_{\text{pump}} = \frac{p_2}{\rho g} + \frac{v_2^2}{2g} + z_2 + h_L
$$

The head loss hL is found from the Darcy-Weisbach equation and loss coefficients for valves and fittings. This form is used to size pumps; see the [centrifugal pump working principle](/mechanical-engineering/centrifugal-pump-working-principle/).

## Venturi meter: the Bernoulli equation at work

A venturi narrows the pipe to a throat and then widens it gradually. Continuity (Q = A₁v₁ = A₂v₂) says velocity rises in the throat, and Bernoulli says pressure falls. Measuring that pressure difference gives the flow rate.

Combining continuity with Bernoulli for a horizontal venturi gives the ideal flow rate:

$$
Q = A_2 \sqrt{\frac{2\,\Delta p}{\rho\left(1-(A_2/A_1)^2\right)}}
$$

Real meters multiply this by a discharge coefficient Cd (about 0.98 to 0.99 for a classical venturi under ISO 5167-4) to account for friction. Orifice plates use the same principle with a lower Cd. Flow increases with the square root of differential pressure, which is why DP transmitters apply square root extraction. More on DP flow devices, such as the [elbow flow meter](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/elbow-flow-meter-working-principle/), is on InstrumentationBlog.in.

## Other applications

- **Pitot tube:** measures stagnation minus static pressure, giving velocity v = √(2Δp / ρ). Used in ducts and on aircraft.
- **Flow from a tank (Torricelli):** a jet leaving an orifice at depth h has velocity √(2gh), before losses.
- **Siphons and suction lines:** low pressure at high points can drop below vapour pressure, causing cavitation.
- **Spray nozzles and ejectors:** high velocity creates low pressure that draws in a second fluid.

The [flow rate formula](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/flow-rate-formula/) Q = A × v, together with the Bernoulli equation, solves most basic flow problems.

## Energy and hydraulic grade lines

Piping engineers often draw the Bernoulli equation as two lines along a pipe. The **energy grade line** plots total head H; the **hydraulic grade line** plots pressure head plus elevation, which is what a piezometer tube would show. The gap between them is the velocity head. Without friction the energy grade line is horizontal. In a real pipe it slopes down in the flow direction because of friction, jumps up at a pump and drops at valves and fittings. If the hydraulic grade line falls below the pipe itself, the pressure there is below atmospheric.

## Common mistakes

- Applying it across a pump or a partly closed valve without adding pump head or losses.
- Mixing gauge and absolute pressures between the two points.
- Using it for high speed gas flow where density changes.
- Forgetting that friction makes the downstream total head lower, never higher.
