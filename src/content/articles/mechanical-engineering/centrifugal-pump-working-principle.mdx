<Key title="Short answer">

**Centrifugal pump working principle:** a rotating impeller throws liquid outwards, giving it kinetic energy. The volute casing around the impeller then slows the liquid down and converts that velocity into pressure. Liquid leaving the impeller eye creates low pressure that draws more liquid in through the suction. The result is a continuous flow at a higher pressure (head).

</Key>

Centrifugal pumps move more liquid than any other pump type. They supply water to buildings, circulate cooling water, feed boilers and transfer chemicals in process plants. They are simple, compact and have no valves inside, but their performance depends strongly on how they are installed and operated. This article explains how the pump works, its main parts and the key terms that appear on a pump datasheet.

The energy conversion happens in three steps:

1. **Suction:** liquid flows axially into the centre of the impeller, called the eye. As the impeller pushes liquid outwards, pressure at the eye falls below the suction pressure, so more liquid flows in.
2. **Energy addition:** the impeller vanes, driven by the motor, force the liquid to rotate and move outwards. Both its pressure and its velocity rise as it travels to the impeller tip. The ideal head added is described by the Euler pump equation, which depends on tip speed and the swirl velocity given to the liquid.
3. **Pressure recovery:** liquid leaves the impeller at high speed into the volute, a spiral casing whose area grows towards the discharge. As the area grows the velocity falls and, following the [Bernoulli equation](/mechanical-engineering/bernoulli-equation/), kinetic energy turns into pressure. Some pumps use a ring of stationary diffuser vanes instead of, or as well as, a volute.

Because the head comes from rotation, a centrifugal pump develops roughly the same head with any liquid of low viscosity, but the pressure in kPa is proportional to the liquid's density.

## Main parts of a centrifugal pump

- **Impeller:** the rotating wheel with vanes, usually curved backwards relative to rotation. Closed impellers have shrouds on both sides for efficiency; open and semi-open impellers handle solids better.
- **Casing (volute or diffuser):** collects the liquid and converts velocity to pressure. Split casings allow inspection without disturbing pipework.
- **Shaft:** carries the impeller and transmits torque from the driver, normally an [induction motor](/electrical-engineering/induction-motor-working-principle/).
- **Bearings:** support the shaft against radial and axial loads; their life depends on correct [lubrication](/mechanical-engineering/types-of-lubrication/).
- **Shaft seal:** a gland packing or a mechanical seal stops leakage where the shaft leaves the casing.
- **Wear rings:** small clearance rings between impeller and casing that limit internal recirculation from discharge back to suction.
- **Suction and discharge nozzles:** flanged connections to the pipework.

## Head, flow and the pump curve

Pump performance is stated as **head H** (metres of liquid) against **flow Q** (m³/h) at a fixed speed. A typical radial flow pump curve shows head highest at zero flow (shut-off head) and falling as flow increases. Power rises with flow, and efficiency peaks at the **best efficiency point (BEP)**. Pumps should run near the BEP; far left or right of it, vibration, recirculation and seal wear increase.

The operating point is where the pump curve crosses the **system curve**, which is the static head plus friction losses that rise with flow squared. Throttling a discharge valve or changing speed moves the operating point. Speed changes follow the affinity laws: Q varies with speed, H with speed squared and power with speed cubed.

The power delivered to the liquid and the shaft power are:

$$
P_{\text{hydraulic}} = \rho g Q H \qquad P_{\text{shaft}} = \frac{\rho g Q H}{\eta}
$$

This is the [first law of thermodynamics](/mechanical-engineering/laws-of-thermodynamics/) applied to the pump: shaft work becomes pressure energy plus losses that slightly warm the liquid.

## NPSH and cavitation

If the pressure at the impeller eye falls below the liquid's vapour pressure, vapour bubbles form and then collapse violently as pressure rises along the vane. This is **cavitation**. It causes noise, vibration, loss of head and pitting of the impeller.

To prevent it, the **net positive suction head available (NPSHa)** from the installation must exceed the **NPSH required (NPSHr)** shown on the pump curve, with a safety margin. NPSHa is increased by raising the suction tank level, using shorter and larger suction pipes, and keeping the liquid cool. Hydraulic Institute guidance recommends margins that depend on the application.

## Priming and operation

- **Prime before starting:** fill the casing and suction pipe with liquid unless the pump is self-priming. A foot valve helps keep a suction lift line full.
- **Start against a nearly closed discharge** for radial pumps to limit starting power, then open the valve.
- **Never run dry:** mechanical seals depend on the liquid for cooling and lubrication.
- **Avoid long running at shut-off** or very low flow; a minimum flow bypass protects pumps that may see low demand.
- **Monitor** suction and discharge pressure, flow, vibration and bearing temperature. Field [pressure measurement](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/pressure-measurement/) and [flow measurement](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/flow-measurement/) instruments make this possible.
