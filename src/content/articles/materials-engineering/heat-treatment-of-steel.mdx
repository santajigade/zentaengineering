  - q: "Why must hardened steel be tempered?"
    a: "As-quenched martensite is very hard but brittle and full of internal stresses, so it can crack in service or even while sitting on the shelf. <Key title="Short answer">

**Heat treatment of steel** changes its microstructure, and therefore its properties, by controlled heating and cooling. **Annealing** (heating into the austenite range and cooling slowly in the furnace) softens steel and relieves stress. **Normalizing** (heating and cooling in air) refines grains and gives moderate strength. **Hardening** (heating to austenite and **quenching** rapidly in water, oil or polymer) forms hard, brittle **martensite**. **Tempering** (reheating hardened steel to 150 to 650 °C) restores toughness at the cost of some hardness. **Case hardening** processes such as carburising and nitriding harden only the surface (compared in [case hardening methods](/materials-engineering/case-hardening-methods/)).

</Key>

The same steel can be soft enough to bend into shape, or hard enough to cut other metals, depending on how it is heat treated. Heat treatment lets engineers machine parts in a soft condition, then harden them for wear resistance, or relieve stresses after welding. Understanding it starts with the [iron-carbon phase diagram](/materials-engineering/iron-carbon-phase-diagram/) and continues with how cooling rate controls the transformation of austenite.

## Why cooling rate matters: TTT and CCT diagrams

Above the critical temperature, steel is austenite. On cooling, austenite transforms, and the product depends on how fast carbon atoms can diffuse:

- **Slow cooling:** coarse pearlite (soft).
- **Moderate cooling:** fine pearlite (stronger).
- **Faster cooling or isothermal holding around 250 to 550 °C:** bainite (strong and tough).
- **Very rapid cooling (quenching):** carbon is trapped, and austenite shears into **martensite**, a body-centred tetragonal structure that is very hard.

**Time-temperature-transformation (TTT)** diagrams show these transformations at constant temperature, and **continuous cooling transformation (CCT)** diagrams show them for real cooling paths. The **critical cooling rate** is the minimum rate that just avoids the pearlite "nose" and produces fully martensitic steel.

## 1. Annealing

**Full annealing:** heat about 30 to 50 °C above the upper critical temperature (A3) for hypoeutectoid steels, soak until uniform, then cool slowly in the furnace. Produces coarse pearlite and ferrite: maximum softness and ductility, good machinability and relief of internal stresses.

Other types:

- **Process (subcritical) annealing:** heating below A1 to restore ductility in cold worked low carbon steel between drawing operations.
- **Spheroidise annealing:** long holding just below A1 turns cementite into small spheres, giving the softest condition for high carbon steels before machining or forming.
- **Stress relief annealing:** heating to about 550 to 650 °C after welding, machining or cold work, then slow cooling, to reduce residual stresses without major structural change.

## 2. Normalizing

Heat about 50 °C above A3 (or Acm for hypereutectoid steels) and cool in still air. The faster cooling gives a finer, more uniform pearlite and grain size than annealing, improving strength and toughness. Normalizing is used to refine coarse structures in castings and forgings and to give a consistent starting structure before hardening.

## 3. Hardening and quenching

Heat into the austenite region (austenitising), hold long enough for uniform temperature and carbon distribution, then **quench** fast enough to form martensite.

- **Quenchants** in order of severity: brine, water, polymer solutions, oil, molten salt, air (for highly alloyed air hardening steels).
- **Maximum hardness** depends mainly on carbon content: about 0.6% C or more gives around 60 to 65 HRC.
- **Hardenability** determines the depth of hardening and depends on alloying elements and grain size, measured by the Jominy end quench test.
- **Risks:** distortion and quench cracking from thermal and transformation stresses. Milder quenchants, proper part design and **martempering** (quench into a hot salt bath, equalise, then air cool) reduce them.

## 4. Tempering

Hardened steel is reheated to a temperature below A1, held (often 1 to 2 hours) and cooled. Carbon precipitates as fine carbides and stresses relax.

| Tempering temperature | Typical result | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 150 to 250 °C | High hardness, slight toughness gain | Cutting tools, gauges, bearings |
| 350 to 500 °C | Medium hardness, good toughness and elasticity | Springs, chisels |
| 500 to 650 °C | Lower hardness, high toughness (sorbite) | Shafts, gears, bolts, axles |

Hardening plus high temperature tempering is called **quenching and tempering (Q&T)**, the standard route for high strength structural and machine parts. Some steels show **temper embrittlement** in certain temperature ranges, which is avoided by alloy choice and tempering practice.

## 5. Surface (case) hardening

Many parts need a hard, wear resistant surface with a tough core.

- **Carburising:** low carbon steel is heated in a carbon rich atmosphere at about 900 to 950 °C to raise surface carbon, then quenched and tempered. Case depths typically 0.5 to 2 mm. Used for gears and camshafts.
- **Carbonitriding:** adds nitrogen as well as carbon, at lower temperatures.
- **Nitriding:** nitrogen diffuses into alloy steel at about 500 to 550 °C, forming hard nitrides with no quench needed and minimal distortion.
- **Induction and flame hardening:** only the surface of a medium carbon steel is rapidly heated and quenched; common for shafts, gear teeth and rails.

## Quality control in heat treatment

Results are checked with **hardness tests** (Rockwell, Brinell, Vickers), microstructure examination, case depth measurements and, for critical parts, [tensile tests](/materials-engineering/tensile-test/) and impact tests. Furnace temperature uniformity and accurate [temperature measurement](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/temperature-measurements/) with calibrated thermocouples are essential; aerospace standards such as AMS 2750 specify furnace surveys and instrument accuracy. Atmosphere control prevents decarburisation and scaling of the surface.

## Summary of heat treatment of steel

Heat treatment of steel is a powerful tool: with the right cycle, one grade of steel can serve as a soft blank for machining and then as a hard, tough, fatigue resistant component. Choosing the process requires knowing the steel composition, section size and required properties, and controlling temperatures, times and cooling rates carefully. Proper heat treatment also improves resistance to [fatigue failure](/materials-engineering/fatigue-failure/) and wear in service.
