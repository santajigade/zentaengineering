    a: "In normal steels, carbon above the solubility limit forms iron carbide (Fe₃C, cementite) rather than graphite, even though graphite is the more stable form. <Key title="Short answer">

The **iron-carbon phase diagram** (strictly the Fe-Fe₃C diagram) shows which phases are stable in iron-carbon alloys at each temperature and carbon content under slow, near-equilibrium cooling. Key phases are **ferrite (α)**, soft and with very low carbon solubility; **austenite (γ)**, the face-centred cubic phase stable at high temperature; **cementite (Fe₃C)**, hard and brittle; and **pearlite**, a lamellar mixture of ferrite and cementite. Key points are the **eutectoid** at **0.76% C and 727 °C** (austenite → pearlite) and the **eutectic** at **4.3% C and 1147 °C** (liquid → austenite + cementite). Steels lie below about **2.14% C**, cast irons above.

</Key>

Steel is the most important engineering metal, and its properties can range from soft and ductile to extremely hard, depending on carbon content and heat treatment. The iron-carbon diagram is the map that explains why. It tells metallurgists which phases form when steel is heated or cooled, and it underpins casting, forging, welding and [heat treatment of steel](/materials-engineering/heat-treatment-of-steel/).

## Allotropy of iron

Pure iron changes crystal structure with temperature:

- **α-iron (ferrite):** body-centred cubic (BCC), stable up to 912 °C; magnetic below 770 °C (the Curie point).
- **γ-iron (austenite):** face-centred cubic (FCC), stable from 912 to 1394 °C.
- **δ-iron (delta ferrite):** BCC again, from 1394 °C to the melting point of 1538 °C.

FCC austenite has larger interstitial spaces and dissolves much more carbon (up to 2.14%) than BCC ferrite (at most 0.022%). This difference drives most steel heat treatment.

## Phases and microconstituents

- **Ferrite (α):** BCC iron with up to 0.022% C at 727 °C. Soft, ductile, magnetic.
- **Austenite (γ):** FCC iron with up to 2.14% C at 1147 °C. Ductile, non-magnetic; not stable at room temperature in plain carbon steels.
- **Cementite (Fe₃C):** iron carbide with 6.67% C. Very hard and brittle.
- **Pearlite:** not a phase but a microconstituent: alternating thin plates (lamellae) of ferrite and cementite formed from austenite at the eutectoid. Strong and moderately ductile.
- **Ledeburite:** the eutectic mixture of austenite and cementite in cast irons.

## Key points and lines

| Point or line | Composition | Temperature | Reaction or meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Eutectoid point | 0.76% C | 727 °C | γ → α + Fe₃C (pearlite) |
| Eutectic point | 4.3% C | 1147 °C | Liquid → γ + Fe₃C (ledeburite) |
| Peritectic point | 0.17% C | 1493 °C | Liquid + δ → γ |
| Maximum C in austenite | 2.14% C | 1147 °C | Boundary between steels and cast irons |
| A1 line | | 727 °C | Lower critical temperature |
| A3 line | Below 0.76% C | 912 to 727 °C | Ferrite starts forming on cooling |
| Acm line | Above 0.76% C | 1147 to 727 °C | Cementite starts forming on cooling |

## Slow cooling of steels

- **Eutectoid steel (0.76% C):** austenite transforms entirely to pearlite at 727 °C.
- **Hypoeutectoid steel (below 0.76% C):** on cooling below the A3 line, **proeutectoid ferrite** forms at austenite grain boundaries; at 727 °C the remaining austenite, now at 0.76% C, transforms to pearlite. Structure: ferrite plus pearlite. Most structural steels are hypoeutectoid.
- **Hypereutectoid steel (0.76 to 2.14% C):** below the Acm line, **proeutectoid cementite** forms along grain boundaries; the rest becomes pearlite at 727 °C. Structure: pearlite plus cementite network, hard but less tough. Used in tool steels.

## The lever rule

In a two-phase region, the fraction of each phase is found with the **lever rule**. For an alloy of composition C₀ between phase compositions C_α and C_β:

$$
W_\alpha = \frac{C_\beta - C_0}{C_\beta - C_\alpha} \qquad W_\beta = \frac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_\beta - C_\alpha}
$$

<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>A 0.40% C plain carbon steel is slowly cooled to just below 727 °C.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>The fractions of proeutectoid ferrite and pearlite, and the total fractions of ferrite and cementite.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Equilibrium cooling; ferrite contains 0.022% C; cementite 6.67% C; eutectoid 0.76% C.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>Pearlite fraction = (0.40 − 0.022) ÷ (0.76 − 0.022) = 0.378 ÷ 0.738 = 0.51<br />Proeutectoid ferrite = 1 − 0.51 = 0.49<br />Total cementite = (0.40 − 0.022) ÷ (6.67 − 0.022) = 0.378 ÷ 6.648 = 0.057<br />Total ferrite = 1 − 0.057 = 0.943</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>About 49% proeutectoid ferrite and 51% pearlite; overall about 94% ferrite and 6% cementite</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Real cooling is faster than equilibrium and alloying elements shift the lines, so actual microstructures differ.</dd>

## Cast irons

Alloys above 2.14% C are cast irons. Their low melting point and good fluidity make them easy to cast. Depending on composition and cooling rate, carbon appears as cementite (**white cast iron**, hard and brittle) or as graphite: flakes in **grey cast iron** (good damping and machinability), spheres in **ductile (nodular) iron** (good toughness), or compact clusters in **malleable iron**.

## Effect of alloying elements and cooling rate

Alloying elements shift the diagram: nickel and manganese stabilise austenite (enough nickel gives austenitic stainless steels at room temperature), while chromium, molybdenum and silicon stabilise ferrite. Faster cooling than equilibrium suppresses diffusion, producing finer pearlite, bainite or martensite. These non-equilibrium products are described by TTT and CCT diagrams and are the basis of hardening.

## Reading the diagram step by step

To use the diagram for a given steel, draw a vertical line at its carbon content. Follow it down from the liquid region: where it crosses a boundary, a new phase begins to form. In a two phase region, draw a horizontal tie line at the temperature of interest; its ends give the compositions of the two phases, and the lever rule gives their amounts. At the eutectoid temperature, any remaining austenite transforms to pearlite. This simple procedure predicts the slow cooled microstructure and therefore the approximate strength and ductility.

## Using the iron-carbon phase diagram in practice

Keep in mind that the diagram describes equilibrium; real heat treatment cycles are faster, so it is a guide rather than an exact prediction.


Engineers use the diagram to choose heat treatment temperatures (for example, austenitising 30 to 50 °C above A3 for hypoeutectoid steels), hot working ranges, and to understand weld heat affected zones. Combined with mechanical tests such as the [tensile test](/materials-engineering/tensile-test/), it explains why a 0.2% C structural steel used in [steel sections](/civil-engineering/steel-sections-types/) is ductile and weldable, while a 1.0% C tool steel is hard and wear resistant. It is the foundation of understanding the [classification of engineering materials](/materials-engineering/engineering-materials-classification/) within the steel family.
