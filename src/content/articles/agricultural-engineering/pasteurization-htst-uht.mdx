<Key title="Short answer">

**Pasteurization** is a mild heat treatment that kills pathogens and reduces spoilage organisms in milk, juices and other liquid foods, while keeping flavour and nutrients. Batch pasteurization holds milk at 63 °C for 30 minutes, HTST heats it to 72 °C for 15 seconds continuously, and UHT reaches about 135 to 150 °C for 2 to 10 seconds.

</Key>

Louis Pasteur showed in the 1860s that gentle heating prevents wine and beer from spoiling. Applied to milk from the late 19th century, pasteurization dramatically reduced diseases such as tuberculosis, brucellosis and typhoid that were spread by raw milk. Today it is one of the most important food safety processes, legally required for most milk sold in many countries.

## How heat kills microorganisms

Microbial death during heating is approximately logarithmic. The **D value** is the time at a given temperature needed to reduce a population by 90% (one log):

$$
\log \frac{N_0}{N} = \frac{t}{D_T}
$$

The **z value** is the temperature increase that reduces D by a factor of 10. For many vegetative pathogens z is about 5 to 8 °C, while for quality changes such as browning and vitamin loss z is larger, about 20 to 40 °C. This difference is why **high temperature, short time** processes kill microbes effectively while causing less quality damage.

Pasteurization targets heat resistant pathogens such as *Coxiella burnetii* and *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* in milk. Enzyme tests, especially **alkaline phosphatase**, verify adequate pasteurization because the enzyme is destroyed at similar conditions.

## Pasteurization methods

| Method | Typical conditions (milk) | Equipment | Shelf life | Storage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Batch (LTLT, vat) | 63 °C for 30 min | Jacketed vat | About 1 to 2 weeks | Refrigerated |
| HTST | 72 °C for 15 s | Plate heat exchanger, holding tube | About 2 to 3 weeks | Refrigerated |

1. **Balance tank:** keeps a constant supply and level, preventing air entry.
2. **Regeneration section:** cold raw milk (about 4 °C) is preheated by hot pasteurized milk flowing on the other side of the plates, recovering up to about 90 to 95% of the heat.
3. **Heating section:** hot water or steam raises milk to at least 72 °C. Plate design follows the principles in [heat exchanger types](/chemical-engineering/heat-exchanger-types/).
4. **Holding tube:** a pipe sized so that the fastest particle spends at least 15 seconds at temperature. Length depends on flow rate and pipe diameter.
5. **Flow diversion valve (FDV):** a temperature recorder controller checks product temperature at the end of the holding tube; if it is below the legal limit, the valve diverts milk back to the balance tank.
6. **Regeneration (pasteurized side) and cooling section:** pasteurized milk gives up heat to incoming raw milk, then chilled water or glycol cools it to about 4 °C for filling.

Pressure on the pasteurized side is kept higher than on the raw side, often with a booster pump, so that any plate leak flows from pasteurized to raw milk, never the reverse. Accurate [temperature measurement](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/temperature-measurements/), calibrated recorders and flow measurement are legal requirements.

## Holding tube sizing

$$
L = v_\text{max} \times t_\text{hold} \qquad v_\text{avg} = \frac{Q}{A}
$$

For laminar flow in a tube, the fastest particle moves at about twice the average velocity; for turbulent flow, about 1.2 times.

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>An HTST unit pasteurizes 10 000 L/h of milk at 72 °C for 15 s in a holding tube of 51 mm internal diameter. Flow is turbulent; assume the fastest particle moves at 1.2 times the average velocity.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>Average velocity and the minimum holding tube length.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Constant flow rate guaranteed by a timing pump; no allowance for fittings.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>Q = 10 000 ÷ 3600 ÷ 1000 = 0.00278 m³/s<br />A = π × 0.051² ÷ 4 = 0.002043 m²<br />v_avg = 0.00278 ÷ 0.002043 = 1.36 m/s<br />v_max = 1.2 × 1.36 = 1.63 m/s<br />L = 1.63 × 15 = 24.5 m</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>Average velocity about 1.36 m/s; the holding tube must be at least about 24.5 m long</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Regulators require verification by a salt conductivity or dye test that the fastest particle holding time meets the minimum.</dd>
</dl>

## Key formulas for pasteurization

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Log reduction | log (N0 ÷ N) = t ÷ D_T | N0, N = initial and surviving counts, t = time, D_T = D value at temperature T (same time unit) |
| Average velocity in holding tube | v_avg = Q ÷ A | Q in m³/s, A in m², v in m/s |
| Tube cross section | A = π × d² ÷ 4 | d = internal diameter in m |
| Fastest particle velocity | v_max = 2 × v_avg (laminar), 1.2 × v_avg (turbulent) | m/s |
| Holding tube length | L = v_max × t_hold | L in m, t_hold in s |

## Pasteurization of other foods

The same principles apply beyond milk. Fruit juices are often flash pasteurized at about 85 to 95 °C for 15 to 30 seconds, because their acidity limits the hazards that can grow. Liquid egg, beer, ice cream mix and some sauces are also pasteurized, each with conditions set for the target organisms and product quality. Non-thermal methods such as high pressure processing and pulsed electric fields are used for some premium juices to preserve fresh flavour.

## UHT processing

UHT products must be handled with even stricter hygiene, since any contamination after sterilisation can grow during months of ambient storage before the pack is opened.


UHT systems heat product to 135 to 150 °C either **directly** (steam injection or infusion, then flash cooling in a vacuum vessel to remove the added water) or **indirectly** in tubular or plate heat exchangers. Product is then cooled and filled under **aseptic** conditions into pre-sterilised packages such as multilayer cartons. The result is commercially sterile: no microorganisms capable of growing under normal storage conditions.

## Pasteurization and food safety systems

Pasteurization is a classic **critical control point** in a [HACCP](/agricultural-engineering/haccp-principles/) plan, with critical limits for temperature and time, continuous monitoring and recording, automatic diversion as corrective action, and regular verification by phosphatase tests, calibration and record review. Hygienic design, **CIP (clean in place)** cleaning and prevention of post-pasteurization contamination are equally important. Pasteurized milk must then be kept cold through the chain, as discussed in [cold storage design basics](/agricultural-engineering/cold-storage-design-basics/).

## Pasteurization interview and viva questions

1. **What is the D value?** It is the time at a given temperature needed to reduce a microbial population by 90%, or one log cycle.
2. **Why do high temperature, short time processes protect quality?** Vegetative pathogens have a z value of about 5 to 8 °C, while quality changes have about 20 to 40 °C, so raising the temperature speeds microbial death much more than quality damage.
3. **Why is the holding tube sized on the fastest particle?** Every particle must spend at least the legal time at temperature, and the fastest one moves at about 1.2 times the average velocity in turbulent flow or about twice in laminar flow.
4. **Why is the pasteurized side kept at higher pressure than the raw side?** If a plate leaks, flow then goes from pasteurized to raw milk, so raw milk can never contaminate the finished product.
5. **How is adequate pasteurization of milk verified?** The alkaline phosphatase test is used, because this enzyme is destroyed at conditions similar to those needed to kill the target pathogens.
