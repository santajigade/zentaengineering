<Key title="Short answer">

**Irrigation water requirement** is the depth of water that irrigation must supply, on top of effective rainfall, for a crop to meet its evapotranspiration. Following FAO-56, crop water use is ETc = Kc × ETo. The net requirement is ETc minus effective rainfall, and the gross requirement is the net value divided by the irrigation efficiency.

</Key>

Getting this number right sizes everything in an irrigation scheme: pumps, pipes, canals, reservoirs and the irrigation schedule. Too low a design figure leaves crops stressed in the peak month; too high a figure wastes capital and water. This article walks through the standard FAO method step by step and finishes with a worked example.

## Components of the irrigation water requirement

The method set out by Allen and co-authors in FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56, and taught in the FAO training manuals, builds the requirement in layers:

1. **Reference evapotranspiration (ETo):** the evaporative demand of the climate.
2. **Crop evapotranspiration (ETc):** ETo adjusted for the crop and its growth stage with a crop coefficient Kc.
3. **Effective rainfall (Pe):** the part of rainfall the crop can actually use.
4. **Net irrigation requirement (IRn):** what irrigation must add to the root zone.
5. **Gross irrigation requirement (IRg):** what must be delivered, allowing for losses.

Other terms, such as leaching water to control salinity, water for land preparation of rice, or a contribution from a shallow water table, are added or subtracted where they apply.

## Reference evapotranspiration

FAO-56 defines ETo as the evapotranspiration from a hypothetical grass reference crop with a height of 0.12 m, a fixed surface resistance of 70 s/m and an albedo of 0.23, actively growing and not short of water. It is calculated with the **FAO Penman-Monteith equation** using daily or monthly radiation, maximum and minimum temperature, humidity and wind speed. Where only temperature data exist, FAO-56 allows the Hargreaves equation as a fallback. ETo is usually expressed in mm/day and varies with season and climate.

## Crop coefficient and crop water use

Crop evapotranspiration under standard conditions is:

$$
ET_c = K_c \times ET_o
$$

The crop coefficient Kc changes over four growth stages, as shown below. FAO-56 Table 12 gives typical values; for grain maize, for example, Kc ini is 0.30, Kc mid is 1.20 and Kc end is 0.60 or 0.35 depending on whether the crop is harvested at high grain moisture or dried in the field. These table values are adjusted for local climate, mainly wind speed and minimum relative humidity, for crops taller than the reference grass.

## Effective rainfall

Not all rain is useful. Some runs off, some percolates below the roots, and light showers evaporate from leaves and soil. The FAO training manual on irrigation water needs (Brouwer and Heibloem) gives a simple empirical estimate of monthly effective rainfall P in mm:

$$
P_e = 0.8P - 25 \quad \text{for } P > 75 \text{ mm/month}
$$

$$
P_e = 0.6P - 10 \quad \text{for } P \le 75 \text{ mm/month}
$$

with Pe taken as zero if the formula gives a negative value. The USDA NRCS method in the National Engineering Handbook is an alternative that also accounts for soil water storage. In dry seasons effective rainfall is small and the irrigation water requirement approaches ETc.

## Net and gross irrigation water requirement

The net irrigation requirement for a period is:

$$
IR_n = ET_c - P_e
$$

Irrigation is never perfectly efficient, so more water must be applied than the crop uses. The FAO training manual on irrigation scheduling gives indicative field application efficiencies of about **60 percent for surface irrigation, 75 percent for sprinkler irrigation and 90 percent for drip irrigation**. The gross requirement is:

$$
IR_g = \frac{IR_n}{E_a}
$$

For a full scheme, the conveyance and distribution losses in canals and pipes are also included, giving a lower overall scheme efficiency. The difference between methods is discussed in [drip vs sprinkler irrigation](/agricultural-engineering/drip-vs-sprinkler-irrigation/).

Finally, convert depth to volume. A depth of 1 mm over 1 hectare is 10 m³, so:

$$
V\,(\text{m}^3) = 10 \times IR_g\,(\text{mm}) \times A\,(\text{ha})
$$

## Key formulas for irrigation water requirement

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crop evapotranspiration | ETc = Kc × ETo | ETc and ETo in mm/day, Kc = crop coefficient (dimensionless) |
| Effective rainfall, P above 75 mm | Pe = 0.8P - 25 | P and Pe in mm/month, Pe taken as zero if negative |
| Effective rainfall, P up to 75 mm | Pe = 0.6P - 10 | P and Pe in mm/month |
| Net irrigation requirement | IRn = ETc - Pe | depths in mm for the period |
| Gross irrigation requirement | IRg = IRn ÷ Ea | Ea = field application efficiency (fraction) |
| Volume | V = 10 × IRg × A | V in m³, IRg in mm, A in ha |
| System flow | Q = V ÷ operating hours | Q in m³/h; divide by 3.6 for L/s |

## Worked example: peak month irrigation water requirement

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>Grain maize on 10 ha in its mid-season month (30 days). ETo = 6.0 mm/day; Kc mid = 1.20; monthly rainfall P = 60 mm. Sprinkler irrigation with application efficiency 75 percent. The system can run 20 hours per day.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>ETc, effective rainfall, net and gross irrigation requirement, monthly volume and the required system flow.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Standard (unstressed) crop conditions; no leaching requirement; no water table contribution; FAO training manual effective rainfall formula.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>ETc = 1.20 × 6.0 = 7.2 mm/day; for 30 days = 7.2 × 30 = 216 mm<br />P = 60 mm, which is 75 mm or less, so Pe = 0.6 × 60 - 10 = 26 mm<br />IRn = 216 - 26 = 190 mm<br />IRg = 190 ÷ 0.75 = 253.3 mm<br />Volume = 10 × 253.3 × 10 = 25 333 m³<br />Operating hours = 20 × 30 = 600 h<br />Flow = 25 333 ÷ 600 = 42.2 m³/h = 42.2 ÷ 3.6 = 11.7 L/s</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>Net requirement 190 mm and gross requirement about 253 mm for the month, which is about 25 300 m³ on 10 ha, needing a system flow of about 42 m³/h (11.7 L/s)</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Monthly averages hide short peaks, so designs often use a peak period ETo. The Kc value should be adjusted for local wind and humidity, and soil water holding capacity sets how often irrigation is applied.</dd>
</dl>
</Example>

The flow found here is the design duty for the pump and mainline. The pump selection that follows is covered in [centrifugal pump working principle](/mechanical-engineering/centrifugal-pump-working-principle/).

## From requirement to schedule

The monthly irrigation water requirement tells you how much water is needed; the **schedule** tells you when and how much per application. FAO-56 uses the soil's total available water, the crop root depth and a depletion fraction to set the readily available water. Irrigation is applied when that amount has been used. Sensor based and weather based controllers, described in [automated irrigation control](/agricultural-engineering/automated-irrigation-control/), apply the same water balance automatically, and [precision agriculture technologies](/agricultural-engineering/precision-agriculture-technologies/) allow it to vary across a field.

## Practical tips

- Use long term weather records and design for the peak month, not the seasonal average.
- Use local Kc values and growth stage lengths where research data exist; FAO-56 tables are a starting point.
- Check that the water source can supply the gross volume in the driest period.
- Allow for salinity leaching where irrigation water or soils are saline.

## Irrigation water requirement interview and viva questions

1. **What is the difference between ETo and ETc?** ETo is the evapotranspiration of a well watered reference grass and depends only on weather. ETc is the crop's own water use, found by multiplying ETo by the crop coefficient Kc.
2. **Why is the gross requirement larger than the net requirement?** No irrigation method is perfectly efficient, so the net depth is divided by the application efficiency to allow for deep percolation, runoff, evaporation and uneven application.
3. **Why is Kc low in the initial stage and highest in mid-season?** Early on the soil is mostly bare and the crop small, so transpiration is low. At full cover in mid-season the canopy transpires most, giving the peak Kc, for example 1.20 for grain maize.
4. **Why is not all rainfall counted as effective?** Part of it runs off, part percolates below the root zone and light showers evaporate from leaves and soil, so only the remainder, estimated with empirical formulas, is available to the crop.
5. **Why should a design use the peak month rather than the seasonal average?** Monthly and seasonal averages hide periods of high demand, so a system sized on the average would leave crops stressed during the peak period.
