<Key title="Short answer">

**HACCP principles** are the seven Codex rules of Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points, a preventive food safety system. Teams analyse hazards, determine critical control points, set critical limits, monitor them, take corrective action, verify the system and keep records. The principles are applied through 12 Codex steps and rest on prerequisite programmes such as cleaning and pest control.

</Key>

HACCP was developed in the 1960s by the Pillsbury Company with NASA and the US Army to make food for astronauts as safe as possible. Instead of relying on end product testing, which can never test every unit, HACCP controls hazards at the points in the process where they can be prevented or eliminated. It is now the international basis for food safety in processing plants, farms, kitchens and logistics.

## Types of food safety hazards

- **Biological:** pathogenic bacteria (Salmonella, Listeria, E. coli O157, Clostridium botulinum), viruses and parasites.
- **Chemical:** cleaning chemical residues, pesticide residues, mycotoxins, veterinary drug residues, toxic metals, unapproved additives.
- **Physical:** metal fragments, glass, hard plastic, stones, bone.
- **Allergens:** undeclared milk, egg, peanut, tree nuts, wheat, soy, fish, shellfish, sesame and others.

## Prerequisite programmes

HACCP only works on a foundation of good manufacturing and hygiene practices:

- Hygienic plant design, layout and equipment.
- Cleaning and sanitation, including CIP systems.
- Pest control.
- Personal hygiene and health of staff.
- Water and air quality.
- Supplier approval and raw material specifications.
- Preventive maintenance and calibration.
- Waste management, storage, transport and traceability.
- Training.

## The seven HACCP principles in detail

### Principle 1: hazard analysis

For each process step on the verified flow diagram, list potential hazards, assess their likelihood and severity, and identify control measures. Only significant hazards are carried forward. A [risk matrix](/safety-engineering/risk-matrix/) is often used to judge significance.

### Principle 2: determine critical control points

A **CCP** is a step where control can be applied and is essential to prevent, eliminate or reduce a hazard to an acceptable level, and where loss of control is not corrected by a later step. A **decision tree** of questions helps decide. Typical CCPs: cooking, [pasteurization](/agricultural-engineering/pasteurization-htst-uht/), retort sterilisation, chilling and [cold storage](/agricultural-engineering/cold-storage-design-basics/) temperatures, acidification, metal detection and allergen label checks.

### Principle 3: establish critical limits

Measurable values that separate safe from unsafe: for example, milk at 72 °C for at least 15 seconds, product core temperature at least 75 °C, pH below 4.6, metal detector rejecting 2.0 mm ferrous test pieces. Limits must be based on scientific evidence or regulation. Operating limits slightly tighter than critical limits allow adjustment before a deviation.

### Principle 4: establish monitoring

Planned observations or measurements that show whether a CCP is under control: what, how, when (frequency) and who. Continuous monitoring with recorders and alarms is preferred, such as temperature charts on a pasteurizer. Reliable sensors and [temperature measurement](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/temperature-measurements/) are essential.

### Principle 5: establish corrective actions

Predefined actions when monitoring shows a deviation: restore control, identify and hold affected product, evaluate its safety, rework or destroy it, find the cause and prevent recurrence, often with [root cause analysis](/safety-engineering/root-cause-analysis-methods/).

### Principle 6: establish verification

Activities that confirm the HACCP system works: reviewing records, calibrating instruments, testing products and environment, validating critical limits, internal audits and annual HACCP review.

### Principle 7: documentation and record keeping

The HACCP plan, hazard analysis, CCP determination, monitoring records, deviations and corrective actions, verification results and training records. Records are evidence for inspectors, auditors and customers.

## Common HACCP mistakes

- Copying a generic plan instead of analysing the actual process, products and plant.
- Too many CCPs, which spreads attention thin, or too few, missing real hazards.
- Critical limits that are not measurable or not validated.
- Monitoring done but records not reviewed, so deviations go unnoticed.
- Weak prerequisite programmes, such as poor cleaning, which undermine every CCP.

## HACCP and food safety management standards

HACCP is the core of food safety management systems such as **ISO 22000**, **FSSC 22000**, **BRCGS** and **SQF**, which add management commitment, document control, internal audits and continual improvement. In many countries regulations, including FSSAI requirements in India, require HACCP based food safety plans.

## Making HACCP principles work in practice

Successful HACCP depends on a multidisciplinary team, an accurate flow diagram verified on the factory floor, simple and practical monitoring, trained operators who understand why limits matter, and regular review whenever products, equipment or suppliers change. Combined with good engineering such as hygienic design, reliable [grain drying](/agricultural-engineering/grain-drying-methods/) and storage of raw materials, and accurate process control, HACCP principles keep food safe from farm to fork.

## HACCP principles interview and viva questions

1. **What is a critical control point?** A CCP is a step where control can be applied and is essential to prevent, eliminate or reduce a hazard to an acceptable level, and where loss of control is not corrected by a later step.
2. **How does a critical limit differ from an operating limit?** A critical limit is the measurable value that separates safe from unsafe product, such as milk at 72 °C for at least 15 seconds. Operating limits are set slightly tighter so operators can adjust the process before a deviation occurs.
3. **Why does HACCP need prerequisite programmes?** PRPs such as cleaning, pest control, personal hygiene and maintenance create the basic hygienic conditions for production. Weak prerequisite programmes, such as poor cleaning, undermine every CCP.
4. **What should happen when monitoring shows a deviation at a CCP?** The predefined corrective action is taken: control is restored, affected product is identified and held, its safety is evaluated, it is reworked or destroyed, and the cause is found to prevent recurrence.
5. **What is the difference between monitoring and verification?** Monitoring is the planned, routine observation or measurement showing whether a CCP is under control. Verification confirms that the whole system works, through record review, instrument calibration, product testing, validation of critical limits and audits.
