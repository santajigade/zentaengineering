  - q: "How much spare I/O should be allowed when selecting a PLC?"
    a: "A common practice is to allow 10 to 20 percent spare installed I/O, plus spare slots or space for future modules. Project specifications often state the exact figure. Spare capacity avoids costly panel changes when small additions are made during commissioning or later."
  - q: "What is the difference between sinking and sourcing inputs?"
    a: "They describe the direction of current. A sourcing device supplies current to the load (PNP sensors are sourcing), while a sinking device provides the path to 0 V (NPN sensors are sinking). A sourcing sensor must be wired to a sinking input card and vice versa, so the sensor type decides which input module is suitable."
  - q: "Should I choose a compact or a modular PLC?"
    a: "Compact PLCs with built-in I/O suit small machines with a fixed, modest I/O count. Modular PLCs, with a separate CPU and plug-in modules on a rack or rail, suit larger systems, mixed signal types, remote I/O and plants that will grow. The cost per I/O point usually favours modular systems as size increases."

<Key title="Short answer">

**PLC hardware selection** starts from the I/O list: count every digital and analog input and output, add 10 to 20 percent spare, then choose modules that match the signal types (24 V DC, 230 V AC, 4 to 20 mA, RTD, thermocouple). Next size the **CPU** for program memory, scan time, special functions and communication ports, then size the **power supply** from the total current of modules and field devices. Finally check environment, redundancy, certifications and spare parts support.

</Key>

A programmable logic controller (PLC) is an industrial computer that reads sensors, runs a control program and drives actuators. Choosing the hardware is one of the first decisions in an automation project, and mistakes are expensive: an undersized CPU, the wrong input type or a missing communication port can force redesign of panels and wiring. A structured selection method avoids these problems.

- **Power supply module (PSU):** converts mains to the internal bus voltage and often supplies 24 V DC for field devices.
- **CPU (processor module):** runs the program, stores data and handles communication.
- **I/O modules:** digital inputs and outputs, analog inputs and outputs, and special modules such as high speed counters, motion and weighing.
- **Rack, backplane or DIN rail bus:** connects modules to the CPU.
- **Communication modules:** Ethernet based networks, PROFIBUS, Modbus serial, IO-Link masters and others.
- **Remote I/O:** I/O stations placed near the process, linked to the CPU by a network, cutting field cabling.

## Step 1: Build the I/O list

List every field device with its tag, signal type and range. The basic signal categories are explained in [AI, AO, DI and DO signals](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/signals-in-instrumentation-ai-ao-di-do/) on InstrumentationBlog.in.

- **Digital inputs (DI):** push buttons, limit switches, proximity sensors, relay contacts, motor run feedback.
- **Digital outputs (DO):** contactor coils, solenoid valves, lamps, horns.
- **Analog inputs (AI):** 4 to 20 mA transmitters, 0 to 10 V signals, RTDs, thermocouples, load cells.
- **Analog outputs (AO):** control valve positioners, variable frequency drive speed references.

Group the totals by type and voltage, then add spare capacity, typically 10 to 20 percent, plus empty slots for growth.

## Step 2: Choose I/O module types

**Digital inputs:** match the voltage (24 V DC is most common, 120 or 230 V AC in some plants), the sensor wiring (sinking or sourcing to suit PNP or NPN sensors) and the response time. High speed counters or encoder inputs are needed for fast pulses.

**Digital outputs:** choose the output type:

| Output type | Suits | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relay | AC or DC loads, mixed voltages | Isolated, but limited switching life and speed |
| Transistor | DC loads such as 24 V solenoids, fast pulses | Long life, fast, DC only |
| Triac | AC loads | No contact wear, small leakage current |

Check the current rating per point and per common group, and whether outputs are protected against short circuits.

**Analog inputs:** check signal type, resolution (12 to 16 bits typical), accuracy, number of channels, channel to channel isolation and whether the module powers 2-wire transmitters. Direct RTD and thermocouple modules avoid separate signal converters.

**Analog outputs:** check current or voltage output, resolution and load resistance limits.

## Step 3: Size the CPU

- **Program and data memory:** estimate from the I/O count and the complexity of logic, PID loops, recipes and data logging, with generous spare.
- **Scan time and speed:** fast machines need short, deterministic scan times; process plants are usually less demanding.
- **Functions:** PID, motion control, safety, high speed counting, math and data handling.
- **Communication:** number and type of ports for HMI, SCADA, drives, remote I/O and other PLCs. Ethernet based protocols are now standard; Modbus remains widely used, as explained in [Modbus RTU vs Modbus TCP](/automation-robotics/modbus-rtu-vs-tcp/).
- **Programming:** support for IEC 61131-3 languages and the software licence costs.
- **Redundancy:** hot standby CPUs and redundant networks for critical processes.

## Step 4: Size the power supply

Add the backplane current of the CPU and every module, then add the 24 V DC current drawn by sensors, transmitters and output loads if they are fed from the same supply. Select a supply with margin, commonly 20 to 30 percent, and consider separate supplies for field devices so that a field short circuit does not stop the CPU. Redundant supplies with diode or decoupling modules are used where downtime is costly.

## Step 5: Environment and certification

Check operating temperature, humidity, vibration and electrical noise against the module specifications and IEC 61131-2. Hazardous areas may need intrinsically safe barriers or certified remote I/O. Marine, railway and safety applications need specific approvals. Panel heat load, enclosure rating and cooling must also be checked.

## PLC hardware selection: final checks before ordering

- Confirm all field device types and voltages with the instrument and electrical lists.
- Check panel space, heat dissipation and cable entry for the chosen modules.
- Standardise on one product family across the plant to reduce spares and training.
- Check lifecycle status: avoid products near end of life.
- Order recommended spares: at least one of each module type and a spare CPU for critical systems.

The PLC is usually supervised by an HMI or a [SCADA system](/automation-robotics/scada-system-architecture/), while large continuous processes may use a [DCS](/automation-robotics/dcs-architecture/) instead. The electronics inside the CPU are related to a [microcontroller](/electronics-engineering/microcontroller-vs-microprocessor/).
