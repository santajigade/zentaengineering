<Key title="Short answer">

**Types of industrial robots** are the main mechanical designs of programmable, multi-axis manipulators, classified by how their joints are arranged. Articulated arms use rotary joints, usually six axes, SCARA robots suit fast assembly, Cartesian gantries move on three linear axes, delta robots pick light parts at high speed, and cobots work safely near people.

</Key>

Industrial robots are programmable, multi-axis manipulators that move parts or tools automatically. They weld car bodies, assemble electronics, pack food, tend machine tools and palletise boxes. ISO 8373 defines an industrial robot as an automatically controlled, reprogrammable, multipurpose manipulator programmable in three or more axes. Different mechanical arrangements give very different strengths, so selecting the right type is the first step in any robot cell design.

## Basic terms

- **Axis or joint:** a single motion, either rotary (revolute) or linear (prismatic).
- **Degrees of freedom (DOF):** number of independent motions; six are needed to reach any position with any orientation.
- **Work envelope:** the volume the robot can reach.
- **Payload:** maximum mass the robot can carry at its wrist, including the gripper.
- **Repeatability:** how closely the robot returns to the same taught point, often a few hundredths of a millimetre for small robots.
- **End effector:** the gripper, welding torch, spray gun, suction cup or tool at the end of the arm.

## Types of industrial robots

### Articulated robots

An articulated robot looks like a human arm: a rotating base, shoulder, elbow and a wrist, usually with six rotary axes. Its work envelope is roughly spherical and very flexible, so it can reach around obstacles and approach parts from many angles. Payloads range from a few kilograms to over a tonne for the largest models.

**Uses:** arc and spot welding, painting, machine tending, material handling, palletising, assembly, deburring and grinding.

### SCARA robots

SCARA (Selective Compliance Assembly Robot Arm) robots have two parallel rotary joints moving in a horizontal plane, plus a vertical linear axis and usually a rotation at the tool. They are stiff vertically but slightly compliant sideways, which helps insert pins and parts into holes. They are compact, fast and accurate within a cylindrical work area.

**Uses:** electronics assembly, screw driving, small part pick and place, dispensing and inspection.

### Cartesian (gantry) robots

Cartesian robots move along three perpendicular linear axes (X, Y, Z), often built from linear modules driven by belts, screws or rack and pinion. Large overhead versions are called **gantry robots**. They are simple to program, very rigid and easily scaled to large work areas and heavy loads.

**Uses:** CNC machine loading, 3D printing, dispensing, large part handling, palletising and storage systems.

### Cylindrical and polar robots

A **cylindrical robot** has a rotating base, a vertical linear axis and a radial linear axis, giving a cylindrical work envelope. A **polar (spherical) robot** combines two rotary joints and one linear extension. Both were common in early robotics and are still used for simple handling, die casting and machine loading, though articulated robots have largely replaced them.

### Delta (parallel) robots

A delta robot has three or more lightweight arms connected to a base above and to a small moving platform below. Because the motors are fixed on the base, the moving mass is tiny, allowing very high speeds and accelerations.

**Uses:** high speed picking and packing of food, pharmaceuticals and small products from conveyors, often guided by [machine vision](/automation-robotics/machine-vision-components/).

### Collaborative robots (cobots)

Cobots are designed to work near or with people. They use power and force limiting, speed and separation monitoring, hand guiding and safety rated stops. They usually have lower payload and speed than industrial arms but are quick to deploy and easy to program, often by moving the arm by hand.

**Uses:** machine tending, screwdriving, quality inspection, packaging and assistance tasks in small and medium sized factories.

### Mobile robots

Autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) and automated guided vehicles (AGVs) move materials around factories and warehouses. Mobile manipulators combine a mobile base with an arm.

## Comparing the main types

| Type | Typical axes | Strengths | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Articulated | 6 (sometimes 4, 5 or 7) | Large, flexible reach; versatile | Higher cost; complex kinematics |
| SCARA | 4 | Fast, accurate, compact | Limited vertical reach and tilt |
| Cartesian | 3 (plus tool rotation) | Rigid, simple, scalable | Large footprint; limited orientations |
| Delta | 3 or 4 | Very fast for light parts | Small payload and work area |
| Cobot | 6 or 7 | Safe near people, easy to deploy | Lower speed and payload |

## Robot programming methods

Robots are programmed by **teach pendant**, jogging the arm to points and recording them; by **offline programming**, creating paths in simulation software from CAD data and downloading them; and, for cobots, by **hand guiding**, moving the arm physically through the motion. Offline programming reduces downtime on the line, while vision and force sensors let modern robots adapt to part position and contact instead of following fixed paths.

Most robot makers also offer simulation software for checking reach and cycle time before purchase.

## How to choose a robot type

1. **Define the task:** what is moved, how far, how fast, with what accuracy.
2. **Check payload:** include the gripper and part, with allowance for offset loads and inertia.
3. **Check reach and work envelope** against the cell layout.
4. **Check cycle time and speed** needed to meet production rates.
5. **Consider the environment:** clean room, food grade, washdown, explosive atmospheres.
6. **Plan safety:** guarding, light curtains, safety rated controllers and a risk assessment to ISO 10218.
7. **Consider integration:** controller, communication with the line [PLC](/automation-robotics/plc-hardware-selection/), vision, tools and programming effort.

## Types of industrial robots interview and viva questions

1. **How does ISO 8373 define an industrial robot?** As an automatically controlled, reprogrammable, multipurpose manipulator programmable in three or more axes.
2. **Why do most articulated robots have six axes?** Six degrees of freedom let the tool reach a point in space (three) with any orientation (three).
3. **Why is a SCARA robot well suited to inserting parts into holes?** It is stiff vertically but slightly compliant sideways, which helps pins and parts align as they are inserted, and it is fast and accurate within its cylindrical work area.
4. **What makes a delta robot so fast?** Its motors are fixed on the base, so the moving mass of the arms and platform is tiny, allowing very high speeds and accelerations for light parts.
5. **What safety functions let a cobot work near people?** Power and force limiting, speed and separation monitoring, hand guiding and safety rated stops, applied only after a risk assessment guided by ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066.

## What drives the axes

Robot joints are driven by [servo motors](/automation-robotics/servo-vs-stepper-motor/) with gearboxes and encoders, controlled by a robot controller that calculates joint motions from tool paths using kinematics. A robot is a clear example of a [mechatronic system](/automation-robotics/mechatronic-system-elements/), and its gears and bearings rely on proper [lubrication](/mechanical-engineering/types-of-lubrication/) for long life. Robot cells are increasingly designed and commissioned virtually with a [digital twin](/automation-robotics/digital-twin-explained/) before the hardware is installed.
