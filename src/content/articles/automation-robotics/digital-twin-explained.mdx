<Key title="Short answer">

A **digital twin** is a virtual model of a specific physical asset, process or system that is kept up to date with data from its real counterpart. Sensors feed the model, the model analyses, simulates and predicts, and the results return as decisions or control actions for design, commissioning, maintenance and optimisation.

</Key>

Industry 4.0 connects machines, sensors and software across factories and supply chains. The digital twin is one of its most useful ideas: instead of studying a generic model of a pump, a production line or a power plant, engineers work with a model of their particular asset, calibrated with its own data and following its real history. That lets them ask "what if" questions safely and see problems before they happen.

A digital twin has three parts:

1. **The physical entity:** a product, machine, production line, building, plant or network, instrumented with sensors and controlled by PLCs or a DCS.
2. **The virtual model:** geometry, physics based models (for example thermodynamics, fluid flow, mechanics), data driven models (machine learning trained on history), and rules or logic.
3. **The connection:** data from the physical side (via industrial networks, gateways and protocols such as OPC UA or MQTT) updates the model, and results go back to people or, in advanced twins, directly to the control system.

ISO 23247 describes a reference framework for manufacturing digital twins built around these elements.

## Levels of integration

Researchers often distinguish three levels by how data flows:

- **Digital model:** no automatic data exchange; data is entered manually. Useful for design studies.
- **Digital shadow:** data flows automatically from the physical asset to the model, but changes to the asset are made by people.
- **Digital twin:** data flows automatically in both directions, so the model can influence the asset, for example by adjusting set points.

## Types of digital twins

- **Component or part twin:** a single critical part, such as a bearing or turbine blade, tracking wear and remaining life.
- **Asset or product twin:** a whole machine, such as a pump, compressor, motor or robot.
- **System or unit twin:** several assets working together, such as a production line or a [distillation](/chemical-engineering/distillation-column-working-principle/) unit.
- **Process twin:** an entire factory or process, used for scheduling, throughput and energy optimisation.

They can also be grouped by life cycle stage: **design twins** for engineering, **commissioning twins** for testing control software, and **operational twins** for running assets.

## Industrial uses

- **Product design:** test designs virtually with [finite element analysis](/mechanical-engineering/finite-element-analysis-basics/) and simulation before building prototypes.
- **Virtual commissioning:** connect the real PLC program to a simulated machine or line, find logic errors in the office and shorten site commissioning. Robot cells for [industrial robots](/automation-robotics/types-of-industrial-robots/) are routinely commissioned this way.
- **Predictive maintenance:** compare live vibration, temperature and performance with the model to detect degradation and schedule work before failure.
- **Process optimisation:** run "what if" scenarios on throughput, quality and energy use, then apply the best settings.
- **Operator training:** realistic simulators connected to copies of the real control system, such as a [DCS](/automation-robotics/dcs-architecture/) training system.
- **Energy and utilities:** grid, water network and building twins for planning and operations.

## Benefits

- Fewer physical prototypes and faster development.
- Shorter commissioning and earlier production start.
- Less unplanned downtime through early warnings.
- Better decisions based on a consistent, data backed view of the asset.
- A record of each asset's history for quality, compliance and service.

## Challenges

- **Data quality and connectivity:** sensors must be accurate and calibrated, and legacy equipment may need retrofitting. Reliable instrumentation is the foundation; see [process instrumentation](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/category/process-instrumentation/) on InstrumentationBlog.in.
- **Model fidelity:** models must be detailed enough for the purpose and kept calibrated as equipment ages.
- **Integration:** data from PLCs, [SCADA](/automation-robotics/scada-system-architecture/), historians, maintenance systems and ERP must be combined with consistent naming.
- **Cybersecurity:** two way links to control systems must be protected according to standards such as IEC 62443.
- **Cost and skills:** a clear business case and a cross disciplinary team are needed.

## Example: a pump digital twin

A cooling water pump is fitted with flow, suction and discharge pressure, motor power and vibration sensors. Its digital twin combines the manufacturer's pump curve with the measured values. As the impeller wears, the measured head at a given flow drops below the curve and efficiency falls; the twin shows the trend and estimates when efficiency will reach the replacement threshold. It can also compare running the pump at a lower speed through a variable speed drive with throttling a valve, and show the energy saved. Maintenance can then be planned at the next shutdown instead of after a breakdown.

## Technologies behind digital twins

Digital twins combine several technologies: industrial IoT gateways and edge computers that collect data close to the machines; time series databases and historians; physics simulation tools; machine learning platforms; 3D visualisation and augmented reality; and cloud or on-premises platforms that host the models. Standard information models, such as OPC UA companion specifications and the Asset Administration Shell, help describe assets consistently so twins from different suppliers can work together.

Successful projects keep the model simple enough to maintain.

## How to start

1. Pick one asset or problem with a clear value, such as frequent pump failures or a bottleneck line.
2. Check which data already exists and how good it is.
3. Start with a digital shadow: monitoring plus a simple model.
4. Validate predictions against real events, then extend to more assets and closed loop use.

Digital twins build on the basics of automation: well designed [PLC systems](/automation-robotics/plc-hardware-selection/), clean data from industrial networks such as [Modbus TCP](/automation-robotics/modbus-rtu-vs-tcp/), and good understanding of each [mechatronic system](/automation-robotics/mechatronic-system-elements/). Machine learning models inside data driven twins face the same risk of [overfitting and underfitting](/ai-data-engineering/overfitting-and-underfitting/) as any other model, so they must be validated on data they were not trained on.

## Digital twin interview and viva questions

1. **What is a digital twin?** It is a virtual model of a specific physical asset, process or system that is kept up to date with data from that real counterpart and is used to monitor, predict and optimise it.
2. **How does a digital shadow differ from a digital twin?** In a digital shadow, data flows automatically from the asset to the model only, and people make the changes. In a digital twin, data flows automatically in both directions, so the model can influence the asset.
3. **What is virtual commissioning?** The real PLC program is connected to a simulated machine or line so that logic errors are found in the office before site commissioning, which shortens start up.
4. **Which standard gives a digital twin framework for manufacturing?** ISO 23247 describes a reference framework for manufacturing digital twins built around the physical entity, the virtual model and the data connection.
5. **Why is cybersecurity important for digital twins?** Advanced twins have two way links to control systems, so those links must be protected according to standards such as IEC 62443.
