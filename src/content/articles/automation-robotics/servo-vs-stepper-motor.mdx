<Key title="Short answer">

**Servo motor vs stepper motor** is a comparison of two motion control motors. A stepper moves a fixed step, commonly 1.8°, per pulse, usually in open loop, so it is simple and cheap but can lose steps. A servo uses an encoder and drive in a closed loop, giving higher speed, dynamic torque and confirmed position.

</Key>

Precise motion is at the heart of CNC machines, robots, packaging lines, 3D printers and pick and place systems. Two motor technologies dominate small and medium motion axes: stepper motors and servo motors. Choosing the right one affects machine speed, accuracy, reliability and cost.

## How a stepper motor works

A stepper motor has a toothed rotor (permanent magnet or hybrid) and a stator with several windings. The driver energises the windings in sequence; each change pulls the rotor to the next stable position, one step. A typical hybrid stepper has 200 full steps per revolution (1.8° per step). Speed is set by the pulse frequency, and position by counting pulses, so no feedback is needed in basic systems.

**Microstepping** drivers control the phase currents in finer increments, for example 16 microsteps per full step, which smooths motion and reduces resonance.

Key characteristics:

- **High torque at low speed** and strong **holding torque** at standstill.
- **Torque falls as speed rises,** because winding inductance limits how fast current can build up at each step.
- **Open loop:** if the load demands more torque than available, the motor **loses steps** and the controller does not know.
- **Resonance** at certain speeds can cause vibration and step loss.
- Draws current even when stationary, so runs warm.

## How a servo motor works

An industrial servo system has three parts: a motor (usually a permanent magnet synchronous AC motor), a **feedback device** (encoder or resolver) and a **servo drive**. The drive compares the commanded position and speed with the feedback and adjusts motor current in nested control loops: current (torque), velocity and position. Any error, from load changes or disturbances, is corrected within milliseconds.

Key characteristics:

- **Near constant torque up to rated speed,** and often several thousand rpm.
- **Peak torque** of typically two to three times the rated value for short accelerations.
- **High dynamic response** and smooth motion at all speeds.
- **Confirmed position:** the drive detects following errors and raises alarms instead of silently losing position.
- Draws current in proportion to load, so runs cooler at light load.
- Requires **tuning** of control loop gains for the mechanical load.

## Servo motor vs stepper motor: comparison

| Feature | Stepper motor | Servo motor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Control | Usually open loop (closed loop versions exist) | Closed loop with encoder or resolver |
| Torque at low speed | High | High |
| Torque at high speed | Falls sharply | Maintained to rated speed |
| Typical maximum speed | Up to roughly 1000 to 2000 rpm usable | Often 3000 to 6000 rpm |
| Overload capability | Little; stalls or loses steps | Short peaks of 2 to 3 times rated |
| Position verification | None in open loop | Continuous |
| Smoothness | Vibration and resonance at some speeds | Very smooth |
| Setup | Simple, no tuning | Needs tuning and parameter setup |
| Holding at standstill | Holding torque, current always on | Active holding; brake for vertical loads |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |

## Torque, speed and power

The mechanical power of a motor is torque times angular speed:

$$
P = T\,\omega = T \cdot \frac{2\pi N}{60}
$$

with T in N·m, N in rpm and P in watts. Because stepper torque falls with speed, a stepper's power peaks at a moderate speed, while a servo's power rises nearly in proportion to speed up to its rating.

<dt>Given</dt><dd>An axis needs 2 N·m at 3000 rpm. An alternative design uses a 200 step per revolution stepper with 16 microsteps to run a lighter axis at 600 rpm.</dd>

<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Steady speed; no gearbox; losses ignored.</dd>

<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>About 630 W of servo power (a 750 W servo would usually be chosen); the stepper needs 32 kHz step pulses</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Acceleration torque, friction and load inertia must be added for real sizing, and the stepper's torque at 600 rpm must be checked on its speed torque curve.</dd>
</dl>

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Angular speed | ω = 2πN / 60 | ω in rad/s, N in rpm |
| Mechanical power | P = Tω | P in W, T in N·m, ω in rad/s |
| Pulses per revolution | full steps per revolution × microsteps per step | dimensionless |
| Step pulse frequency | pulses per revolution × N / 60 | pulses per second (Hz), N in rpm |

## How to choose

**Choose a stepper motor when:**

- Loads are light and predictable, and speeds are low to moderate.
- Cost and simplicity matter more than top performance.
- Typical uses: 3D printers, small CNC routers, laboratory instruments, camera sliders, valve actuators, label printers.

**Choose a servo motor when:**

- High speed, fast acceleration or high throughput is needed.
- Loads vary or position must be guaranteed.
- Smooth motion is important, or the motor must run long periods at high duty.
- Typical uses: industrial [robots](/automation-robotics/types-of-industrial-robots/), CNC machine tools, packaging machines, printing presses, electronic assembly.

**Closed loop steppers** (steppers with an encoder) are a middle option: they prevent lost steps and reduce heating at modest extra cost.

## Sizing tips

Accurate sizing prevents both overheating and wasted cost.


- Calculate the load inertia reflected to the motor, including gearbox ratio, and keep the load to motor inertia ratio within the drive maker's guidance for good stability.
- Size for the peak torque during acceleration and the RMS torque over the duty cycle.
- Check speed against the motor's torque speed curve, not just its rated values.
- Add a brake for vertical axes.

## Servo motor vs stepper motor interview and viva questions

1. **Why does stepper torque fall as speed rises?** Winding inductance limits how quickly the current can build up at each step, so at higher step rates the current, and therefore the torque, is lower.
2. **What happens when an open loop stepper is overloaded?** It loses steps: the rotor slips behind the field, and the controller does not know because there is no feedback.
3. **What are the three nested control loops in a servo drive?** The current (torque) loop inside, the velocity loop around it and the position loop outermost, all closed using the encoder or resolver feedback.
4. **What does microstepping improve, and what does it not?** It smooths motion and reduces resonance by driving the phase currents in finer increments, but the positional accuracy of each microstep under load is lower than the resolution suggests.
5. **Why do vertical servo axes need a holding brake?** A servo holds position actively only while powered, so when power is removed a brake is needed to stop the load from falling.
