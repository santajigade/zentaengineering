<Key title="Short answer">

**Quality control vs quality assurance**: quality control checks products and outputs against requirements, finding and removing defects through inspection and testing. Quality assurance manages the processes, procedures, training and audits that give confidence the requirements will be met, preventing defects. QC is product focused and detective; QA is process focused and preventive.

</Key>

Quality control vs quality assurance is one of the most common points of confusion in manufacturing, partly because many companies use one department name for both. The two are related but not the same. ISO 9000:2015 treats both as parts of quality management: quality assurance is the part focused on providing confidence that quality requirements will be fulfilled, and quality control is the part focused on fulfilling those requirements.

## Quality control vs quality assurance comparison table

| Aspect | Quality control (QC) | Quality assurance (QA) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ISO 9000 focus | Fulfilling quality requirements | Providing confidence that requirements will be fulfilled |
| Object | Products, parts, batches, outputs | Processes, systems, procedures |
| Approach | Detective: find and remove defects | Preventive: stop defects occurring |
| Timing | During and after production | Before and throughout, starting at design |
| Typical activities | Inspection, testing, SPC charts, acceptance sampling | Quality planning, procedures, training, supplier approval, audits, FMEA |
| Typical people | Inspectors, test technicians, operators doing checks | Quality engineers, auditors, process owners, management |
| Output | Accept or reject decisions, test records, defect data | Documented system, audit reports, corrective and preventive actions |
| Cost of quality category | Mainly appraisal | Mainly prevention |

## Quality control explained

Quality control asks a direct question: does this product, part or batch meet its specification? Answering it needs a requirement (a drawing, specification or standard), a measurement, and a decision rule.

Common quality control activities are:

- Inspection of incoming materials, in-process parts and finished products, by measurement, gauging or visual checks.
- Testing, such as functional, pressure, electrical or destructive tests on samples.
- Statistical process control, which uses [control charts](/manufacturing-engineering/control-charts-spc/) to detect when a process starts to drift, before many bad parts are made.
- Acceptance sampling, in which a lot is accepted or rejected based on a sample, using plans such as those in ISO 2859-1.
- Control of nonconforming product: identifying, segregating and deciding on rework, repair, concession or scrap.

QC is essential, but on its own it is reactive. It finds a defect after the material, machine time and labour have already been spent. Inspection is also not perfect: inspectors miss some defects, especially in repetitive visual checks, so quality cannot be inspected into a product.

## Quality assurance explained

Quality assurance asks a different question: how do we know the process will keep producing conforming products? It builds confidence for management, customers and regulators that requirements will be met. Activities include:

- Quality planning: defining requirements, control plans, inspection points and acceptance criteria.
- Documented procedures and work instructions, so tasks are done the same way every time.
- Training and competence records for operators, inspectors and engineers.
- Supplier approval and monitoring, so purchased parts arrive right.
- Design reviews and risk analysis, such as failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA).
- Calibration of measuring equipment, so QC decisions can be trusted.
- Internal and external audits of the quality management system, for example against ISO 9001.
- Corrective and preventive action: finding the root cause of problems and changing the process so they do not recur.

Improvement methods such as [Six Sigma DMAIC](/manufacturing-engineering/six-sigma-dmaic/) and workplace organisation through [5S](/manufacturing-engineering/5s-methodology/) support quality assurance because they reduce variation and error at the source.

## Differences explained with an example

Consider a machining line that produces shafts.

- A QC inspector measures the diameter of every tenth shaft with a micrometer and rejects any outside tolerance. That is quality control.
- The quality engineer who wrote the control plan, chose the sample frequency, arranged micrometer calibration, trained the inspector, and audits the line each quarter is doing quality assurance.
- When rejected shafts are traced to tool wear, QC records the defect; QA leads the corrective action, for example a change to the tool change interval in the work instruction.

The two depend on each other. QA needs QC data to know whether the system works, and QC needs QA to make its checks consistent and trustworthy.

## Quality control vs quality assurance in the cost of quality

Feigenbaum and Juran grouped quality costs into four categories:

- Prevention costs: planning, training, supplier development, process design. Mostly QA.
- Appraisal costs: inspection, testing, calibration, audits of product. Mostly QC.
- Internal failure costs: scrap, rework, re-inspection before delivery.
- External failure costs: warranty claims, returns, recalls, lost customers.

Spending more on prevention usually reduces both appraisal and failure costs.

## When to use which

Both are always needed; the question is the balance.

- New process or product: emphasise QA. Plan, assess risk with FMEA, qualify suppliers and set control plans before launch, with tighter QC until the process is proven capable.
- Stable, capable process: QC can move from 100% inspection to sampling and SPC, while QA audits keep the system healthy.
- Regulated industries such as aerospace, medical devices and pharmaceuticals: both are formal and documented, with QA often holding release authority.
- Recurring defects: QC data identifies them, QA removes the cause.

Lean manufacturing counts defects among the [seven wastes](/manufacturing-engineering/seven-wastes-lean/) and aims to prevent them rather than sort them out.

## Quality control vs quality assurance interview and viva questions

1. **How does ISO 9000:2015 distinguish QC and QA?** Both are parts of quality management: QC is focused on fulfilling quality requirements, while QA is focused on providing confidence that those requirements will be fulfilled.
2. **Why is QC described as detective and QA as preventive?** QC finds defects in products after or during production, while QA works on processes, procedures and training so defects do not occur.
3. Give two examples each of QC and QA activities. QC: final inspection and acceptance sampling. QA: internal audits and supplier approval.
4. **Which cost of quality category does inspection fall into?** Appraisal cost, the cost of checking products and processes against requirements.
5. **Why can quality not be inspected into a product?** Inspection only sorts products already made and misses some defects, so a defect free output depends on a capable, well controlled process.
