<Key title="Short answer">

**Production planning and control** (PPC) is the function that decides what, how much, when, where and how to produce, then makes sure it happens. Planning covers forecasting, aggregate planning, the master production schedule, MRP, routing and scheduling; control covers dispatching, follow-up and corrective action, so demand is met on time at minimum cost.

</Key>

A factory has limited machines, people and materials, and many orders competing for them. Without planning, some orders are late while others pile up as inventory, machines sit idle one week and are overloaded the next, and expensive overtime becomes routine. PPC coordinates sales, purchasing, production, quality and logistics so that the right products are made at the right time.

## Objectives of production planning and control

- Deliver orders on time to meet customer due dates.
- Use machines and labour efficiently, avoiding both idle time and overload.
- Keep raw material, work in progress and finished goods inventory at economic levels.
- Reduce lead times and manufacturing cost.
- Respond quickly to changes in demand, breakdowns and supply problems.

## Planning functions

### 1. Forecasting

Estimates future demand using past sales, market information and customer orders. Methods range from moving averages and exponential smoothing to regression and machine learning.

### 2. Aggregate planning (sales and operations planning)

Sets overall production rates, workforce levels, overtime, subcontracting and inventory for product families over 6 to 18 months, balancing demand with capacity at minimum cost. Strategies include **level** production (steady output, inventory absorbs swings), **chase** (output follows demand) and **mixed**.

### 3. Master production schedule (MPS)

Breaks the aggregate plan into specific end products and quantities per week. It is the main link between sales commitments and production.

### 4. Material requirements planning (MRP)

MRP calculates the components and materials needed, and when, by exploding the MPS through the **bill of materials (BOM)** and netting against inventory and scheduled receipts, then offsetting by **lead times**:

$$
\text{Net requirement} = \text{Gross requirement} - \text{On hand} - \text{Scheduled receipts} + \text{Safety stock}
$$

MRP II and modern **ERP** systems extend MRP to capacity, finance and the whole business.

### 5. Process planning and routing

Defines the sequence of operations, machines, tools, setup and run times for each part: the **route sheet**.

### 6. Capacity planning and loading

Compares the load from planned orders with available capacity at each work centre (rough cut capacity planning for the MPS, capacity requirements planning for MRP) and adjusts plans or capacity with overtime, extra shifts or subcontracting.

### 7. Scheduling

Assigns start and finish times to jobs on each machine. Rules include **first come first served**, **earliest due date (EDD)**, **shortest processing time (SPT)** and **critical ratio**. Gantt charts and advanced planning and scheduling (APS) software visualise and optimise schedules.

## Control functions

### 8. Dispatching

Releasing work orders, material requisitions, tool orders and drawings to the shop floor so work can start as scheduled.

### 9. Follow-up and expediting

Tracking progress against the schedule, identifying delays from breakdowns, absenteeism, material shortages or quality problems, and chasing critical jobs.

### 10. Evaluation and corrective action

Comparing actual output, lead times and costs with plans, and feeding results back to improve future planning.

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>A gearbox needs 2 shafts each. The MPS calls for 300 gearboxes in week 6. Shaft inventory on hand is 150, with a scheduled receipt of 100 shafts in week 5. Safety stock is 50 shafts. Shaft machining lead time is 2 weeks.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>The net shaft requirement and when the work order must be released.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Lot for lot ordering; no other demand for shafts.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>Gross requirement = 300 × 2 = 600 shafts in week 6<br />Net requirement = 600 − 150 − 100 + 50 = 400 shafts<br />Release = week 6 − 2 weeks = week 4</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>Release a work order for 400 shafts in week 4 so they are ready for assembly in week 6</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>MRP assumes fixed lead times and infinite capacity; capacity checks and lot sizing rules such as EOQ may change the order.</dd>
</dl>
</Example>

Lot sizing in MRP can use lot for lot, fixed order quantities or the [EOQ model](/manufacturing-engineering/eoq-inventory-model/), trading setup cost against inventory carrying cost.

## Key formulas for production planning and control

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gross requirement of a component | Gross = MPS quantity × quantity per parent from the BOM | Units of the component in the period |
| Net requirement (MRP) | Net = Gross − On hand − Scheduled receipts + Safety stock | All in units of the component |
| Order release period | Release = Need period − Lead time | Periods, usually weeks |

## Production planning and control by type of production

| Production type | Example | PPC focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job shop | Tool room, custom machinery | Detailed scheduling of varied routes, due dates |
| Batch production | Machined components, garments | Lot sizing, changeovers, MRP |
| Mass / line production | Car assembly | Line balancing, takt time, material flow |
| Continuous process | Refinery, cement | Throughput, maintenance planning, inventory of feedstock |

## Key performance indicators

Planners measure how well the system works with indicators such as on time delivery to customers, schedule adherence (the share of work orders completed as planned), manufacturing lead time, inventory turns, capacity utilisation and the number of expedited orders. A rising number of urgent changes usually means the plan is unrealistic, either because capacity was overestimated or because demand forecasts were poor. Regular sales and operations planning meetings align commercial, production and purchasing teams on one plan.

## Common problems in production planning and control

- Inaccurate bills of materials or inventory records, which make MRP order the wrong parts.
- Unrealistic lead times or standard times, giving schedules that cannot be met.
- Frequent schedule changes that cause setups, confusion and excess work in progress.
- Poor communication between sales, planning and production.
- Ignoring maintenance windows, so planned work collides with production peaks.

## Modern trends

Planning tools keep improving, but accurate data and clear ownership remain the foundation.

ERP and manufacturing execution systems (MES) connect planning with real time shop floor data from machines and operators. Lean pull systems with kanban complement MRP by controlling work in progress and exposing the [seven wastes](/manufacturing-engineering/seven-wastes-lean/). Demand driven MRP, finite capacity scheduling, digital twins and AI based forecasting help plants react quickly. Accurate standard times from [time and motion study](/manufacturing-engineering/time-and-motion-study/) and reliable equipment through planned [maintenance](/manufacturing-engineering/types-of-maintenance/) remain the foundation of good production planning and control.

## Production planning and control interview and viva questions

1. **What is the difference between the planning and control parts of PPC?** Planning decides what, how much, when and how to produce through forecasting, aggregate planning, the MPS, MRP, routing and scheduling, while control covers dispatching, follow-up and corrective action when output differs from the plan.
2. **What is the master production schedule?** It breaks the aggregate plan into specific end products and quantities per week, and it is the main link between sales commitments and production.
3. **How does MRP calculate component requirements?** It explodes the MPS through the bill of materials, nets the result against inventory and scheduled receipts, and offsets orders by lead times.
4. **What is the difference between level and chase strategies in aggregate planning?** Level production keeps output steady and lets inventory absorb demand swings, while chase changes output to follow demand.
5. **What are the main limitations of basic MRP?** It assumes fixed lead times and infinite capacity, so capacity checks and lot sizing rules may change the planned orders.
