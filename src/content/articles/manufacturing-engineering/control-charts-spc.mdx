<Key title="Short answer">

**Control charts** are the main tool of statistical process control (SPC). They plot a process measurement over time against a centre line and upper and lower control limits, usually at ±3 standard deviations. Points inside the limits with no unusual pattern show a stable process; points outside or non-random patterns signal special causes to investigate.

</Key>

Every process varies: no two parts are exactly the same. The question is whether the variation is the normal, predictable noise of a stable process, or a sign that something has changed: a worn tool, a new batch of material, a different operator or a drifting sensor. Control charts, developed by Walter Shewhart at Bell Laboratories in the 1920s, answer that question in real time, so problems are caught before many bad parts are made.

## Common causes and special causes

- **Common cause variation** is inherent in the process: many small, random influences such as minor temperature fluctuations, material variation and machine vibration. Reducing it requires changing the process itself, usually a management or engineering decision.
- **Special (assignable) cause variation** comes from specific events: a broken tool, a wrong setting, a contaminated batch. It should be found and removed by the people running the process.

Reacting to common cause noise as if it were a special cause (tampering) actually increases variation, while ignoring special causes lets defects pile up. Control charts help tell the two apart.

## Types of control charts

### Variables charts (measurements)

- **X-bar and R chart:** small subgroups (typically n = 2 to 10, often 5) are taken at regular intervals. The X-bar chart tracks subgroup means (process centring); the R chart tracks subgroup ranges (process spread).
- **X-bar and S chart:** uses standard deviation instead of range, better for larger subgroups (n above about 10).
- **Individuals and moving range (I-MR):** for one measurement at a time, such as batch processes or slow production.
- **CUSUM and EWMA charts:** sensitive to small, gradual shifts.

### Attributes charts (counts)

- **p chart:** proportion defective in samples of varying size.
- **np chart:** number defective in samples of constant size.
- **c chart:** number of defects per unit of constant size, for example scratches per panel.
- **u chart:** defects per unit when the inspection area varies.

## Control limit formulas for X-bar and R charts

With k subgroups of size n, the grand mean X̿ and average range R̄:

$$
UCL_{\bar{X}} = \bar{\bar{X}} + A_2\bar{R} \qquad LCL_{\bar{X}} = \bar{\bar{X}} - A_2\bar{R}
$$

$$
UCL_R = D_4\bar{R} \qquad LCL_R = D_3\bar{R}
$$

For n = 5, the constants are A₂ = 0.577, D₃ = 0 and D₄ = 2.114 (from standard SPC tables).

<Example>
<dl>
<dt>Given</dt><dd>A shaft diameter is checked in 25 subgroups of 5 parts. The grand mean is 25.012 mm and the average range is 0.020 mm. Specification is 25.00 ± 0.05 mm.</dd>
<dt>Find</dt><dd>Control limits for the X-bar and R charts, and estimate process capability Cpk.</dd>
<dt>Assumptions</dt><dd>Process stable during data collection; normal distribution; d₂ = 2.326 for n = 5 so σ = R̄ ÷ d₂.</dd>
<dt>Solution</dt><dd>X-bar UCL = 25.012 + 0.577 × 0.020 = 25.0235 mm; LCL = 25.012 − 0.0115 = 25.0005 mm<br />R chart UCL = 2.114 × 0.020 = 0.0423 mm; LCL = 0<br />σ = 0.020 ÷ 2.326 = 0.0086 mm<br />Cpk = min[(25.05 − 25.012), (25.012 − 24.95)] ÷ (3 × 0.0086) = 0.038 ÷ 0.0258 = 1.47</dd>
<dt>Answer</dt><dd><strong>X-bar limits 25.0005 to 25.0235 mm, R chart UCL 0.042 mm; Cpk about 1.47, capable though slightly off centre</strong></dd>
<dt>Limitations</dt><dd>Capability is only meaningful when the process is in statistical control; recentring to 25.000 mm would raise Cpk further.</dd>

## Key formulas for control charts

| Quantity | Formula | Symbols and units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| X-bar chart upper limit | UCL = X̿ + A₂R̄ | X̿ grand mean, R̄ average range (units of the measurement), A₂ table constant (0.577 for n = 5) |
| X-bar chart lower limit | LCL = X̿ − A₂R̄ | Same symbols as above |
| R chart upper limit | UCL = D₄R̄ | D₄ table constant (2.114 for n = 5) |
| R chart lower limit | LCL = D₃R̄ | D₃ table constant (0 for n = 5) |
| Process standard deviation | σ = R̄ ÷ d₂ | d₂ table constant (2.326 for n = 5) |
| Process capability | Cpk = min(USL − X̿, X̿ − LSL) ÷ 3σ | USL, LSL specification limits, dimensionless result |

## Rules for detecting out of control signals

Besides points outside the limits, the **Western Electric** and **Nelson rules** look for non-random patterns, for example:

1. One point beyond 3σ.
2. Two of three consecutive points beyond 2σ on the same side.
3. Four of five consecutive points beyond 1σ on the same side.
4. Eight consecutive points on one side of the centre line (a shift).
5. Six points in a row steadily increasing or decreasing (a trend), such as tool wear.

More rules catch smaller shifts sooner but also raise false alarms, so organisations choose a sensible set.

## Implementing control charts

1. Choose critical characteristics linked to customer requirements.
2. Make sure the measurement system is capable (gauge R&R), since measurement error appears as process variation.
3. Decide subgroup size and frequency to capture variation between, not within, subgroups.
4. Collect initial data, calculate trial limits, investigate and remove special causes, and recalculate.
5. Train operators to plot points and follow a **reaction plan** when a signal appears.
6. Review charts regularly and recalculate limits after deliberate process changes.

## Choosing the right chart

The choice depends on the data type and how samples are taken. If the characteristic is measured on a continuous scale, such as length, weight or temperature, and parts can be grouped into rational subgroups, use X-bar and R or X-bar and S charts. If only one measurement is available at a time, use an individuals and moving range chart. If parts are simply judged good or bad, use p or np charts, and if defects are counted on each unit, use c or u charts. When small, slow drifts matter, such as tool wear or catalyst ageing, consider EWMA or CUSUM charts.

## Control charts in modern manufacturing

The value of a chart lies in the reaction to it, so every chart needs a named owner and a clear response when a signal appears.


Today, SPC software collects data directly from gauges, CMMs and machine sensors, plots charts in real time on shop floor screens and alerts engineers automatically. Control charts are also the core tool of the **Control** phase of [Six Sigma DMAIC](/manufacturing-engineering/six-sigma-dmaic/), and they help eliminate defects, one of the [seven wastes](/manufacturing-engineering/seven-wastes-lean/) of lean manufacturing. The same ideas apply to process plants, where variables such as temperature, pressure and flow from process instruments are monitored with control charts for consistent quality.

## Control charts interview and viva questions

1. **What is the difference between common cause and special cause variation?** Common cause variation is the inherent noise of a stable process from many small random influences, while special cause variation comes from specific events such as a broken tool or a contaminated batch.
2. **Why are control limits placed at ±3 sigma?** Shewhart chose ±3 sigma as an economic balance: only about 0.27% of points fall outside by chance, yet real shifts are still detected reasonably quickly.
3. **When would you use an X-bar and S chart instead of an X-bar and R chart?** When subgroups are larger, above about 10 parts, because the standard deviation uses all the data in the subgroup better than the range.
4. **Which chart suits a count of scratches per panel of constant size?** A c chart, which plots the number of defects per unit of constant size; a u chart is used when the inspection area varies.
5. **What is tampering and why is it harmful?** Tampering is adjusting the process in response to common cause noise as if it were a special cause, and it actually increases variation.
